Video Production Hamilton

Filming A Live Site

Video Production Hamilton runs into a specific obstacle in a city that issued $2.55bn of building permits in a record year: the most useful footage is on active sites, and getting a camera onto one is a question of access, induction and timing long before it is a question of lenses.

Why the access matters

Video Production Hamilton On An Active Site

The footage everybody wants is the footage hardest to get, and the difficulty is procedural.

A finished building is a photograph. The thing that sells the next job is the work in progress: the pour, the lift, the trade doing the part nobody else can do, and the person on site explaining why it was done that way.

Getting that requires permission from whoever controls the site, an induction, the right protective equipment, and an agreement about where a crew can stand while work continues. None of it is difficult and all of it takes longer than booking a camera.

Which is why it is worth arranging once and using for a year. We spent two days on an active City of Barrie site with around forty trades and engineers working around us and came away with seven finished videos.

The constraint is not the filming. It is that a site exists in that state for a few weeks, and once it is finished the footage cannot be recovered at any price.

What to capture

Film The Stage, Not The Project

Each stage disappears, so the planning is a calendar rather than a shot list.

The stage that is about to vanish

Whatever will be covered up next week. Services before the walls close, structure before the cladding. These shots are impossible a fortnight later and they are the ones a client has never seen.

The people doing the work

Short pieces to camera from the trades and the supervisors. Unpolished and specific beats polished and general, and it is also the material that helps most with hiring.

The questions clients ask

Each answered on camera in ninety seconds, on site, with the thing in shot. The most reused material a shoot produces because it goes into email replies for years.

Stills, deliberately

A crew produces photographs almost incidentally and those run a website and a tender document for years. They belong in the deliverables rather than being discovered to be missing.

Decide those in advance. A crew improvising on a live site produces one good film, and the second piece is where the economics of an induction and a day live.

Where it earns

Tenders, Recruitment And The Pages

The commissioning reason is rarely the most valuable use.

A film made for social gets two days of attention. The same material in a tender submission, on the page a prospective client lands on, and in the reply to a request for a reference is working for years.

Recruitment is the use most firms never consider and frequently the highest return. In a city where 75.2% of businesses employ nine people or fewer, one filled vacancy can be worth more than a quarter of enquiries, and a short film of the actual work does what a job advertisement cannot.

The same footage carries paid campaigns, where real material also lasts longer. Creative fatigue in a single city arrives in weeks, and a library is what lets a campaign keep running without the audience learning to scroll past you.

And it carries the reply to an enquiry. Sending ninety seconds of the exact process somebody asked about closes more than a paragraph does, at no cost once the day is paid for.

Customers on camera

What A Video Production Hamilton Testimonial Should Actually Contain

Most are unusable because the customer was asked to be positive rather than specific.

A customer saying the service was excellent persuades nobody, because every competitor has a customer saying that. A customer describing the problem they had, what they were worried about, and what actually happened is evidence.

So the questions are about the situation rather than about you: what was the problem, what did you try first, what were you unsure about, what surprised you.

Let the awkward bit stay in. A customer who mentions that it took longer than expected and explains why is more believable than one who does not, and the explanation does the work.

Film it where the work was done. A customer standing in front of the thing is specific; the same person in a neutral room is a quote with a face attached.

Ask the week the job finishes rather than months later. The detail is still present, the goodwill is at its height, and the answer to the request is yes far more often than it will ever be again.

Before the camera arrives

An Hour Of Tidying Changes The Whole Day

The cheapest improvement available on any shoot, and it has to happen beforehand.

Clear the background of whatever should not be in it: a bin, a cable, a sign with a competitor’s name, a noticeboard three years out of date. The camera sees all of it and the eye in the room does not.

Clean what will be close to the lens. Equipment, a counter, a vehicle — anything shot in detail shows every mark, and it reads as how the business keeps things rather than as one dusty morning.

Think about what people are wearing. Branded clothing where it exists, nothing with a loud pattern, and a decision made a day earlier rather than on the spot.

None of this is production value in any expensive sense. It is an hour of preparation that is worth more than the difference between two cameras, and it is the most reliably skipped item on any shoot.

Video Production Hamilton on an active site starts with the programme rather than the brief: we ask what gets covered up next, book around that, and film everything at that stage while we are inducted and on site, because the stage will not be there on a second visit.

Can you film on an active site?

Yes, with the access arranged in advance: induction, insurance, protective equipment and a site contact who knows we are coming. None of it is difficult and all of it takes longer than booking a camera, which is why it is worth arranging once and using across a year rather than per visit.

When should we film?

Against the programme rather than the budget cycle. Film whatever is about to be covered up — services before the walls close, structure before the cladding. Those shots are impossible a fortnight later and they are the ones a prospective client has never seen.

What is the highest-return use of the footage?

Who should own the raw footage?

You should, and on construction work it matters more than usual: the project will never exist in that state again, so the rushes are genuinely irreplaceable. Settle it before the day rather than after, and make sure the stills are a named deliverable rather than a by-product.
