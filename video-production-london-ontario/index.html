Video Production London Ontario

Book The People First

Video Production London Ontario overruns for a reason that has nothing to do with cameras: the day is built around locations when it should be built around the people who have to appear in it, and people are far harder to reschedule than a room.

Observed across Ontario client work

A day has six or seven usable hours once equipment is set up and packed down. Every person who has to appear in it is a constraint on when those hours can happen, and in a working business the people worth filming are the ones with the least slack.

Built around locations, a schedule looks efficient and then collapses when the one person who can explain the work is on site until four. Built around people, the locations arrange themselves around the gaps.

The practical rule is unglamorous: confirm the humans before anything else, in writing, with a time each. Rooms do not cancel and machines do not have dentist appointments.

That single reordering is the difference between a day that produces what the brief described and one that produces a good film and an apologetic email about what did not get shot.

Getting usable answers

What A Video Production London Ontario Interview Should Ask

Most unusable footage is a good speaker answering a badly framed question.

A question that can be answered yes or no will be. A question beginning “tell me about the time” produces a story, and stories are the only part of an interview anybody watches.

Ask for the answer to include the question, so each reply stands alone in the edit. Without that, every clip depends on hearing an interviewer who will not be in the film.

Send the questions in advance and then ask them differently on the day. The preparation produces thought; the variation prevents recitation.

And ask the awkward one. What goes wrong, what people get surprised by, what you wish customers knew — those answers are specific, and specificity is the entire difference between a film that persuades and one that decorates.

Allow for the first ten minutes being unusable. Almost nobody is comfortable immediately, and a schedule that assumes the first answer will be the good one produces an interview where everybody is tense and nothing is said well.

Keeping it current

A Price On Screen Dates The Whole Film

Worth deciding deliberately, because it determines how long the asset lasts.

Anything that will change within two years — a price, a staff count, an award, a piece of equipment, a telephone number — shortens the life of the film it appears in.

Sometimes that is the correct trade. A campaign film for one season can be as specific as it likes, because it will be retired anyway.

For anything meant to sit on a page for years, keep the perishable detail out of the picture and put it in the text beside the film, where it can be edited in seconds.

Where a detail must appear, put it on a title card rather than in speech. A card can be replaced in an afternoon; a spoken sentence means a reshoot.

Where it is hosted

Putting The File On Your Own Site Is The Wrong Default

A decision with consequences for speed, for measurement and for who holds the asset.

A video file loaded directly by a page is large, slow on a phone, and will not adapt to a weak connection. A hosted player handles all three and costs nothing at the volumes most businesses have.

What a hosted player costs instead is control over what surrounds it. The free tiers of some platforms show other people’s material afterwards, which is an odd thing to buy with a page you paid to build.

So the practical answer is usually a hosted player with the recommendations switched off, embedded on your own page, with the original file kept by you.

That last part matters most. The platform is a convenience; the file is the asset, and an account closure or a policy change should not be able to take it.

Ask for the finished pieces in a high-quality master as well as the web versions. A compressed copy pulled back down from a platform has already lost quality, and re-uploading it elsewhere loses more, which is how a film looks worse every year it is moved.

What to watch afterwards

How Far People Got Is The Only Useful Number

View counts say almost nothing. The drop-off curve says where the film is wrong.

Every player reports what share of viewers were still there at each second. Read it once and the film’s faults are visible: a cliff at four seconds means the opening fails, a steady decline means it is too long, a flat line means it is working.

That curve is also the brief for the next one. Whatever caused the cliff is a decision somebody made, and the same decision will be made again unless the curve is looked at.

Compare across films rather than against a benchmark. Industry averages are gathered from material nothing like yours, and your own second film against your first is the comparison with any meaning.

Five minutes per film, once, a fortnight after it goes up. It is the only measurement in this subject that reliably changes what gets made next.

Write the conclusion down in a line beside the film’s name. A year of those lines is a document nobody else in the business has, and it is what stops the same expensive opening being commissioned a fourth time.

How we schedule a video production london ontario day

We book a video production london ontario day around the people rather than the locations: every person who has to appear is confirmed in writing with a time, and the rooms are arranged around those slots. It is the least interesting part of the planning and it is the part that decides whether the day delivers what the brief described.

We are in Minesing, a couple of hours away, so travel appears as its own visible line rather than folded into a fee. Where a project needs a crew on call weekly, a closer supplier is the better buy and we will say so.

Why do shooting days overrun?

Almost always scheduling rather than filming. A day has six or seven usable hours and every person who has to appear is a constraint on when those hours can happen. Built around locations a schedule looks efficient and then collapses; built around people, the rooms arrange themselves around the gaps.

How many pieces should one day produce?

Far more than most people ask for. Once a crew is set up another piece costs minutes, and the expensive part was getting everybody in one place. A planned day yields a main film, several short answers, a piece from each person present, cutaways and stills. An unplanned day yields one film.

What is the most valuable use of the footage?

Usually not the one it was commissioned for. The page a prospect lands on, the reply to a specific enquiry, and recruitment all outlive a social post by years. One filled vacancy is frequently worth more than a quarter of enquiries, and almost nobody films for it.

Do we need a crew for everything?

No, and a supplier who says otherwise is protecting a day rate. A phone with a clip microphone handles updates, replies and anything unexpected. A crew is for lighting, clean audio and the deliberate piece at the top of a page.
