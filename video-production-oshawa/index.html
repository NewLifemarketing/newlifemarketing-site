Video Production Oshawa

Watched With The Sound Off

Video Production Oshawa is almost always briefed as though somebody will watch it properly, and in a city where a large share of the audience is on a train with one hand on a phone, the realistic viewing condition is silent, vertical and about two seconds long before a thumb moves.

A film made for a meeting room and posted to a phone loses the opening, the music and most of the narration. What survives is whatever is visible in the first frame and whatever is legible as text.

That is not an argument for worse films. It is an argument for deciding the viewing condition before filming rather than discovering it afterwards, because the same material shot with that in mind costs exactly the same.

The decisions are small and they are all taken before the camera arrives: frame it vertically or square, put the point in the first two seconds, and treat captions as a deliverable rather than an afterthought.

Taken afterwards, each of those means re-editing the same footage a second time, which is the commonest avoidable cost in a video project that nobody budgets for.

Sound

What Video Production Oshawa Should Get Right Before The Picture

Viewers forgive a soft image and leave within seconds of unclear speech.

A phone recording with a clip microphone on the speaker beats a well-shot interview recorded on the camera from three metres away, every time, and the difference costs very little.

The common faults are predictable: a room with hard surfaces, an extractor fan nobody noticed, traffic through an open window, a speaker who turns away mid-sentence.

Listen on headphones for thirty seconds before moving on. On a shoot it is the single check most worth making, because audio cannot be repaired afterwards in the way an image can be graded.

Where a location is genuinely noisy, record the words somewhere quiet and use the noisy footage as picture. That is a planning decision and it rescues material that would otherwise be unusable.

Record a minute of the room with nobody speaking as well. It sounds like nothing and it is what lets an editor smooth the joins between takes, and a day that skipped it produces an edit that audibly jumps every few seconds.

What gets shown first

The Still Frame Is Doing Half The Work

Before anybody presses play, they see one picture, and it is usually chosen at random.

Most platforms will take a frame from the opening of the film unless told otherwise, which means the image representing a two-minute piece is frequently somebody blinking.

Choose it deliberately, or shoot one. A clear image of the thing, a face looking at the camera, or a result worth seeing will be clicked considerably more than an arbitrary frame.

Text on it helps where the platform allows a custom image. Three or four words naming what the film answers is the difference between a view and a scroll.

It is five minutes of work per film and it is skipped on almost every project, because it happens after everybody considers the job finished.

Ask for it as a named deliverable in the quote, alongside the film itself. Anything not written into the list arrives only if somebody remembers, and by the time the film is being uploaded the crew has moved on to the next job.

Shapes and sizes

Agree The Formats Before The Edit Starts

The same film in the wrong shape is cropped by a platform into something nobody composed.

A wide frame placed into a vertical feed loses the sides, and whatever was happening there — frequently the subject — goes with it. A vertical film on a page sits as a narrow strip with black on both sides.

Which means the shapes have to be decided before filming, not after, because a shot composed for one cannot always be recovered for the other.

The practical approach is to frame with room at the edges, knowing a vertical crop is coming, and to list the required shapes in the brief alongside the lengths.

Ask for the project files or at least each cut in every shape you will use. Returning to an editor six months later for a differently shaped version of the same film is a paid job that should have been free.

What it should ask for

Every Film Needs One Ending

A piece that finishes on a logo has wasted the attention it earned.

Somebody who watched to the end is the most interested person the film will reach. What they are asked to do next should be specific, spoken as well as written, and the same every time.

One action rather than three. A closing frame offering a website, a telephone number, an email address and two social accounts is a closing frame that produces nothing.

Make it reachable where the film actually sits. A spoken address is useless on a platform where nothing is clickable, and a clickable link is wasted if nobody says what is on the other side of it.

And keep it short. Four seconds is plenty; anything longer is the part people leave during, which teaches the platform the film was abandoned.

Use the same ending across every film you make. Repetition is doing useful work here: somebody who has seen three of your pieces should already know what the last four seconds will say, and familiarity is most of what makes a small business feel established.
