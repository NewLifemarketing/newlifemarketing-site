What to do, in order

Ranked by return against effort. The first two are an afternoon. The third is the quarter.

  1. Add an FAQ to all six service pages

    Not one page on the site has one. It is the easiest slot you own: a question is how people type, so the phrase and a natural sentence are the same thing. Take the questions straight from the People Also Ask box.

    An afternoon

  2. Fix the H1 on the home page and /garages/

    Your home page uses the big display line as its H1 and /garages/ has no H1 at all. The small line above the headline should be the H1 and should carry the phrase exactly. It is a one-line edit each.

    An hour

  3. Build the eight missing area pages

    You serve nine towns and have a page for one. An area page is not your service page with the town swapped in — it needs what is actually different about working there, or it reads as the template it is.

    A month

  4. Then the grid, highest-volume cells first

    Fifty cells is not fifty months. Research which combinations people actually search — some towns want renovations and nothing else — and build only those. Half the grid is usually all the grid worth having.

    A quarter

Two things no checker can score

Filling all eleven slots makes a page correctly built. It does not make it rank. These two decide that, and both need a person to look.

Does the format match?

Search your keyword and look at what Google returns. If the first ten results are guides and yours is a service page, the page has lost before it was written — however well built it is.

What does it say that the others don't?

Permit processes and span tables are already on nine competitors' sites. A real job, in a named town, with what it cost and what went wrong, is the one thing nobody can copy from you.

This is built for trades businesses

Builders, contractors, electricians, plumbers, roofers, landscapers, HVAC, excavation — anyone who does a job in named towns. The grid is services multiplied by towns, so it only describes a business that works that way. If you run a shop or sell online, it does not, and we would rather say so than hand you a score that means nothing.

Questions about the audit

Is it really free, and what do you do with my details?

Free. We ask for your name, company, email and phone because the report is worth following up on and because we would rather know who we are auditing than hand a crawler to anybody who finds the page. You get the whole report on screen either way, and a copy by email. No list, no drip sequence.

How does it know which keyword each page is targeting?

It doesn't — it infers one from the page's own slug, title tag and first heading. That is stated on every page in the report. If the inferred keyword is wrong, the slots underneath it are being measured against the wrong phrase, which is worth knowing in itself.

What counts as a service area page?

A page about one place you work — /service-areas/orillia/ or /home-builder-orillia/. A service × area page is one service in one place, which is the combination with actual buying intent behind it and the one most sites never build.

Why fifty-four pages? That sounds like a lot.

Because six services multiplied by nine towns is fifty-four. It is the ceiling, not a target. Most of those combinations have little or no search behind them, and the right number to build is usually between a third and a half — which is what research decides, not arithmetic.

Will you crawl my whole site?

Up to 20 pages on the free crawl, following your sitemap where you have one — services and service areas first, so the budget goes on the pages that answer the question. If your site is bigger than that the report says so and tells you how many it read. We score the HTML and keep none of it afterwards.

Does a good score mean I will rank?

No, and any tool that tells you otherwise is selling something. A high score means the page is correctly built. Whether it ranks depends on whether it answers the search better than the ten pages already there, and no checker can measure that.
