Software Developer Cambridge

The Gap Between Two Systems

Most of what a software developer cambridge is asked for in a manufacturing town is not an application at all: it is the connective work between a system that runs the floor and a system that runs the office, which have never spoken to each other and were never meant to.

What actually gets asked for

Software Developer Cambridge: Integration Before Application

The brief says "we need something built". The requirement underneath is usually narrower and cheaper.

Toyota has manufactured here since 1988 and a dense supplier base grew around it. The software estate in a business like that is layered by decade: something on the floor, something in accounts, a quoting spreadsheet somebody built, and a scheduling system bought to fix the spreadsheet.

None of them were designed to talk to each other, so a person does it. Somebody retypes a job number, somebody exports a report and pastes it into another report, somebody keeps a parallel list because the official one is never current.

That person is the requirement. A software developer cambridge brought in to write an application frequently discovers that what is needed is an integration measured in weeks, and that the application was a way of describing the pain rather than a specification of the cure.

Finding the real requirement

Follow The Spreadsheet

Every manual workaround in a business is a map of where the systems do not meet, and it is free to read.

Ask what spreadsheets exist outside the official systems and who maintains them. The answer is a list of places where software failed to cover something and a human filled the gap, and it is far more reliable than any workshop about future state.

Each one carries three useful facts: what data is being moved, where it comes from, and how often. That is most of an integration specification, written by the person who needs it, before anybody has been paid.

It also ranks the work honestly. A spreadsheet somebody updates daily is worth more to fix than one updated quarterly, regardless of which sounds more strategic in a meeting.

The one caution: some workarounds exist because the process is wrong rather than because the software is. Automating those makes a bad process faster, which is worse. Ask why the step exists before asking how to remove it.

What is worth building

Three Things That Reliably Pay For Themselves

In a supplier business these come up repeatedly and are small enough to quote honestly.

The quote that writes itself

Pricing logic that lives in somebody’s head or in a protected spreadsheet, turned into something anybody in the office can run. It shortens the response time to an enquiry, which in a supplier market is frequently the whole competitive difference.

The job status somebody can see

A customer asking where their order is costs a phone call, an interruption on the floor and a call back. A page they can check themselves removes all three and is a small piece of work.

The report nobody assembles by hand

Whatever somebody compiles monthly from three sources. Automating it saves the day and, more usefully, makes the number current rather than three weeks old when decisions are made from it.

How to pay for it

Pay A Software Developer Cambridge In Phases That Each Leave Something Behind

The rule is that stopping after any phase leaves you with something usable.

A build paid in three instalments against internal milestones leaves you with nothing if it stops at two. A build paid in three phases, each ending in something that runs, leaves you with two thirds of a working tool.

That shape is almost always possible and it is almost never proposed, because it is more work to plan and it gives the client a decision point the supplier would rather not create.

It also changes the conversation after phase one. You now have evidence about how this supplier estimates, how they communicate, and whether the thing they built matches what you described — none of which a reference call can tell you.

Where a supplier insists the work cannot be broken up, ask why. Sometimes the answer is genuine and technical. More often it is that the first phase alone would not be worth what they need to charge, which is worth knowing before signing rather than after.

Before automating anything

Count The Exceptions First

A process with too many exceptions is a process that resists automation at any price.

Take the last fifty jobs and count how many went through the standard path without somebody intervening. If it is forty-five, the process is stable and worth encoding. If it is twenty, what you have is not a process, it is a habit with a lot of judgement attached.

Automating the second kind produces software that handles the easy half and is worked around for the rest, which is more expensive than the manual version because now there are two systems.

The useful response is to reduce the exceptions before writing anything. Most turn out to be three or four recurring situations that could each become a rule, and agreeing those rules is a management decision rather than a technical one.

Done in that order, the build gets smaller and the thing it produces gets used. Done in the other order, the exceptions arrive as change requests at the worst possible point.

Our scope

Where We Fit, And Where A Software Developer Cambridge Does Not

Worth establishing in the first ten minutes rather than the second meeting.

Our work is the connective layer: an enquiry reaching a record, systems agreeing with each other, and a report that pulls from them rather than from a spreadsheet somebody maintains by hand. We buy the advertising those enquiries come from too, over $70,000 a month of it for Ontario businesses, run by the owner personally.

The jobs a software developer cambridge should get instead of us

Anything with its own users outside your company, anything that will need development every week for a year, and anything being built to sell belongs with a software developer cambridge on a continuing arrangement. We work in bounded pieces with an end date, which suits a different kind of problem.

We do not do bookkeeping, payroll or inventory systems, and we would not replace a working industry package. We are based in Minesing; the work itself is done remotely.

How do we know whether we need an integration or an application?

List the spreadsheets that exist outside your official systems and who updates them. If the pain is that data has to be moved between two things that already work, it is an integration and it is measured in weeks. If the pain is that something nobody has does not exist at all, it is an application and it is measured in quarters.

Our machine software is old. Can it be connected to anything?

Often, and the deciding factor is whether the vendor exposes an interface and whether your licence includes it. That is a question to ask the vendor before anybody quotes the work, because the answer can end the project. Where there is no interface, there is frequently a database or an export that can be read instead, which is less elegant and works.

Should we hire somebody instead?

If software becomes central to how the business runs, eventually yes. For a bounded piece of connective work with an end date, an employed developer is an expensive way to buy six weeks of effort and leaves you supporting a system with one person who understands it. Documentation and repository access matter either way.

Who owns the code?

Whatever the contract says, so read it before signing. You want the source in a repository you control, documentation good enough for another developer to continue, and any hosting in your own name. A supplier resisting any of the three is describing their retention strategy rather than a security policy.

Next step

Which Spreadsheet Hurts Most?

Name it and say who updates it. That is usually enough for us to tell you within an hour whether this is weeks of work or quarters.
