Bracebridge Web Design

Written For Somebody Absent

Bracebridge Web Design is bought for a town of 17,305 and read by a great many people who are nowhere near it — property owners in another city, visitors planning a trip, and buyers who will decide entirely from the page because they cannot drop in to look.

A resident can drive past, ask a neighbour and form an impression before ever opening a website. An absent property owner and a visitor planning a trip cannot do any of those things, and the site is the whole of what they have.

That raises the stakes on the unglamorous pages. Who you are, where you are, how far you travel, what things cost roughly, and what happens if something is found while nobody is there.

It also means the site has to be genuinely current. A copyright year from three years ago or photographs of a van you no longer own reads, to somebody who cannot check, as a business that may not exist.

None of that is a design problem, which is why a rebuild judged on appearance frequently changes nothing at all.

The useful audit is correspondingly dull. Open your own site as a stranger would — on a phone, from somewhere else, with the address typed in rather than reached from a bookmark — and write down every question you cannot answer from it in two minutes. That list is the brief, and it is almost always shorter and cheaper to fix than a redesign.

What it has to answer

Four Questions From A Reader Two Hours Away

All four are cheap to answer and most small-town sites answer none of them.

How far do you come?

Named places and a drive time, with what changes beyond them. It is the first question an absent reader has and the last thing most sites say.

Will you work unsupervised?

Access, keys, how you report back, what you do if something is wrong. For a property owner elsewhere this is the whole decision.

What does it cost, roughly?

A range with the reason it varies. Somebody who cannot ask a neighbour has no other way to calibrate, and its absence reads as something being hidden.

Are you still trading?

Recent dated work, a current year, photographs that match the business now. Absent readers are unusually alert to signs a site has been left alone.

Four paragraphs, an afternoon of writing, and they do more for enquiries than a redesign would. They are also the paragraphs most likely to be forwarded to a neighbour, which in a market like this one is how a recommendation actually travels.

What goes on the pages

Photographs Of The Actual Work

The single biggest difference between a site that persuades and one that decorates.

Stock photography is recognisable and it reads as a business with nothing of its own to show. A reader two hours away is trying to establish whether you are real, and a picture that could belong to any company in the country answers that question the wrong way.

What works is ordinary: the finished job, the van, the person who will turn up, the workshop. Taken on a phone in reasonable light, these outperform anything bought, because they are evidently yours.

Caption them with where and what. “Kitchen, two days, Bracebridge” does more than any adjective, and it also puts real words on a page that would otherwise be pictures only.

Collect them as work happens rather than arranging a session. A standing habit of two photographs per job produces a library within a season, and the marginal cost of it is nothing.

Proof

Where Bracebridge Web Design Should Put The Reviews

Not on a page called testimonials, which is the page nobody visits.

Proof works where the doubt occurs. A review about punctuality belongs beside the paragraph about scheduling; a review about the finish belongs under the photographs of finished work.

A dedicated testimonials page gathers all of it in the one place a sceptical reader has already decided to skip, because a page of selected praise is understood to be selected.

Use the words people actually wrote, including the slightly awkward ones. Polished quotes read as written by the company, and a reader who suspects that discounts all of them at once.

Name and town, with permission. An unattributed quote is worth close to nothing, and a reader from the same area recognising a place name is worth more than a page of five-star graphics.

Getting them is a habit rather than a campaign. Ask at the moment a job finishes well, by message, with a direct link, and the rate of response is several times what a later request achieves. Asked a fortnight afterwards the same customer is still pleased and no longer motivated.

Email

An Address At Your Own Domain

A small detail with two separate consequences, and the second one is expensive.

An enquiry from a business using a free webmail address is read differently from one at its own domain. That is unfair and it is also true, and it costs nothing to fix.

The second consequence matters more. Mail sent from a domain that has not been configured properly gets filtered, and the sender never finds out: the reply to an enquiry simply never arrives and the customer concludes you did not bother.

Three settings on the domain handle it. They are technical, they are quick, and they are routinely left undone because nothing visibly breaks when they are missing.

Ask whoever manages the site to confirm all three are in place and to send a test to an address outside the business. A quarter of the small businesses that check discover something was wrong.

What matters most on a site here?

Answering what somebody who cannot visit needs to know: how far you come, whether you will work unsupervised, what it costs roughly, and whether you are still trading. A resident can drive past and ask a neighbour; an absent property owner has only the page.

How fast does it need to be?

Fast on a phone with a weak signal, which is the realistic worst case here and the condition almost nobody tests in. Every large image, font and tracking script is a delay paid for by somebody standing outside with one bar, and it is invisible on the machine the site was designed on.

Why do quotes vary so much?

Because they describe different projects in the same words. How much is designed from scratch, who writes the content, what the site connects to and what happens after launch move the number. Page count moves it least and is usually what gets compared.

What if our web designer disappears?

Arrange for it before the build. Hosting in your company’s name with your own payment method, the domain registered to you, administrative access you hold, and the source or platform account in your control. All four cost nothing at the start and are awkward afterwards.
