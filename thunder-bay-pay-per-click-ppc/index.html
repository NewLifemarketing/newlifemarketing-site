Thunder Bay Pay Per Click PPC

One City, Two Centres

A thunder bay pay per click ppc account has a geography problem that no other city in this grid shares: Fort William and Port Arthur became one city in 1970 and still function as two centres, so a radius drawn from either one quietly excludes a large part of the other.

The geography

Thunder Bay Pay Per Click PPC: A Radius Drawn On The Wrong Shape

Two downtowns, one municipal boundary, and a default setting that assumes a single centre.

Most campaign geography is configured as a circle around a point, which works in a city with one centre and fails in one with two. Drawn tightly around either, the circle excludes a meaningful share of the city’s own population.

Drawn loosely enough to cover both, it also reaches a great deal of land with nobody on it, which is cheaper than it would be elsewhere but not free, and it blurs the reporting so that nobody can tell which half of the city is producing anything.

The better configuration is not a radius at all. It is the city named explicitly, with the reporting split so you can see what each centre produced, and travel costs reflected in what you are willing to pay for an enquiry from each.

That takes an afternoon to set up and it is the single highest-return change available in most accounts here.

The reporting half of that change is the part worth insisting on, because it is what makes every later decision possible. Without a split, a campaign that performs well in one half of the city and badly in the other reports as mediocre overall, and the usual response is to adjust something that was never the problem. With a split, the same data says plainly which half to put more behind and which to leave alone, and that single distinction is frequently worth more than any change to the creative or the bidding.

It also tells you something about the business rather than the account. Most firms here have an intuition about which side of the city their customers come from, and the intuition is frequently a few years out of date. The report settles it with evidence, costs nothing beyond the configuration, and the answer tends to change what gets built next rather than only what gets bid.

What is easy here

What A Thunder Bay Pay Per Click PPC Account Does Not Have To Worry About

The isolation that makes other things hard makes one thing simple.

No neighbouring market to leak into

With the nearest larger city 700 km away, a generous boundary reaches highway and bush rather than a costlier adjacent market with its own suppliers. The expensive mistake available in southern Ontario is not available here.

A calculable audience

A defined population with no commuter overlap means the cost of reaching a meaningful share of it more than once is a number rather than a guess.

Little competitive bidding

The local auction is thin, so click prices are set by relevance rather than by somebody else’s budget. What you pay is mostly a function of how good your page and your account are.

The trade-off is saturation. An audience you can cover completely you can exhaust completely, and cost per result climbing after three weeks with nothing changed in the account is the audience having seen it rather than the campaign failing.

Who counts as local

What A Thunder Bay Pay Per Click PPC Account Means By "Here"

One setting, two options, and the default is the wrong one for most businesses.

A location target can mean people who are in the area, or people who are in it or have shown interest in it. The second is the default, and it includes somebody three provinces away reading about the place.

For a business selling to residents that is pure waste, invisible in the headline figures and perfectly visible in the location report once anybody opens it.

For a business selling to visitors it is exactly right, because the people planning a trip are the customers and they are not here yet.

So it is a commercial decision rather than a technical one, and it should be made deliberately on the first day. Most accounts have made it by accident.

Showing it again

The People Who Nearly Bought Are The Cheapest Audience

And the one most likely to be shown the same advertisement forty times.

Somebody who visited a page and left is cheap to reach again and far likelier to act than a stranger. For anything with a considered purchase, this is usually the best-performing spend in the account.

What ruins it is frequency. The same advertisement shown to the same small audience day after day stops working and starts irritating, and in a small market the audience is small enough for that to happen within a fortnight.

So cap how often somebody sees it, and set an end: thirty days after the visit is plenty for most purchases, and anything beyond ninety is following people who have decided.

Exclude the ones who already bought. Advertising to existing customers for a purchase they have made is the most visible way to look like nobody is watching the account.

The exclusion needs a list of who bought, which means the purchase has to be recorded somewhere the advertising can read. For most small businesses that is a single page shown after a booking or a form, and it takes ten minutes to set up on the day the campaign starts and is effectively impossible to backfill afterwards.

Where the sale happens by telephone and nothing online marks it, the honest answer is a shorter window rather than a clever one. Thirty days following somebody who has already decided wastes a little; ninety days wastes a great deal and irritates a customer you now have to keep.

The other half of this is what the repeat advertisement says. Showing the same message again to somebody who already read it and did not act is asking the same question twice and expecting a different answer. A second message that addresses the likeliest reason they hesitated — price, timing, whether you cover their area — is a different proposition and performs like one.

That requires knowing why people hesitate, which is not in any report. It is in what the person answering the telephone hears several times a week, and writing those three objections down is an afternoon that improves every advertisement in the account.

A last point about the audience itself, because it is routinely misunderstood. The list of people who visited is not a mailing list and it cannot be exported, read or contacted directly; it is a bucket the platform uses to decide who to show something to. That matters for two reasons: nothing about it is portable to another supplier, and nothing in it identifies anybody to you.

The practical consequence is that the list is slow to rebuild and easy to lose. It accumulates over months, it lives inside whichever account holds it, and starting again elsewhere means starting that accumulation from zero while still paying for the clicks that fill it.

Which is another reason the account should sit under the business rather than a supplier, and another reason to set the tracking up properly in the first week rather than the first quarter. Both are administrative decisions with no cost attached at the start and a real one later.

There is also a minimum size below which none of this is available at all. The platform will not show advertisements to a group smaller than a threshold, for reasons of privacy, so a business with very few visitors a month simply cannot use the feature until the traffic is there. That is worth knowing before anybody builds a campaign around it.

What else drifts

What A Thunder Bay Pay Per Click PPC Account Must Constrain

Campaign types that choose their own geography interact badly with a city shaped like this one.

Left to select their own placements and locations inside whatever boundary they are given, automated campaign types will find the cheapest impressions available. In a city with two centres and a great deal of empty land around it, cheap impressions are abundant and nearly worthless.

Used with a boundary that is enforced rather than suggested, clean conversion data and a maintained exclusion list, they work well. Used as a substitute for those three they produce a confident report describing activity nobody chose.

The question to put to any supplier is therefore not whether they use them, but what constrains them: which conversions are being optimised toward, what geography is enforced, what is excluded, and how often any of it is reviewed.

The habit that catches the drift is simple: read the search terms report and the geographic report side by side each month and ask of every line whether you would have chosen to pay for it. Anything failing that test becomes a negative keyword or an excluded location that week, and the account gets tighter every month instead of looser.

It is worth saying what this is not. None of it is an argument against automation, which genuinely outperforms what manual bidding achieved in almost every account we have moved across. It is an argument for giving it the three things it needs to work: a target worth optimising toward, a boundary it is not allowed to leave, and a list of what it may never buy. Supplied with those, it is the best operator most small accounts have ever had. Supplied with none of them, it is an expensive and very confident guess.

Before spending anything

Fix The Free Things First

Two of them usually move more than a month of advertising.

The Business Profile decides a large share of local results and is almost always wrong somewhere: categories chosen once, a service area that does not match where you go, lapsed hours, photographs from a previous decade.

The landing page is the second. Advertising magnifies whatever the page already does, so paid traffic over a page that loads slowly or buries the number is paying to lose people faster. Test it on a phone away from the office and time how long before anything is readable.

Both are an afternoon’s work and both are finished before the first click is bought. An account launched over a broken page produces a cost per enquiry that looks like a market problem and is not.

And in a city with two centres, check the address and hours are consistent everywhere they appear. A contradiction between the profile and the site is expensive where the map produces most of the phone calls.

The third free thing is knowing what a customer is worth, without which no cost per enquiry can be judged and the budget conversation has no arithmetic in it at all. Those three take an afternoon between them and they decide what every later dollar is worth; an account launched before they are settled is not really an experiment, because nothing it reports can be interpreted.

How should the targeting be set up?

Not as a radius. Fort William and Port Arthur became one city in 1970 and still function as two centres, so a circle drawn from either excludes a meaningful share of the other. Name the city explicitly, split the reporting so you can see what each half produced, and reflect travel in what you will pay per enquiry from each.

Is there a risk of wasting budget on neighbouring markets?

Far less than in southern Ontario. With the nearest larger city 700 km away, a generous boundary reaches highway and bush rather than a costlier adjacent market with its own suppliers. The real risk here is saturation rather than leakage.

Because the audience has seen it. A market you can cover completely you can exhaust completely, and cost per result climbing with nothing changed in the account is saturation rather than failure. Bursts with a reason behind them and new material planned in advance work better than a permanent drip.
