What to film

Video Production Markham That Does The Demonstration

A brand film is watched for nine seconds. A demonstration is watched to the end by the only people who matter.

A technical buyer wants to see the thing working. Not a montage of it working — the actual sequence, including the part where somebody types something, waits, and the result appears.

That film is unglamorous to commission and it is the one that gets forwarded internally, which is where most business-to-business decisions actually happen. The person you met is rarely the person who signs.

It also answers the question a brand film cannot: is this real. In a market where everybody’s website claims the same capability, three minutes of the thing operating is the cheapest proof available.

And it shortens the sales cycle in a specific way: the first meeting stops being an explanation and starts being a conversation about whether it fits, which is a different and much better meeting.

Reviewing the edit

Two Rounds, One Voice, Written Down

More projects go over on this stage than on filming, and it is entirely preventable.

Agree the number of revision rounds before work starts, and agree that feedback arrives in one document from one person. Three people sending contradictory notes is the normal case and it is where a fixed price stops being fixed.

Give feedback against timecodes rather than impressions. “At 0:42, the figure is wrong” is actionable; “it feels slow” produces a guess and another round.

Review the structure before anything is polished. Asking for a different order after the colour and sound are finished means paying for that work twice.

And see a rough version early. A first cut that looks unfinished is doing its job — it is cheap to change at that point and expensive to change later.

A set rather than a one-off

Making Video Production Markham Pieces Look Like One Set

Consistency across a series is worth more than quality in any one of them.

The same opening device, the same typeface, the same ending, the same framing for anybody speaking. None of it is expensive and together it is what makes six separate pieces read as one company.

Decide it on the first film and write it down as a page of notes, because the second film is frequently made by somebody else a year later with no reference to the first.

It also compounds. A viewer who has seen two of your pieces recognises the third in a second and a half, which is attention you did not have to earn again.

The common failure is a set made in three bursts over four years, each in whatever style was current, with nothing in common. That is six one-off films rather than a library.

Keep the notes with the brand files rather than in an email thread. The person who needs them is a supplier you have not hired yet, and the handover that matters is the one nobody is planning for.

Where else it goes

Film In A Proposal Does Work No Page Can

The under-used placement, and the one with the highest value per view.

A proposal or a tender is read by people who never met you, after the meeting, and frequently decides it. A short film embedded in or linked from that document puts a person and a process in front of them at exactly that moment.

It does not need to be bespoke. A ninety-second piece explaining how the work actually runs, reused across every proposal, is one of the highest-return things a day of filming produces.

A second one answering the objection you always get is worth the same again. Both are made from material a general shoot already captured if somebody thought to ask for them.

The view count will be tiny and irrelevant. Forty views from people deciding on a contract is worth more than forty thousand from a feed, and no standard report presents it that way.

Between the days

A Screen Recording Covers More Than People Expect

For a technical business the cheapest useful video needs no crew at all.

A screen recording with clean audio answers a specific question better than a produced film does, and it can be made the same afternoon somebody asks. For anything demonstrated on a screen, that is most of the value available.

What it does not do is light a room, record an interview, or produce the deliberate piece at the top of a page. Those need a crew, planning and a day.

So the honest division is this: the crew captures what needs equipment and people on camera, and somebody internal captures what needs only a microphone and a screen. Two things make the second half work: a decent microphone, and a habit of recording the answer the first time somebody asks rather than the fifth.

A supplier who insists everything must be produced is protecting a day rate. One who tells you which half you should do yourselves is being honest about where the value is.

One habit makes the self-recorded half worth far more than it usually is. Keep the recordings somewhere findable and named for the question they answer, rather than scattered through an inbox. Within a year that folder becomes the single most useful sales asset most technical businesses own, and it costs nothing beyond the discipline of saving things properly the first time.

Straight answers

Video Production Markham FAQ

Straight answers before you ever book a call.

What should we film first?

The demonstration, at real speed, with the waiting left in. A technical buyer wants to see the thing working rather than a montage of it working, and cutting out every pause is what makes a demonstration look like an advertisement. It is also the piece that gets forwarded internally, which is where the decision is actually made.

How many pieces should one day produce?

Far more than most people ask for. Once a crew is set up another piece costs minutes; setting up is what costs. A planned day yields the demonstration, several short answers to questions you are asked weekly, pieces to camera from the team, cutaways and stills. An unplanned day yields one film.

Can we just record it ourselves?

For anything that happens on a screen, largely yes, and you should. A screen recording with clean audio answers a specific question better than a produced film and can be made the same afternoon. A crew is for lighting a room, interviews and the deliberate piece at the top of a page.

Who should own the raw footage?

You should, and settle it before the day along with consent from everyone who appears. The rushes are the asset: holding them means a different editor can produce something new in three years, including a cut nobody planned for when the day was booked.
