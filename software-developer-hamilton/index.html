Software Developer Hamilton

Remove One Spreadsheet

Hiring a software developer hamilton goes wrong in a predictable way in a city of small firms: the project that gets specified is a product, and the project that would have paid for itself is the removal of one spreadsheet that three people retype every week.

A firm of three does not have a software problem in the sense the word usually implies. It has four spreadsheets, a shared inbox, a filing convention somebody invented in 2019, and one person who knows how it all fits together.

The expensive mistake is treating that as a brief for a system. Replacing all of it produces a long project, a large invoice and a tool nobody uses, because the thing being replaced worked and was understood.

The useful project is narrower: find the one step that is retyped, the one piece of information that exists in three places, the one hour a week somebody spends reconciling. Remove that, and nothing else.

It pays for itself quickly, it is finishable, and it leaves the rest of the business intact. A second small project can follow; a failed large one usually ends the subject for years.

Before writing anything

Four Questions That Decide Whether To Build At All

The best answer a developer can give is frequently that you should not.

Does something already do this?

Most small-firm problems have an off-the-shelf answer costing a fraction of a build. Paying for a bespoke version of something you could subscribe to is the commonest waste in this market.

What does the manual version cost?

Hours a week times a wage is the budget. If nobody has worked it out, the project has no size and will be priced on impressions instead.

Who maintains it afterwards?

Custom software is a commitment, not a purchase. In a firm of four, the honest question is who applies updates when the person who commissioned it has left.

What happens if it stops?

If the answer is the business stops, the thing needs redundancy, documentation and an exit. If the answer is somebody retypes it for a week, the project can be much simpler and cheaper.

A supplier who works through those four with you before quoting is worth more than one who agrees enthusiastically, even when the conclusion is that there is no project.

What to build

Small, Finishable, And Yours

Three properties that separate a project that helps from one that becomes a liability.

Small means it can be described in a sentence and delivered inside a quarter. A project that cannot be described in a sentence has not been understood yet, and building it will be how the understanding gets paid for.

Finishable means there is a point at which it is done and nothing further is owed. Open-ended development in a firm of four is a subscription to a developer rather than an asset, and it is the arrangement most likely to end badly.

Yours means the code, the hosting and the accounts are in your company’s name, with documentation a different developer could pick up. In a market of small suppliers the realistic risk is not a bad build; it is a good build nobody else can touch.

Agree all three before any code exists. All three are free to specify and expensive to retrofit.

The integration question

Where A Software Developer Hamilton Adds The Most Value

The common small-firm win is not new software. It is making two existing systems talk.

The quote arrives in one system, the job lives in another, the invoice is raised in a third, and somebody retypes the same five fields three times. Nothing is broken and everything takes longer than it should.

Joining those is usually days rather than months, costs far less than any of the three systems it connects, and removes the step most likely to introduce an error. It is also unglamorous, which is why it rarely gets proposed.

The same applies to the website. A form that emails somebody is trivial; a form that creates a record, notifies the right person and shows up in the pipeline is an integration, and it is where small-project estimates most often break.

Ask for integrations to be priced separately. It is the line that moves a quote by a factor of three and the one most likely to be assumed rather than specified.

There is a practical reason it gets assumed. Joining two systems is only predictable once somebody has looked at what each of them will actually allow, and that look takes a few hours nobody wants to charge for before a project exists. Paying for those hours as a small separate piece of work is the cheapest insurance available on a fixed-price build, because it converts the single largest unknown into a number.

Who you are hiring

One Person Is The Normal Case Here

In a city where three in four firms employ nine people or fewer, that applies to your supplier too.

A single developer is frequently the right choice: cheaper, faster, and genuinely accountable. The risk is not competence, it is availability, and it is a risk you manage rather than avoid.

The protections are all decided before the work starts. Code in a repository you own. Hosting and domains in your company’s name. Written documentation of how it runs. A list of the accounts and credentials somebody else would need.

A supplier who offers those unprompted has been on the wrong end of it before. One who resists is describing their retention strategy, and in a firm of four that is a genuine operating risk rather than a commercial preference.

Ask it plainly: if you stopped answering tomorrow, what would I need in order to carry on. A good answer takes thirty seconds.

What We Build And What We Decline

Narrow, and said before a meeting rather than during one.

We build the capture-to-record path: forms that reach a system, the joins between tools you already pay for, the automatic acknowledgements and follow-ups, and the tracking that connects an enquiry back to what produced it.

When a software developer hamilton is the wrong hire

Where an off-the-shelf tool already does the job, hiring a software developer hamilton to rebuild it is the most expensive way to get something slightly worse, and we would say so before quoting. Most small-firm problems here have a subscription answer costing a fraction of a build.

We also do not take on open-ended product development, bookkeeping, payroll or inventory systems, and we would not replace an industry package that already works.

Should we build something custom or buy a tool?

Buy, unless you can name something the tool genuinely cannot do that costs you real hours. In a city where 56.9% of firms employ fewer than four people, the usual answer is a subscription plus a small piece of joining work, at a fraction of a bespoke build and with somebody else maintaining it.

What is the highest-return small project?

Removing a retyping step. The quote lives in one system, the job in another and the invoice in a third, and somebody re-enters the same fields three times. Joining them takes days rather than months, costs less than any of the systems it connects, and removes the step most likely to introduce an error.

It is the normal case here and the risk is availability rather than competence.
