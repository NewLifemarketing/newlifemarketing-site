Go To Market Strategy Agency Toronto

Five Decisions, Then A Budget

Hiring a go to market strategy agency Toronto founders recommend should produce five decisions, not a deck: who exactly, what problem, what it displaces, how it reaches them, and what makes it believable. A budget set before those is a guess with a spreadsheet attached.

The context

Why A Go To Market Strategy Agency Toronto Starts By Subtracting

Toronto is the easiest market in the country to launch into and the hardest to be noticed in, and those are the same fact.

The City of Toronto's 2025 Employment Survey counted 5,160 establishments opening in a single year. Each of them is somebody's launch, and a good share of them will spend money describing themselves in terms that could describe four competitors.

In a thin market a vague proposition still gets found, because there is nothing else. Here it does not. The cost of being imprecise is not that people disagree with you; it is that nobody can tell you apart from the alternative they already have.

So the work here is mostly subtraction: narrowing who it is for until the claim becomes specific enough to be worth making.

The decisions

The Five Questions A Go To Market Strategy Agency Toronto Should Answer

The test for every answer is whether a reasonable person could disagree with it. If not, it is not a decision.

Who, exactly

Not a segment. A describable company with a describable person in it, specific enough that you could name ten today. "Mid-sized Ontario manufacturers" is a segment. "Operations directors at plants running two shifts with no maintenance system" is a decision.

What problem, in their words

Phrased as they would phrase it, not as your product describes it. The gap between those two phrasings is where most launch messaging dies, and the only way to close it is to ask ten of them.

What it displaces

Every purchase replaces something, including doing nothing in a spreadsheet. If you cannot name what loses when you win, you do not yet know what you are selling against, and the pricing will be wrong.

How it reaches them

One primary route, chosen because that is where they already are. Launches that list five channels have chosen none and will spread a budget too thin to learn from any of them.

What makes it believable

The proof a sceptic needs: a reference, a number, a guarantee, a pilot. New companies are short of this by definition, which makes deciding early what will serve as proof part of the strategy rather than an afterthought.

The common failure

Building The Machine Before Choosing The Target

Most of the wasted money in a launch is spent on infrastructure that assumes a decision nobody made.

The pattern is recognisable. A site is built, a CRM is configured, sequences are written, advertising accounts are opened, and only then does anybody discover that the audience is wrong or the message does not land. Every one of those assets now has to be redone, and the budget that would have funded the second attempt was spent on the first.

The cheaper order is to test the five answers before building anything that assumes them. Ten conversations with real buyers cost nothing but time and will settle more than a quarter of advertising. A single landing page and a small spend will tell you whether the phrasing lands, before a full site is built around it.

What a go to market strategy agency Toronto founders keep does in the first month

Talks to buyers, writes down the five answers, and tests the riskiest one for the smallest amount of money. Nothing gets built until the answer that would invalidate the build has been checked.

Sequencing

The First Ninety Days, Honestly

What should actually happen, in order, and what should deliberately not happen yet.

Weeks one to four: evidence

Buyer conversations, competitor teardown, and the five answers written down in a page anybody can argue with. No advertising, no building. The output is a document, and its value is that it can be wrong in public.

Weeks five to eight: the smallest test

One page, one message, one channel, a small budget. The question is not whether it produces revenue. It is whether the phrasing attracts the right person at a cost that could ever work.

Weeks nine to twelve: build what survived

Only now the site, the system and the sequences, built around the answer that held up rather than the one assumed in week one. This is also the point at which a budget means something.

The thing not to do in those ninety days is hire a team around an unvalidated proposition. It is the most expensive form of commitment available and the hardest to reverse.

Pricing the risk

Which Of The Five Would Hurt Most If It Were Wrong?

Rank the decisions by what it would cost to discover the answer late, then test in that order.

Not all five carry the same risk. Getting the channel wrong costs a quarter of wasted media and is reversible. Getting the buyer wrong costs the product roadmap, the pricing and the sales hiring, and is not.

So the sequence of testing should follow the cost of being wrong rather than the ease of testing. The temptation is the reverse, because channel tests are quick and satisfying while buyer interviews are slow and uncomfortable, and a quarter can disappear into optimising a channel that is pointed at the wrong person.

A useful exercise in the first week: for each of the five decisions, write one sentence describing what you would have to undo if it turned out to be wrong in twelve months. The decision with the longest sentence is the one to test first, and it is almost never the channel.

The second-order benefit is that it makes the strategy arguable. A document in which each decision carries its own stated downside is one a sceptical colleague can engage with, which is more than can be said for most launch decks.

Our part

What We Do And Where We Stop

A narrow and honest scope is more useful to a founder than a capability list.

We run the evidence phase, write the five answers with you, build and run the smallest test, and then build the machine around whatever survived — site, paid media, CRM and reporting. We manage over $70,000 a month in advertising spend for Ontario businesses, and the owner runs the paid media personally.

We do not do pricing strategy, channel partner recruitment, or sales team hiring. Those are specialist disciplines and a marketing firm offering all of them is offering a diagram rather than a service.

One practical note on how the evidence phase runs, because it is the part founders are most sceptical about. The buyer conversations are not surveys and they are not sales calls. They are twenty minutes asking somebody who has recently solved the problem you solve how they went about it: what triggered the search, who else was involved, what they looked at first, what nearly stopped them buying. Ten of those produce the vocabulary, the objections and the trigger events, and they produce them in the buyer’s words rather than yours.

Founders usually resist this on the grounds that they already know their market, and they usually do know it. What they do not have is the phrasing. A founder describes the product; a buyer describes a Tuesday that went badly. Advertising written in the first register reaches people who already understand the category, which in a new market is almost nobody.

The other reason to do it early is that these conversations are the cheapest source of the proof the fifth decision needs. Somebody who talks for twenty minutes about solving this problem is frequently willing to be a reference, a pilot or a quote, and asking at the end of the call costs nothing.

And where the evidence phase says the proposition is not ready, we would rather tell you that and stop than spend a launch budget demonstrating it slowly. That is the one promise a go to market strategy agency Toronto founders should insist on, because it is the only one that costs the agency anything.

What should a launch budget be?

Unanswerable until the five decisions are made, which is the point of the question. What can be said is that the budget should be split: a small amount for the test and the larger part held back until the test has told you which version to spend it on. Committing the whole budget to the first guess is the most common and most expensive launch error.

We already launched and it is not working. Is this still relevant?

More so, because now there is evidence. A launch that underperformed has produced data about who did and did not respond, which is exactly the input the five questions need and which a pre-launch company does not have. The useful work is a post-mortem that takes it seriously rather than a relaunch that changes the colours.

How long does this take?

The evidence phase is weeks, not months, and it is tempting to extend it because research is comfortable and commitment is not. The discipline is to stop at the point where more conversations stop changing the answers, write the five decisions down, and go and test the riskiest one.

Do you work with pre-revenue companies?

Sometimes, and the honest caveat is that the work is different: with no customers there is no closed-won history to build a target list from, so the evidence has to come from conversations with people who are not yet buyers. That is slower and noisier. It is still far cheaper than discovering the same thing through a year of advertising.

If you can name ten companies that should buy this, send the list. If you cannot, that is the first and cheapest piece of work.
