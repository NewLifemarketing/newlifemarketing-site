B2B SaaS Marketing

Pipeline You Can Actually Afford

B2B/Go-To-Market

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 28, 2026

B2B SaaS marketing is decided by one question most teams never ask out loud: who closes the deal? A product that a buyer signs up for alone, one that a rep has to walk through a committee, and one sold through a partner need three different marketing plans, and borrowing tactics from a company with a different answer is the most common way a software budget gets wasted.

The tactics are the easy part. Paid search, LinkedIn, content and email are available to everyone, and every competitor is running them. What separates the companies that grow from the ones that keep buying more leads is the order of the decisions underneath: the motion, the economics, the buyer, and what happens after the first invoice.

B2B SaaS Marketing Starts With the Motion

In the go-to-market work we do, the motion is one of six decisions taken in a fixed order: whether the product is sold by a person, bought without one, or routed through somebody else. It sits after segment, offer and pricing, and before launch, because the motion decides the cost of everything downstream.

Here is what each answer asks of marketing:

  • Bought without a person. Self-serve trials and freemium plans. Marketing owns the whole path from first visit to paid account, so the product itself, the onboarding and the pricing page are marketing assets. Volume matters, and the contract value is usually small enough that a sales call would cost more than it earns.
  • Sold by a person. Demos, pilots and procurement. Marketing's job is to create meetings with the right accounts and then arm the people who will argue for you inside them. A thousand sign-ups from companies that will never pass security review are worth less than six conversations at the right ones.
  • Routed through somebody else. Resellers, integrators, marketplaces. Your buyer is first the partner, who needs a reason to recommend you over the products they already know how to sell.

Most software companies end up running more than one. That is fine, as long as each is planned on purpose. The failure is the hybrid nobody chose: a product priced for self-serve, sold by reps who cannot afford to spend time on it, marketed with content written for a buyer who never sees it.

So the first piece of B2B SaaS marketing is a sentence, written down: who closes the deal at each price point. Everything below depends on it.

The Numbers That Set the Budget

Software has an advantage most businesses do not. The revenue recurs, so you can work out what a customer is worth before deciding what you can afford to spend getting one. The most widely used version of that arithmetic comes from David Skok, whose guide SaaS Metrics 2.0 sets out two rules of thumb:

  1. Lifetime value of a customer should be more than three times what it cost to acquire them. The best businesses he describes run at seven or eight.
  2. The cost of acquiring a customer should be recovered within about 12 months. Longer than that, and growth eats cash faster than it returns it.

Skok is clear that these are guidelines, and there are good reasons to break them for a while. Their value is that they turn "how much should we spend on marketing?" into a question with an answer. If you know your gross margin, your average contract and how long customers stay, you know the ceiling on acquisition cost. Spend under it and you can afford to grow. Spend over it and every new customer makes the hole deeper.

Why Cost per Lead Misleads B2B SaaS Marketing

The common mistake is to manage B2B SaaS marketing by cost per lead, because it is the number the ad platforms report. A cheap lead that never becomes an opportunity is the most expensive thing you can buy. We report cost per opportunity rather than cost per lead, and win rate by source, so the cheap channel that fills the CRM with companies who will never sign shows up as what it is.

Pick the Segment You Win First

Software can be sold to almost anybody, which is the problem. A product that could serve accountants, logistics firms and hospitals will be marketed to all three, and the message ends up general enough that none of them recognise themselves in it.

The discipline is to pick one segment to win first, chosen because it is reachable and because winning it makes the next one easier. Reachable means you can name where those buyers already spend attention. Easier means a reference in the first segment is believable to the second.

Then describe the offer as the outcome somebody buys, not the capability you have built. Product teams describe features, because features are what they made. Buyers pay for a result: a close finished three days sooner, a compliance audit passed, a sales team that stops re-keying orders. The gap between those two descriptions is where most launches quietly fail.

Price belongs in the same conversation. Price is a positioning decision before it is a revenue one, and in software it also decides the motion. A plan at $49 a month cannot pay for a sales call. A contract at $60,000 a year cannot be bought with a credit card by one person. Set the number and you have set much of the marketing.

Selling Software to a Committee

Above the self-serve tier, software is not bought by a person. A meaningful deal goes to a committee of six to ten people, most of whom never fill in a form, and each one is asking a different question:

ReaderThe question they bringWhat answers it
Economic buyerDoes this move a number I own, safely?Outcomes in figures, from companies like theirs
ChampionCan I put my name behind this?A short case they can forward without rewriting it
Technical evaluatorWill it work with what we already run?Integration detail, documentation, a matching reference
BlockerWhat risk does this create for security, legal or procurement?Answers published before the question is asked

A deal that runs through one person dies when that person goes quiet, changes role or gets overruled. So B2B SaaS marketing aimed at larger accounts has to reach the rest of the committee too. Where your market is a countable list of companies, that is what account-based marketing is for: tiering the accounts, mapping who sits on each committee, and running a play for each tier.

The blocker is the one software marketers forget. A security questionnaire that takes three weeks to answer stalls more deals than any competitor. A published page covering data residency, access controls and certifications is marketing, even though nobody on the marketing team wants to write it.

Capture Is Not the Whole Market

Most software marketing budgets go to capture: search ads on category terms, review-site listings, retargeting. All of it is aimed at the small slice of the market already looking, and every competitor is bidding on the same slice. That is why cost per lead climbs every year while the pipeline stays flat.

The arithmetic behind the slice comes from a 2021 B2B Institute paper by John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. He noted that corporations change providers such as their principal bank around once every five years, so perhaps 5% of a market is buying in any given quarter. He was explicit that this is not a precise rule. The direction holds for most software categories: at any moment, most of the companies that will buy in the next two years are not looking.

Demand generation is the work aimed at them. It names the problem in the buyer's words, so that when the problem becomes urgent they already know who solves it. It is slower and harder to attribute than capture, and it compounds over two to four quarters. We cover the mechanics in our post on demand creation.

Use the signals you already own

Software companies sit on better buying signals than almost anyone. A named contact returning three times in a week, a pricing page read twice, a trial account that invites four colleagues: these are first-party signals, they are accurate, and they cost nothing. Most companies capture none of them because nobody connected the product and the site to the CRM. Exhaust those before paying for third-party intent data, which tells you where to look rather than what will close.

B2B SaaS Marketing After the Signature

The first signature is the smallest revenue event in the relationship. In a recurring-revenue business, a customer who renews and expands is worth several times one who signs and leaves, and the cost of acquiring them has already been paid. Yet most B2B SaaS marketing plans stop at the closed deal, and expansion is left to whoever remembers to ask at renewal.

Treat existing customers as a segment with its own plan:

  • Onboarding is a campaign. The first few weeks decide whether the product becomes a habit. Lifecycle email and in-product messages that get each new account to its first result are worth more than another top-of-funnel channel.
  • Adoption predicts renewal. Accounts where usage is falling are telling you something months before the cancellation notice. Route that signal to a person the same way you would route a hot prospect.
  • Expansion has its own committee. The team that bought the first licence is rarely the one that buys the second. The next department needs its own reason, and a reference from inside its own company is the strongest one available.

Retention also feeds acquisition. Customers who stay produce the case studies, references and reviews that the next buying committee asks for, and those are the proof assets that make the rest of the marketing believable.

How to Measure B2B SaaS Marketing

Agree the measures before anything is spent, and keep the fast ones and the slow ones on separate lines. Capture converts this month. Creation compounds over quarters. Reporting them as one number hides which is working, and the slower half gets cut the first time a quarter tightens.

  • Pipeline created and influenced, not leads.
  • Cost per opportunity, and the payback period it implies against your contract value.
  • Win rate by source, so a channel is judged on the deals it produces, not the forms.
  • The split between demand creation and capture, with its own targets for each.
  • Branded search volume over a quarter, the clearest sign that more of the market knows your name.
  • Net revenue retention: what this year's customers pay next year, after churn and expansion.

Label platform-reported numbers as platform-reported, state the attribution window on every view, and flag early data as early. A board that has been shown a summed revenue figure from three ad platforms once will not trust the next report, however accurate it is.

Decide the Order, Then Spend

Good B2B SaaS marketing looks like a channel plan from the outside. Underneath, it is a short list of decisions taken in order: who closes the deal, what a customer is worth, which segment you win first, who sits on the committee, how you reach the buyers who are not looking yet, and what happens after the contract.

  • Write down the motion at each price point before choosing a single channel.
  • Use lifetime value and payback to set the ceiling on acquisition cost, and manage to cost per opportunity.
  • Win one reachable segment first, and sell the outcome rather than the feature list.
  • Reach the whole buying committee, including the blocker, and arm the champion.
  • Fund demand creation separately from capture, and treat existing customers as a segment with its own plan.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask before they set a software marketing budget.

What is B2B SaaS marketing?

It is the marketing of subscription software to businesses. It differs from other B2B marketing in two ways: revenue recurs, so retention and expansion matter as much as acquisition, and the sales motion can range from a self-serve trial to a months-long committee purchase. The motion decides most of the plan.

How much should a SaaS company spend to acquire a customer?

David Skok's widely used guidelines are a lifetime value of more than three times acquisition cost, and acquisition cost recovered within about 12 months. Work out your own ceiling from gross margin, contract value and how long customers stay, then manage spend against cost per opportunity rather than cost per lead.

Should we be product-led or sales-led?

Let the price and the buyer decide. A low-priced product one person can adopt alone suits self-serve. A larger contract that needs security review and several sign-offs needs a person. Many companies run both at different price points, which works when each is planned on purpose rather than drifted into.

Which channels work best for B2B software?

Whichever ones reach your first segment, at a cost your unit economics can carry. Search and review sites capture buyers already looking; content, video, LinkedIn and account-based programmes reach the larger group who are not looking yet. A channel earns its place on pipeline and win rate, not on lead volume.

How long does B2B SaaS marketing take to show results?

Capture can produce opportunities within weeks. Demand creation compounds over two to four quarters, and shows first as branded search volume, direct traffic no campaign claims and enquiries from people who already knew the name. Agree both sets of measures before you start, so the slower half is not cut early.
