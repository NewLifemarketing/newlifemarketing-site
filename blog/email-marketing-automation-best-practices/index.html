Email Marketing Automation Best Practices

Rules for When Your Emails Should Stop

Marketing Automation/Email/B2B

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 28, 2026

Email marketing automation best practices are mostly written for shops selling to one person who buys in one sitting. Send a welcome series, recover the abandoned cart, wish them a happy birthday. In B2B none of that is the hard part. The hard part is the email that should not have gone out: the nurture step that lands the day after the buyer booked a call, the fourth message in a week because three workflows each thought it was their turn, the "still interested?" sent to someone whose consent ran out last spring.

So the practices below are mostly about stopping. When a workflow ends, how many emails one person can receive, how long you are allowed to keep writing to them, and how to prove the whole thing produced anything at all.

Email Marketing Automation Best Practices Start With When to Stop

Every workflow gets a goal and an exit before it gets a first email. The goal is the thing the sequence exists to cause. The exit is the list of events that end it, whether or not the sequence has finished.

In B2B the exit list is longer than the goal, and it is the part teams skip:

  • They replied. To anything, from anyone on your side. A reply means a conversation has started, and a conversation does not need a drip running underneath it.
  • They booked. A meeting on the calendar ends every nurture aimed at getting one.
  • An opportunity opened on their account. Not on their contact record. On the account. Once a rep is working the deal, marketing email to anyone at that company should either stop or switch to a sequence the rep has approved.
  • They became a customer. Prospect nurture and customer email are different programmes. A new customer getting "see how we compare" a week after signing reads as a system that does not know who they are.
  • They unsubscribed or bounced. Obvious, and still the one that most often leaks, because the unsubscribe lives in one tool and the sequence in another.

Write the exits as triggers in the platform, not as a note in the brief. A rule that depends on somebody remembering to pull a contact out is a rule that will be broken the week that person is on holiday.

Trigger on What the Account Did, Not on a Date

"Day 3, day 7, day 14" is a schedule, not a reason. The emails that get read are the ones that arrive because something happened: a second visit to the pricing page, a webinar attended by a new person at an account you are already talking to, a deal stage that has not moved in three weeks.

Most of those signals are already in your own systems. Your site, your CRM, your email and your sales-engagement data are, as our intent and signal marketing guide puts it, under-used at most companies and cost nothing extra. Before buying a data source, wire up the ones you own.

Roll the Signal Up to the Account

A B2B purchase is made by a group, so read behaviour at the account level. One person downloading a guide is curiosity. Three people from the same company on the same week's pricing page is a buying committee doing its homework, and it deserves a different email, or a call instead of one. Our post on CRM marketing automation covers how to structure the records so that roll-up is possible.

One Contact, Five Workflows: Set a Frequency Cap

Each workflow is usually built by someone thinking only about that workflow. The webinar follow-up, the content nurture, the product update, the event invite and the newsletter are each reasonable on their own. Stacked on one person in the same week, they are five emails from a company that has not yet earned one.

Three settings fix it:

  1. A global cap. A ceiling on marketing emails per person per rolling seven days, enforced by the platform across every workflow. Two is a sensible starting point for a cold or warming contact. Raise it only when the numbers say you can.
  2. A priority order. When two workflows want the same slot, one wins. Something triggered by what the person just did outranks a scheduled send. A send tied to an open deal outranks both.
  3. A suppression list that every workflow reads. Current customers in an active renewal, accounts in legal review, anyone a rep has flagged. One list, checked by every send, not a copy in each workflow.

The cap is the least glamorous of these email marketing automation best practices and the one that changes results fastest, because the damage it prevents is invisible in any single workflow's report.

Consent Has an Expiry Date

If you email Canadians, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation decides how long you may keep writing to someone who never explicitly opted in. Per the Government of Canada's guidance on getting consent to send email, implied consent from a business relationship is valid for up to two years, or six months in the case of an inquiry or application. And every unsubscribe request must be actioned within 10 business days, at no cost to the recipient.

That makes consent a date, and dates are what automation is good at:

  • Store the consent type (express or implied) and the date it was given as fields on the contact, not as a tag somebody added once.
  • Calculate the expiry for implied consent: purchase date plus two years, inquiry date plus six months.
  • Run a workflow that asks for express consent before the expiry, and moves anyone who does not give it out of marketing sends on the day it lapses.

Most B2B lists are full of inquiry contacts whose six months ran out long ago. Asking them properly is also a decent re-engagement campaign, because the ones who say yes are the ones worth writing to.

Earn the Inbox Before You Write a Word

Copy does not matter if the email lands in spam. Google's published sender guidelines set the bar for anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts:

RequirementWhat Google asks for
AuthenticationSPF, DKIM and DMARC, all three, for bulk senders
Spam rateBelow 0.30% as reported in Postmaster Tools, with 0.10% as the level to aim for
UnsubscribeOne-click unsubscribe on marketing messages, plus a clearly visible unsubscribe link in the body

Most B2B programmes sit under 5,000 a day, and the same rules are still the right floor. A spam complaint rate is a measure of how many people you are emailing who did not want it, and every practice above, the exits, the cap and the consent expiry, is a way of keeping that number low before it becomes a deliverability problem.

Keep marketing email on its own sending domain or subdomain, separate from the one your sales team uses for one-to-one mail. If a campaign goes wrong, the reputation damage stays with the campaign and the reps' inboxes keep working.

Write Each Email for One Role and One Next Step

A nurture email written for "the buyer" is written for nobody, because in B2B the buyer is several people who care about different things. The finance lead wants the cost and the risk. The person who will use it wants to know it will not make their week worse. Write the email for one of them, and send it to that role.

Then give it one next step. Not a menu of three links and a calendar button. One thing the reader can do, sized to how far along they are: a short guide early, a comparison in the middle, a conversation at the end. Our B2B email marketing post goes further into writing for each role.

Plain Text: The Most Overlooked of the Email Marketing Automation Best Practices

Designed templates suit newsletters and announcements. Once an account is close to a decision, an email that looks like it came from a person, because it did, usually does better than one that looks like a campaign. Sign it from the rep who owns the account, write it in their voice, and make it easy to reply to.

Decide Where the Automation Hands Off to a Person

Automation is good at being fast and consistent. It is bad at judgement. The best practice is to decide, in writing, which moments it handles and which it passes to a person.

On the CRM systems we build, each automation is designed around the client's sales process using triggers such as a new lead, a booking, a pipeline-stage change or a call status, actions such as sending an email or SMS, waiting, updating fields or notifying someone, and branching logic. An instant text and email go out within seconds of a form fill or ad lead, and leads nobody reached drop into a timed drip so they do not go cold. That is the automation's job: speed and persistence.

What it should not do is negotiate, answer an objection or decide whether an account is worth a senior person's time. Those moments get a notification to a named owner, with the context attached, and the workflow stops. Our CRM buildout and automation service covers how that whole lead-to-customer system is set up.

Judge Email Marketing Automation Best Practices on Pipeline, With a Holdout

Open rates were always a weak signal and are now a misleading one. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, in Apple's own description, prevents senders from knowing when you open an email, so an open recorded from Apple Mail no longer means a person read anything. Clicks are better. Neither tells you whether the automation caused anything.

The test that does is a holdout. Take a random slice of the contacts who qualify for a workflow, say 10%, and do not send it to them. After a full sales cycle, compare the two groups on what you actually care about: meetings booked, opportunities opened, revenue closed. If the group that got the emails is not ahead, the workflow is busy rather than useful.

Report it at the account level, against pipeline, the same way an account-based programme is judged on account engagement and pipeline rather than leads. And close the loop in the other direction too: closed-won deals can be pushed back to LinkedIn and Google as offline conversions, which lets those platforms optimise toward revenue rather than form fills.

A One-Hour Audit Against These Email Marketing Automation Best Practices

Most teams do not need new workflows. They need the existing ones checked against the rules above. Open your platform and answer these:

QuestionWhat good looks like
Does every active workflow have a written goal and exits?Reply, meeting, open opportunity, customer, unsubscribe and bounce all end it
What is the most email one contact received last week?At or under your cap, across every workflow combined
Is consent stored with a type and a date?Yes, with implied consent expiring automatically
Are SPF, DKIM and DMARC passing on the sending domain?All three, on a domain separate from the reps' mail
How many workflows have a holdout group?At least the two that send the most
Can you name the person each workflow hands off to?A named owner, not "sales"

Any "no" is the next thing to fix. Our post on common mistakes in marketing automation covers what usually sits behind them, and the marketing automation strategy post covers which programmes to build first if you are starting over.

The Bottom Line

The email marketing automation best practices that matter in B2B are less about what to send than about when not to. Give every workflow exits that fire on what the account does, cap what one person can receive across all of them, treat consent as a date that runs out, meet Google's sender requirements as a floor, and hand the judgement calls to a person. Then prove it worked with a group that did not get the emails.

  • Write the exits before the first email: reply, meeting, open opportunity, customer, unsubscribe.
  • Trigger on what the account did, and read signals at the account level.
  • One frequency cap and one suppression list, read by every workflow.
  • Under CASL, implied consent lapses: two years from a purchase, six months from an inquiry.
  • Judge it on pipeline against a holdout, not on opens.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing leaders ask when their automated email is busy but the pipeline is not moving.

What are the most important email marketing automation best practices?

Give every workflow a goal and exit rules before writing the first email, trigger sends on behaviour rather than dates, cap how many emails one person receives across all workflows, store consent with a type and a date, authenticate the sending domain, and measure against a holdout group on pipeline rather than open rates.

How many automated emails should a B2B contact receive per week?

There is no universal number, but set one ceiling that every workflow respects. Two marketing emails per person per rolling seven days is a sensible starting point for a contact who is not yet in a sales conversation. Raise it only if a holdout test shows the extra sends produce more meetings or pipeline.

How long does implied consent last under CASL?

According to the Government of Canada's guidance, implied consent from a business relationship is valid for up to two years, or six months in the case of an inquiry or application. Unsubscribe requests must be actioned within 10 business days, at no cost to the recipient.

Do I need SPF, DKIM and DMARC for marketing email?

Google requires all three of anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail accounts, along with a spam rate below 0.30% and one-click unsubscribe on marketing messages. Below that volume they are still the right floor, because they are what lets inbox providers confirm the email really came from you.

How do you measure whether email automation is working?

Hold back a random share of eligible contacts, around 10%, and do not send them the workflow. After a full sales cycle, compare meetings, opportunities and revenue between the two groups. If the group that received the emails is not ahead, the workflow is not earning its place.
