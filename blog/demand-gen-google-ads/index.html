Demand Gen Google Ads

The One Google Campaign Built for Buyers Who Aren’t Searching Yet

B2B/Google Ads

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 20, 2026

Every Google Ads campaign type except one waits for somebody to type something. Search, Shopping and most of Performance Max are built to catch a buyer in the act of looking. Demand Gen is the exception. It puts an image or a video in front of people scrolling YouTube, Discover and Gmail who were not looking for you at all.

For a B2B company with a long sales cycle, that difference is the whole point, and it is also why most accounts get Demand Gen Google Ads wrong. They run it like Search, optimise it toward a form fill, judge it on cost per lead after six weeks and switch it off. This post is the other way to run it: as the paid half of demand creation, aimed at the buying committee that will be in market next year.

What Demand Gen Actually Is

Demand Gen Google Ads is the campaign type that replaced Discovery. According to Google’s own help documentation, it serves ads on YouTube (including Shorts), Discover, Gmail and the Google Display Network, in image, video, carousel and product-feed formats. One campaign, one set of assets, cut for feeds rather than for results pages.

Two things make it different from the rest of the account. The first is that nobody asked. A Demand Gen ad interrupts somebody watching a video or reading their inbox, which means it behaves much more like paid social than like Search. The second is audience. Demand Gen is where Google offers lookalike segments, built from your own first-party data, so you can reach people who resemble your customers rather than people who typed a keyword.

It is also getting bigger. Google is folding standalone Display campaigns into Demand Gen: the migration tool opened to eligible advertisers in June 2026, and campaigns not moved by hand are being migrated automatically through 2027. If your account still has a Display campaign, it is going to become a Demand Gen campaign whether or not you plan for it. Better to plan for it.

Where It Fits in a B2B Account

Most B2B buyers are not in market most of the time. A company replaces its accounting platform, its ERP or its agency every few years, and in the quarters between, nothing you bid on in Search will reach them because they are not searching. Search catches the narrow slice that is. Demand Gen is one of the few paid tools Google gives you for the rest.

That is exactly how we scope it on our own Google Ads work. For a B2B company with a long sales cycle, the plan we publish is Search on high-intent terms only, plus YouTube and Demand Gen for the long middle where nobody is searching yet. The two jobs are different, so they get different budgets, different measures and different patience.

Three signs an account is ready for Demand Gen Google Ads:

  • Search is already tidy. Conversion tracking verified, a negative list maintained, the search terms report read weekly. Demand Gen spends money creating searches; if the Search campaign leaks, you are paying twice to lose the buyer you created.
  • You have a point of view worth repeating. A clear problem you want to be known for. Without one, Demand Gen has nothing to say except your logo.
  • You have creative, or the means to make it. We will come back to this, because it decides more than the budget does.

If none of those is true yet, the honest answer is that the first dollar belongs in Search and the tracking under it. A Demand Gen campaign launched on top of a broken account produces a lovely reach number and nothing you can act on.

Seed It With the Accounts You Want

The audience is where B2B teams can get far more out of Demand Gen than a consumer brand can, because they usually know exactly who they want to reach. The tool for it is the lookalike segment.

Google builds a lookalike from a seed list of your first-party data: a Customer Match list, or people who have interacted with your website, app or YouTube channel. It then finds similar users in your target location at one of three reach levels, as Google describes them: narrow aims for about 2.5% of users there, balanced (the default) about 5% and broad about 10%. The segments refresh every one to two days.

The quality of the seed is the whole game. A list of everyone who ever visited your site teaches the model to find people who visit sites. A list of closed-won customers, or of the contacts at opportunities that reached a sales stage, teaches it something worth learning. For B2B, three seeds tend to work:

  1. Closed-won customers from the CRM, as a Customer Match upload. The strongest signal you have, and usually the smallest list.
  2. Contacts at open opportunities, if the list is big enough. These are the people inside accounts that already look like buyers.
  3. High-intent site visitors: pricing page, case studies, the demo page. Not the blog, which is full of students, job seekers and competitors.

Start narrow. A broad lookalike in a B2B market reaches a lot of people who resemble your customers only in that they own a phone.

One change to watch. Google has said the default behaviour of Demand Gen lookalikes will evolve toward a suggestion mode, where the seed acts more as a signal and the system may reach beyond the reach level you chose. For a consumer brand that may be fine. For a B2B team running Demand Gen Google Ads against a defined buyer profile it can quietly widen the audience, and Google provides an opt-out form to keep lookalikes working as a strict targeting constraint. Decide which you want on purpose rather than discovering it in the placement report.

If you sell to a named list of accounts rather than a broad market, treat Demand Gen as one layer of an account-based marketing programme rather than the whole of it. Google cannot target a company the way LinkedIn can. What it can do is put video in front of people who look like that company’s buying committee, at a fraction of the cost per impression, while LinkedIn does the precise work.

The Creative Is the Targeting

In Search, the keyword does the selecting: whoever typed it is the audience. In a feed there is no keyword, so the ad has to do the selecting itself. The first two seconds of the video, the headline on the image, the problem it names: those decide who stops and who scrolls past. That is why weak creative is where Demand Gen leaks most, and it is why most Google-only agencies quietly avoid the campaign type. It is a visual, interruption-based channel, and the rules that govern paid social apply.

What good Demand Gen creative looks like for a B2B advertiser:

  • It names the problem in the first line or the first two seconds. “Your month-end close is two days late because of three spreadsheets” selects a finance lead. “Innovative solutions for modern teams” selects nobody.
  • It comes from a person. A founder or a practitioner explaining something on camera is remembered in a way a logo animation is not.
  • It is cut for the placement. Vertical for Shorts, square or landscape for in-feed, a still with a strong headline for Gmail and Discover. One horizontal video stretched across all of them looks like what it is.
  • It is a set, not a single ad. Several angles on the same problem, rotated, so the people you reach see the idea more than once without seeing the same ad until they hate it.
  • It asks for attention, not a demo. Most of the people who see it are not ready to talk to sales. An ad that demands a meeting filters for the handful who are, and wastes the rest.

This is the clearest overlap between our Google work and our creative production. Our Demand Gen Google Ads campaigns are fed from our own shoot days, which is the main reason we treat it as a real channel rather than an afterthought. If you cannot produce for it, you are better off not running it than running stock footage with your logo on the end.

Choose the Conversion Before the Bid

Here is the decision that sinks most B2B Demand Gen Google Ads campaigns, and it is made before anyone looks at a bid strategy. What are you telling Google a success looks like?

Google’s bidding works from conversions, and it needs enough of them to learn. A B2B company that books six demos a month cannot feed a conversion-based strategy with demos alone. Google’s own Demand Gen guidance recognises this: where full conversions are scarce, it suggests optimising toward lighter events such as a site visit. And for a target CPA strategy, Google recommends a daily budget of at least 10 times that target.

The trap is in the lighter event. Tell the system that a page view is a success and it will find you page views: the cheapest, least interested visitors on the network, at a cost per conversion that looks wonderful in the report. The campaign is succeeding at the thing you asked for and failing at the thing you needed.

A better ladder for B2B:

  1. Start on reach or clicks while the account has no Demand Gen history, and judge the first weeks on who you reached and what they did next, not on cost per lead.
  2. Define a quality engagement event that a tyre-kicker rarely triggers: a pricing page view after a case study, a video watched to the end, a return visit within 14 days. Make it the conversion the campaign optimises toward.
  3. Import offline conversions from the CRM, so that qualified opportunities and closed deals flow back into Google Ads against the click or view that started them. This is part of the conversion tracking we set up before a dollar is spent on any account, and for B2B it is what turns Demand Gen from a guess into a system.
  4. Move to value-based bidding only once those imported conversions are arriving in enough volume to learn from.

Two more settings worth deciding on purpose. Demand Gen offers channel controls, so you can prioritise Google’s own channels or the Display Network rather than letting the campaign drift into whichever inventory is cheapest. And brand safety: check where the ads actually ran in the first fortnight, and exclude what does not belong next to your name.

Prove It Without a Last Click

Demand Gen Google Ads will almost always lose a last-click comparison with Search, and it should. Its job is to make somebody search for you next quarter. When they do, the conversion is credited to the Search ad they clicked, and the campaign that created the search shows nothing.

Judge it on that basis and it gets switched off every time. So agree in advance what it will be judged on, and report it separately from Search. The measures we use for the creation side of demand generation were chosen because none of them depends on a click:

  • Branded search volume over time. The most direct evidence that more people know your name.
  • Direct traffic to key pages. People who typed you in rather than clicking anything.
  • Self-reported attribution. A free-text “How did you hear about us?” field. Buyers will mention the YouTube video that no pixel connected to the deal.
  • Sales acceptance rate and win rate by source. Buyers who already understand the problem arrive better qualified.

Google adds its own tools on top. Its Demand Gen documentation points to brand lift, search lift and conversion lift studies to judge whether the spend is paying off, and supports data-driven attribution. Lift studies are the closest thing to a controlled experiment you will get from the platform, and Brand Lift is available on an allowlist basis, so ask about access before you need it.

A First 90 Days That Survives Review

Demand Gen Google Ads campaigns rarely fail on the first day. They fail at the first quarterly review, when somebody puts them next to Search and asks why the cost per lead is four times higher. A launch plan that expects that review is the one that survives it.

  1. Before launch: verify conversion tracking, build the offline conversion import, and write down what the campaign will be judged on and when. Take a baseline of branded search and direct traffic.
  2. Weeks 1 to 2: launch with a narrow lookalike from your best seed, three to five creative angles cut for each placement, and channel controls set on purpose. Read the placement report at the end of week two.
  3. Weeks 3 to 6: drop the creative that nobody watches, make more of what holds attention, and exclude placements that do not belong next to your name. Leave the bid strategy alone; changing it every week resets the learning.
  4. Weeks 7 to 12: test a second seed or a wider reach level against the first. Start reading branded search and self-reported attribution against the baseline.
  5. Day 90: report creation and capture separately. Expect branded search and inbound quality to move before pipeline does, and say so in the report before anybody asks.

That last line is the one to agree with finance before launch, not during the review. The creation half of demand generation compounds over two to four quarters. A 90-day report should show it moving in the right direction, not paying back in full.

Run It Like Demand Creation, Not Like Search

Demand Gen is the one place in Google Ads built for buyers who are not looking yet, which makes it the most useful Google campaign for a B2B company with a long sales cycle and the easiest one to judge by the wrong standard. Seed it with the accounts you want, feed it creative that names a problem, optimise toward a conversion that means something, and measure it on lift rather than last click. Do those four and it earns its budget. Skip them and it becomes the cheapest traffic in the account and the first line cut.

  • Demand Gen runs on YouTube, Shorts, Discover, Gmail and Display, and Display campaigns are being migrated into it through 2027.
  • Seed lookalikes from closed-won customers and real opportunities, start narrow, and decide on purpose whether to opt out of suggestion mode.
  • In a feed the creative does the targeting. Name the problem in the first line, cut for each placement, and run a set.
  • Never optimise toward a cheap event. Build a quality engagement event, import offline conversions, then move to value-based bidding.
  • Judge it on branded search, direct traffic, self-reported attribution and lift studies, reported separately from Search.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B teams ask before they put budget behind a Google campaign nobody searched for.

What are Demand Gen campaigns in Google Ads?

Demand Gen Google Ads campaigns replaced Discovery campaigns. They show image, video, carousel and product-feed ads on YouTube (including Shorts), Discover, Gmail and the Google Display Network. Unlike Search, it reaches people who are not actively looking, which makes it a tool for creating demand rather than capturing it.

Is Demand Gen good for B2B?

Yes, for B2B companies with a long sales cycle and creative worth putting in a feed. It reaches the large share of the market that is not searching yet. It is a poor first campaign for an account whose Search and conversion tracking are not working, and it should be judged on lift and branded search rather than cost per lead.

What is the difference between Demand Gen and Performance Max?

Performance Max runs one campaign across all of Google’s inventory, including Search and Shopping, and allocates budget between them itself. Demand Gen runs only on visual, feed-based inventory and gives you direct control over audiences, including lookalike segments, and over creative by placement. Performance Max mostly captures demand; Demand Gen is built to create it.

How much budget does a Demand Gen campaign need?

Google does not set a hard minimum for Demand Gen Google Ads, but for target CPA bidding it recommends a daily budget of at least 10 times your target CPA. For B2B, plan the budget around a full quarter of steady running rather than a short test, because the results that matter show up in branded search and pipeline over several months.

What audiences work best for Demand Gen in B2B?

Lookalike segments seeded from your best first-party data: closed-won customers and contacts at real opportunities, uploaded through Customer Match, or high-intent site visitors such as pricing and case-study viewers. Start at the narrow reach level, which Google aims at about 2.5% of users in the target location, and widen only once it is working.
