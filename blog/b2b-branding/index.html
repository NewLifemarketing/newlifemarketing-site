B2B Branding

The Brand Is the Reason You Win

B2B/Positioning

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 27, 2026

B2B branding gets handed to a designer far too early. The logo, the palette and the new site arrive first, and the harder question underneath them, why a buyer should pick you over the company they already use, is left for the sales team to answer on its own, one call at a time.

A brand in a business market is not what you look like. It is what a buying committee already believes you are for, before anyone from your company is in the room. That belief can be decided, written down, funded and checked. Most companies do none of the four, and then wonder why the category reads as interchangeable.

B2B Branding Is a Decision, Not a Logo

Ask five people at a company what it is for and count the answers. If sales describes the customer one way and marketing another, you do not have one brand. You have two funnels, two messages and a permanent argument about lead quality.

That is the real starting point for B2B branding: a short set of decisions somebody owns. Who exactly is this for? What are we the obvious choice for, against the incumbent and against doing nothing? What do we refuse to be? A logo cannot answer any of those, and a new logo on an undecided position is a more expensive version of the same confusion.

The symptoms of an undecided position are easy to spot once you know them:

  • You win deals you should not, and lose ones you should. In our go-to-market work this is almost always a positioning problem: the offer is being understood as something adjacent to what it is.
  • A competitor repositioned and now you sound like them. Differentiation that was never written down erodes quietly, until the category reads as a list of interchangeable names.
  • Every rep pitches a slightly different company. Each one has filled the gap with their own version, and the market hears all of them.

None of these are fixed by design. All of them are fixed by deciding, and then saying the decision the same way everywhere.

Most of Your Buyers Are Not Buying Yet

The case for brand in a business market rests on one uncomfortable piece of arithmetic. In a 2021 paper for the B2B Institute, Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute pointed out that corporations change providers such as their principal bank or law firm around once every five years. If roughly a fifth of buyers are in the market over a year, something like 5% are in it in any given quarter.

Dawes was clear that the 95% is not a precise rule, and your category will have its own cycle. The direction holds almost everywhere, though: at any moment, most of the companies that will buy from you in the next few years are not looking.

That changes what marketing is for. A lead-capture programme only talks to the small slice already searching, and so does every competitor, which is why cost per lead climbs while the pipeline stays flat. Brand is the work aimed at everyone else. Its job is to be the name that comes to mind first, and comes with the right meaning attached, on the day a buyer's situation changes and they start to look.

Advertising that reaches someone eighteen months before they buy cannot be judged on this month's form fills. It works by building memory, and memory only pays out later.

How Much of the Budget Is Brand

The most quoted answer comes from Les Binet and Peter Field, whose research for the same institute found that B2B businesses grow best when roughly 46% of the budget goes to brand building and 54% to activation, the marketing that asks for an immediate response. The same work found that marketing which appeals to emotion is far better at building brands, and that B2B brands need that appeal too.

Treat 46:54 as a benchmark, not a law. A company with a long sales cycle and a countable market will weight it differently from one selling a self-serve product to thousands of firms. What the number does settle is the argument most leadership teams are actually having: whether B2B branding deserves real money at all. It does, and in most B2B plans it is getting far less than half.

The practical problem is not the split. It is that both halves are usually bought from one budget and judged by one report. The half with clean, fast numbers keeps its funding, and the half that compounds slowly gets cut in the first difficult quarter. Two years later the pipeline has stopped growing, and nobody connects it to the cut.

The fix is administrative. Give the brand half its own targets and its own timescale, agreed before anything is spent. We cover that in more detail in our post on demand creation, which is the same problem seen from the pipeline end.

Write the Position Down First

In the go-to-market framework we use, positioning is not a standalone exercise. It sits inside the Offer decision, next to proof, objection handling, pricing and packaging, because those are the same question asked four ways. A position you cannot prove, defend in an objection or price consistently is a slogan.

Write it down as a document a new hire could read on day one:

  1. The segment you win first. Not everybody. One group, chosen because it is reachable and because winning it makes the next one easier.
  2. The outcome you sell, described as what somebody buys rather than the capability you have built. The gap between those two is where launches quietly fail.
  3. The alternative you replace, named. Usually the incumbent, sometimes a spreadsheet, often doing nothing.
  4. The proof that makes the claim believable to a sceptic: a result, a reference, a specification.
  5. The price, and what it signals. Price is a positioning decision before it is a revenue one, and it is the hardest thing to change later without explaining yourself.

Build it from evidence rather than opinion. Customer interviews and lost-deal interviews tell you what buyers actually believe about you, which is often not what the leadership team believes. A competitive teardown tells you which claims are already taken.

When the brand is new

One launch we are running now shows why the order matters. A Health Canada licensed manufacturer in Thunder Bay spent four years making product under other companies' labels and is now launching its own brand. Before any site or campaign, the work was positioning: who the brand is for, what it stands on, and how it is allowed to say it in a tightly regulated category. The launch is recent, so there are no results to publish yet. The sequence is the point: decide first, design second.

One Brand, Several Readers

A consumer brand has to make sense to one person. A business brand has to make sense to a group, and each member of it is answering a different question. In account-based programmes we write the value proposition separately for the economic buyer, the champion, the technical evaluator and the blocker, and the same discipline applies to brand.

ReaderWhat they need the brand to meanWhat proves it
Economic buyerA safe decision that moves a number they ownOutcomes in figures, from companies like theirs
ChampionA name they can put forward without risking their ownMaterial they can forward without rewriting it
Technical evaluatorSomething that will actually work in their environmentSpecifications, integration detail, a matching reference
BlockerNothing that creates risk for procurement, legal or securityAnswers to the hard questions before they are asked

This is not five brands. It is one position, expressed so that each reader finds the part that answers them. A brand built only for the economic buyer looks confident and then stalls in security review. One built only for the technical evaluator wins the evaluation and loses the budget meeting.

The champion matters most, and is the easiest to forget. Their job is to argue for you in a meeting you will not attend. Strong B2B branding gives them a short, consistent story to repeat, and proof they can attach to it.

B2B Branding Lives in the Sales Conversation

Most buyers meet your brand properly for the first time through a person: a rep, a demo, a proposal, a reference call. If what that person says does not match the website, the website loses. So the brand has to be built into the material sales actually uses, or it does not exist where the decision is made.

That means battlecards, objection handling and proof assets built from real deals, not from a brand deck. A battlecard that tells a rep exactly what to say when the incumbent comes up is doing more brand work than a page of values ever will. So is a one-page case a champion can forward to finance as it is.

Test B2B branding on real reps before you lock it

Before a messaging framework is final, we run it past the people who will say it out loud. Reps find out quickly which lines buyers repeat back and which ones produce a blank look. A position that only works in a slide presentation will not survive its first discovery call, and it is far cheaper to find that out before the new site is built around it.

Then put sales and marketing on one definition of the customer. One funnel, agreed stage criteria, and one description of who you are for. Without that, every other piece of B2B branding work is being contradicted daily by the people closest to the buyer.

How to Tell B2B Branding Is Working

Brand does not show up in a fortnight, and anyone who promises a brand result in a month is selling activation with a different label. Creation compounds over two to four quarters. What you can do is agree the evidence in advance, so nobody has to argue about it later.

  • Branded search volume, read over a quarter, not a week.
  • The ratio of branded to unbranded search. If unbranded is flat while spend climbs, more spend is a tax rather than an investment.
  • Direct arrivals that no campaign claims.
  • The share of enquiries that already knew the name before they typed it. Ask every new lead how they heard of you, and write the answer down.
  • Win rate against the incumbent, and whether the deals you lose are the ones you expected to lose.

The last one is the most honest test of positioning. If the brand is clear, you start losing the deals that were never a fit, quickly and cheaply, and winning more of the ones that were. That shift shows up in the CRM before it shows up anywhere else.

Report these softer measures beside pipeline, not instead of it, and label which numbers are leading and which are lagging. A brand that cannot be connected to revenue eventually loses its budget, however good it looks.

Decide It, Then Repeat It

The visible parts of a brand are the last step, not the first. Decide who you are for and why you win, write it down, fund the work aimed at buyers who are not looking yet, put it in the hands of the people selling, and agree how you will know it is working before you spend a dollar on it.

  • Settle the position before the logo: segment, outcome, alternative, proof and price.
  • Most of your future buyers are out of the market today. Brand is the work aimed at them.
  • Use 46:54 as a benchmark, and give the brand half its own targets and timescale.
  • Express one position for every reader on the buying committee, and arm the champion.
  • Build it into battlecards and proof, test it on real reps, and measure branded search, direct arrivals and win rate.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask before they spend on a brand.

What is B2B branding?

It is the work of deciding what a business is the obvious choice for, and making sure buyers believe it before they meet you. The visual identity is part of it, but the core is positioning: who you serve, what you replace, what proves it, and how you price. In a business market the brand also has to make sense to a whole buying committee, not one person.

How is B2B branding different from B2C branding?

The purchase is made by a group over a long cycle, so the brand has to answer different questions for an economic buyer, a champion, a technical evaluator and a blocker. Much of it is delivered by people, through sales conversations and proposals, rather than by advertising alone. Emotion still matters: Binet and Field's research concluded that B2B brands need emotional appeal too.

How much should a B2B company spend on brand?

Les Binet and Peter Field's research for the B2B Institute found B2B businesses grow best with roughly 46% of budget on brand building and 54% on activation. Treat it as a benchmark. The more important step is giving the brand half its own targets and timescale, so it is not judged on this month's leads.

How long does it take for a brand to show results?

Expect two to four quarters. It shows first as branded search volume, direct traffic no campaign claims, and more enquiries from people who already knew the name, and later as pipeline and win rate. Agree those measures before you start, so the work is not cut before it has had time to compound.

Do we need a rebrand?

Usually not first. If sales and marketing describe the customer differently, or you keep winning the wrong deals, the problem is positioning, and a new identity on an unchanged position fixes nothing. Rebrand when the position itself changes, such as a new segment, a new offer or a launch under your own name, and settle the position before the design work starts.
