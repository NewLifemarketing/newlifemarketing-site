LinkedIn Ads Pricing

The Click Is the Wrong Thing to Price

B2B/LinkedIn Ads/Paid Media

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 20, 2026

LinkedIn ads pricing has no rate card. Every impression is sold in an auction, the price moves with the audience you pick and how people react to your ad, and the number most buyers fixate on, the cost per click, is the least useful figure in the account.

That does not make the cost unknowable. LinkedIn publishes how the auction works, what you can be charged for, the minimum it will let you spend and how far a day's spend can run past your budget. All of it is below, checked against LinkedIn's own help centre on November 20, 2026. Then the part LinkedIn cannot tell you: how to work out whether the price is worth paying for your business, and how to size a budget that buys something.

How LinkedIn Ads Pricing Is Set: An Auction, Not a Price List

LinkedIn's help article on advertising cost and pricing is blunt about it: the cost of your ads is set by an online auction. You are competing with every other advertiser who wants the same people in front of them at the same moment, and what you pay depends on four things LinkedIn names.

  • Your audience. The price to win depends on how desirable that audience is. An audience that many advertisers want costs more to win than one few of them are bidding on.
  • Your bidding strategy. It decides how much you pay per key result, and which charge types are available.
  • Your objective. It decides which optimization goals and bidding strategies you can pick in the first place.
  • Relevance. LinkedIn scores ads on click-through rate, comments, likes and shares, and says the more relevant your ad, the lower the price you pay.

That last one is the lever most budgets ignore. Two advertisers bidding on the same audience do not pay the same price. The one whose ad people actually engage with pays less for the same placement, so creative is a pricing decision as much as a brand one. Our LinkedIn ads specs reference covers the format limits that decide whether an ad gets read at all.

What You Are Actually Charged For

"Pay per click or pay per impression?" depends on the bidding strategy, not on a setting you choose separately. LinkedIn's bidding strategies article, updated this week, lists three strategies and how each one bills:

Bidding strategyHow you are chargedWhat you control
Maximum deliveryPer 1,000 impressions (CPM)The budget only. There is no bid or cost cap to set, and LinkedIn bids to spend the whole budget.
Cost capPer 1,000 impressionsA cost per key result LinkedIn uses as a benchmark when it sets bids. Available for brand awareness, website visits, engagement, video views and lead generation.
ManualPer click (CPC), per 1,000 impressions (CPM), per video view (CPV) or per send (CPS)The bid itself. Which charge applies depends on the objective, the format and the optimization goal.

Two details matter more than they look. First, the cheapest-looking option is maximum delivery, because it asks nothing of you, and it is also the one that bills on impressions whether anyone clicks or not. Second, a cost cap is a benchmark, not a guarantee. LinkedIn's own wording is that there is no set cost per result, because the inventory is auctioned rather than reserved.

Cost per send is the model behind message ads, which are billed on delivery to an inbox rather than on what the recipient does with it. If you set up a lead generation campaign, LinkedIn now defaults the goal to qualified leads and the strategy to maximum delivery, so check the bidding screen before you launch rather than assuming you are on a click price.

The Minimums, and How Far a Day Can Run Over

LinkedIn's own budget guidance sets the floors of LinkedIn ads pricing:

  • $10 a day is the minimum daily budget, for any ad format.
  • $100 is the minimum lifetime budget for a new campaign that has not run yet. Once it is live, the minimum becomes $10 multiplied by the number of days scheduled.
  • $0.01 is the lowest manual bid LinkedIn accepts per result. A bid that low simply means your ad wins very few auctions, and Campaign Manager warns you when a bid will not reach the audience.

Then there is the rule that surprises finance teams. On a continuous schedule with a daily budget, LinkedIn's budgets article says actual daily spend can be up to 100% higher than the daily budget on a given day, and it will not spend more than seven times the daily budget in a week. A $100 daily budget can spend $200 on a Tuesday and up to $700 across the week. Plan the month on the weekly ceiling, not on the daily number, and nobody gets a surprise invoice.

One naming note, because older guides use different words. LinkedIn's help centre now calls the unit that holds the targeting and the bid an ad set, and the level above it the campaign. A campaign budget is optional and is shared across all of its ad sets.

What a LinkedIn Click Actually Costs

Because the price is auctioned, any single number is a range rather than a quote. On our LinkedIn Ads page we put clicks at a common range of $8 to $15, with CPMs that make Google look cheap. Your own price will sit wherever your audience, your relevance and your bidding put it, which is why a pricing estimate you have not tested is a guess.

The reason for the premium is what you are buying. On Google you buy intent, someone searching for the thing you sell. On LinkedIn you buy access: the ability to put an ad in front of a named job title at a named company, again and again. Nobody is on LinkedIn to buy your service today, and no other platform reliably sells that precision. You pay for it per impression and per click, and it only makes sense if a customer is worth enough to justify it.

Put the minimum and the range together and the arithmetic is sobering. At $10 a day and a $10 click, LinkedIn ads pricing buys you roughly one click a day. That is a working campaign by the platform's rules and an invisible one by any commercial measure.

Cost Per Click Is the Wrong Unit to Judge LinkedIn Ads Pricing

Most teams compare LinkedIn to their other channels on cost per click or cost per lead, LinkedIn loses, and the budget moves. That comparison is measuring the wrong thing.

The example on our service page makes the point in one line: a $90 lead that closes at 40% beats a $12 lead that never had budget. Work it through. Two and a half of the $90 leads produce a customer, so the lead cost behind each closed deal is $225. The $12 leads never close, so no number of them produces a customer at all. The cheap lead is the expensive one.

So the price we report on is cost per qualified lead: a lead your sales team accepted, not one the form produced. Beside it go the named accounts that engaged and how far they progressed, the pipeline LinkedIn touched, and how many roles inside each target account the campaign reached. What we refuse to report as a result is raw cost per lead with no quality filter, and impressions or engagement on a campaign meant to produce pipeline.

This needs one thing most accounts do not have: closed-won data flowing back from the CRM into LinkedIn through offline conversion import. Without it, LinkedIn optimizes toward form fills, the cheapest people to convert, and reports a falling cost per lead while the pipeline does nothing.

Sizing a Budget: Start From the Audience, Not the Number

Most budgets are set top-down: here is what marketing can spare for LinkedIn this quarter. The better question runs the other way. Audience size and budget are one decision, and getting them out of step is the quietest way to waste LinkedIn spend.

Audience sizeWhat happens
Under about 50,000Frequency problems. The same small group sees the ad until it stops working.
About 50,000 to 300,000The working range for most B2B programmes: enough scale to deliver, tight enough to stay precise.
Over about 300,000The precision that justifies the CPM starts to dilute, and you pay premium rates for reach you could buy cheaper elsewhere.

That is the audience size rule we work to on our LinkedIn engagements, and the budget follows from it. A target has to see the ad several times across the campaign, because recognition is the whole point and once is not recognition. A small monthly budget spread across a 300,000-person audience means effectively nobody sees you often enough to remember you.

How to Turn LinkedIn Ads Pricing Into a Budget

  1. Build the audience first. A named account list, with job function and seniority layered on top, sized into the working range.
  2. Decide the frequency. How many times each person in it should see you over the campaign.
  3. Price it against your own CPM. Audience times frequency is the impressions you need, and the auction tells you what they cost once you launch.
  4. Check it against deal value. If one closed account is worth five figures, a $12 click on exactly the right person is cheap. If it is not, see the last section.
  5. If the money does not cover it, narrow the audience. Do not accept invisible delivery to keep the list big.

LinkedIn does not work on a token budget. If yours cannot support frequency against a viable audience, we would rather tell you on the call and recommend another channel than take it.

Which Bidding Strategy, When

The bidding choice is where most accounts overpay, usually by changing it too early.

  • Start on maximum delivery while the campaign gathers data. It spends the budget and shows you what your audience actually costs.
  • Move to manual or cost cap once there is enough signal to control cost deliberately. Switching before that is how accounts end up paying premium CPCs with nothing to show.
  • Give each audience its own budget while testing, rather than one shared pot, so one audience cannot quietly absorb the whole envelope. In LinkedIn's current naming that means budgeting at the ad set rather than leaning on the shared campaign budget.

Thought Leader Ads: the Objective Changes the Price

Thought Leader Ads sponsor a real person's organic post rather than a company page post, and only two objectives are available for them: brand awareness and engagement. Practitioner analysis across large Thought Leader Ad datasets consistently finds engagement delivering a substantially lower cost per click to the landing page, on the same creative and the same audience. We default to engagement, and we explain why rather than leaving it as a setting.

LinkedIn Ads Pricing Across Three Campaign Stages

A LinkedIn budget is not one price. It is three campaigns doing three jobs, and each one is buying something different:

StageWhat it is forWhat the spend is buying
ProspectReach the account list cold, with Thought Leader Ads and value-led content. No ask.Recognition inside target accounts, and the retargeting pool the next two stages need.
NurtureEducate and build credibility with more Thought Leader Ads, documents and case content.Engagement from named accounts, and the first list of which companies are paying attention.
ConvertRetarget the engaged only, and ask.Qualified leads from people who already recognise the name.

In our 90-day plan, the convert stage deliberately does not open until month three, because there is nothing warm to convert before then. That has a direct consequence for how you judge the price. In month one, LinkedIn ads pricing is buying recognition, not leads, and a cost-per-lead verdict at week two will always say the channel does not work.

Keeping the stages as separate campaigns matters for cost too. Mix cold, retargeting and account-list traffic in one campaign and LinkedIn optimizes toward whoever is cheapest to reach, which is reliably not your best-fit accounts.

When the Price Is Not Worth Paying

Some businesses should look at LinkedIn ads pricing and walk away. These are the cases where we say so on our own service page:

  • You sell to consumers. The CPM cannot be justified, and Meta or Google will do it better and cheaper.
  • Your average deal is small. The maths does not work at LinkedIn's rates, and no amount of optimization fixes the arithmetic.
  • You need leads this month. If you need leads inside 30 days, Google Ads is the honest answer.
  • The budget cannot support frequency against a viable audience.
  • There is no CRM. Nothing can be measured past the form fill, so there is no way to tell whether any of it worked.

If none of those apply, and you can name the companies you want to win, the price stops being the question. The question becomes whether you are reaching the whole buying committee inside those accounts, which is what account-based marketing is built to answer. For how LinkedIn compares with a cheaper B2B channel, see Reddit vs LinkedIn.

The Bottom Line

LinkedIn ads pricing is an auction, so the honest answer to "how much does it cost" is "it depends on who you want to reach and how good your ads are". The useful answer is that the click price is the wrong thing to price. Size the budget from the audience and the frequency it needs, give it the months a B2B cycle takes, and judge it on what a qualified lead costs.

  • Cost is set by audience desirability, bidding strategy, objective and ad relevance.
  • Maximum delivery and cost cap bill per impression; only manual bidding can bill per click, view or send.
  • The minimum is $10 a day, and a day can spend up to double that, capped at seven days' budget a week.
  • Clicks commonly run $8 to $15, so $10 a day buys roughly one click.
  • Report cost per qualified lead, not cost per click or cost per lead.
  • Size the audience into the 50,000 to 300,000 range, then fund the frequency it needs.

FAQ

What B2B marketing and revenue leaders ask before they commit a budget to LinkedIn.

How much do LinkedIn ads cost per click?

There is no fixed price, because LinkedIn sells ad space in an auction. On our LinkedIn Ads service page we put clicks at a common range of $8 to $15. Your own cost depends on how desirable your audience is, your bidding strategy and objective, and how relevant LinkedIn rates your ad from its click-through rate, comments, likes and shares.

What is the minimum budget for LinkedIn ads?

LinkedIn's minimum daily budget is $10 for any ad format, and the minimum lifetime budget for a new campaign is $100. Those are the platform's floors, not a working budget: at a $10 click, $10 a day buys about one click. A budget that works is sized against an audience of roughly 50,000 to 300,000 and the frequency it needs.

Are LinkedIn ads charged per click or per impression?

It depends on the bidding strategy, which is the part of LinkedIn ads pricing you actually choose. Maximum delivery and cost cap both charge per 1,000 impressions. Manual bidding can charge per click, per 1,000 impressions, per video view or per send, depending on the objective, the ad format and the optimization goal.

Can LinkedIn spend more than my daily budget?

Yes. On a continuous schedule, LinkedIn says actual daily spend can be up to 100% higher than the daily budget on a given day, but it will not spend more than seven times the daily budget in a week. A $100 daily budget can spend $200 in one day and at most $700 in a week.

Why are LinkedIn ads more expensive than Google or Facebook?

Because you are buying something different. On Google you buy intent and on Meta you buy attention. On LinkedIn you buy access: putting an ad in front of a named job title at a named company, repeatedly. If a closed account is worth five figures, that precision is cheap. If your deals are small or you sell to consumers, it is not.
