B2B Content Marketing

Content That Moves Deals Forward

B2B/Demand Generation

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 25, 2026

Most B2B content marketing is written for one person filling in a form, while the purchase is decided by a group of people who never will. The blog post gets a download, the download becomes a lead, the lead goes to sales, and the deal stalls three months later in a meeting with procurement and finance that none of the content was ever written for.

The fix is not more content. It is content aimed at the right people at the right moment: an opinion that earns attention before anyone is shopping, material that answers each person on the committee once they are, and production that makes enough of it without lowering the bar. Then measure it as pipeline, because that is what it is for.

Write for the Committee, Not the Form

A B2B purchase is a group decision, and the group is bigger than the people you meet. Edelman puts the average at 13 stakeholders per purchase, and finds that more than 40% of deals stall because the buying group cannot agree internally.

Most of those 13 never talk to your sales team. Edelman and LinkedIn's 2025 B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report calls them hidden buyers: people in procurement, legal, finance and HR who do not use the product but can stop the deal. They read, though. Edelman found that 91% of hidden buyers say strong thought leadership makes them more receptive to a company's sales and marketing outreach, and 79% say they are more likely to argue internally for a company that consistently produces it.

That changes what B2B content marketing is for. Its job is not to generate one more contact record. It is to make sure every person around the table has already met your thinking, and has something they can point to when the room starts to disagree.

Map B2B content marketing to the roles that decide

Start with the roles, not the topics. When we build the value proposition for an account-based programme, we answer four people separately: the economic buyer, the champion, the technical evaluator and the blocker, because they are asking different questions. The economic buyer wants to know what changes in the business. The champion needs something to forward. The evaluator wants proof it works in their environment. The blocker wants the risk named and handled.

A content plan built on those four questions looks very different from one built on a list of keywords or a calendar of "thought leadership Tuesdays". It has fewer pieces, and every one has a reader.

B2B Content Marketing Has Two Jobs

Content does two different things, and most programmes judge both by the standard of one.

The first is capture: reaching the buyers who are already looking. Comparison pages, search content, retargeting and review-site presence all belong here. It converts this month, and it is easy to measure, which is why budgets drift towards it.

The second is creation: reaching the much larger share of your market that is not shopping yet, so they arrive already understanding why the problem matters. That is education, point-of-view content, video and executive presence, aimed at the problem your product solves rather than at the product. It compounds over quarters, and in a given month it looks like it is doing nothing.

On our demand generation work we report the two separately for exactly that reason. Capture converts now and creation compounds; reported as one number, the faster half always wins the argument and the slower half gets cut first. If you have read our post on demand creation, this is the same argument applied to the content itself.

In practice, B2B content marketing needs a budget line for each, and a different expectation for each. Capture content should pay back inside the quarter. Creation content should show up first in branded search and the quality of inbound enquiries, and only later in pipeline.

Thought Leadership Needs an Opinion

Almost everybody already claims to be doing it. The Content Marketing Institute's B2B research for 2026, a survey of more than 1,000 B2B marketers, found that 96% say their organization creates thought leadership content, while in many companies fewer than 5% of employees contribute to it. CMI's own summary is blunt: many programmes are content teams trying to look smart on LinkedIn.

The buyers are asking for something harder. In the Edelman and LinkedIn research, 86% of hidden buyers said they want content that challenges their assumptions, and 71% said thought leadership demonstrates a company's potential better than traditional sales and marketing material, against 64% of target buyers who said the same.

So the test for a piece of thought leadership is simple: would anyone in your category disagree with it? If not, it is a summary. Real positions come from the work itself:

  • What you have stopped doing for clients, and why.
  • The metric your industry reports that you think misleads people.
  • The deal you lost and what it taught you about how buyers decide.
  • The common advice that is right for a large company and wrong for a growing one.

Write it the way you would say it. Edelman found 65% of hidden buyers prefer a conversational, less formal tone, and 57% want quick, high-level takeaways over a deep dive. A clear argument in 1,200 words beats a 30-page report that nobody outside marketing opens.

And put names on it. An organization cannot hold an opinion; a founder, a head of sales or a lead engineer can. Executive presence is one of the things we build on demand generation programmes because a buyer weighing a claim wants to know who is making it.

Build What the Deal Asks For

Once an account is evaluating, the content it needs is not top-of-funnel at all. It is the material a buying committee actually asks for in the middle of a decision, and most marketing teams never make it, so sales improvises it in email at the worst possible moment.

On our account-based marketing programmes, that set is specific: a comparison against the incumbent, the business case a champion can forward internally, security and procurement answers, and proof that survives scrutiny. Objection handling and one-pagers are written so a rep can send them without editing them first, which is the only test that matters.

Who is askingWhat they need to knowContent that answers it
Economic buyerWhat changes in the business, and what it costsA business case with the numbers in their terms
ChampionHow to win the argument insideA forwardable summary and a comparison against the incumbent
Technical evaluatorWhether it works in their environmentSpecifications, integration detail, a reference that matches their setup
BlockerWhat could go wrong, and who carries the riskSecurity, procurement and contract answers, written down before they ask

This is the least glamorous B2B content marketing there is, and often the most valuable. A single well-built business case can be forwarded a dozen times inside one account. A blog post rarely is.

For the accounts that matter most, go further. Our one-to-one tier is reserved for the accounts a board can name, and it gets individual account research, a custom value hypothesis and content written for that company. When a specific deal or renewal is live, we build the content and the executive air cover around that one account for as long as the deal is live.

One Shoot, a Month of Material

The usual objection to all this is volume. There are four roles, two jobs, a handful of tiers, and a team of two. The answer is to make content from a source rather than from a blank page.

A source is something real that can be cut many ways: a recorded conversation with a founder, a customer interview, a working session on a hard problem, a day filmed on site. We plan our shoots that way. The scripts and plan are agreed on paper before anyone picks up a camera, and one or two days on site becomes a full month of content, ads and design. One afternoon with an engineer can produce the technical evaluator's explainer, a short cut for LinkedIn, the quote that anchors the business case, and the raw material for a written argument.

It also keeps the material specific. Our own crew films and designs the assets, the account-relevant video, one-pagers and landing pages, with no stock and no subcontracting, which is why the creative can be specific enough to matter. Generic creative gets ignored, and in a committee sale it gets ignored by 13 people at once.

Where AI fits

Use it for the cutting, not the thinking. CMI's 2026 research found that nine in 10 B2B marketers already use AI to produce content, but fewer than four in 10 say it has actually improved performance. Faster drafts of a generic idea are still a generic idea. Transcripts, summaries, variants for different roles and the first cut of a long recording are where it saves real hours.

Put B2B Content Marketing in Front of Named Accounts

Publishing is not distribution. A post on your blog reaches the people who already know where your blog is, which in most B2B markets is your customers, your competitors and your staff.

If you sell to a finite list of companies, aim the content at that list. In practice most of that air cover runs on LinkedIn, because it is the one platform that can target a named company and the roles inside it. That lets the economic buyer at a target account see the business argument while the evaluator at the same account sees the technical one.

Personalization is where most programmes stop short. CMI found 89% of B2B marketers say their organization personalizes content, but nearly 60% do it in only one or two channels, and most stick with personalized email (85%). Personalizing by role and by account across paid social, your site and sales follow-up is where the gap is.

And give sales the content in the form they will use. A rep will not dig through a resource library mid-deal. A short list of pieces per stage and per role, with a line on when to send each, gets used. A content hub does not.

Measure B2B Content Marketing as Pipeline

Pageviews, downloads and social engagement tell you a piece was seen. They do not tell you whether anyone who could buy saw it, or whether it moved a deal.

Hold it to the same numbers as the rest of the programme. On our demand generation work those are pipeline created and influenced, cost per opportunity rather than cost per lead, the split between demand creation and capture, branded search volume over time, win rate by source and sales acceptance rate. On account-based programmes we add the engagement view: depth of engagement per account, buying-committee reach, and content consumed by role.

Creation content needs patience and a fair test. The capture half of a programme can produce inside weeks; the creation half compounds over two to four quarters and shows up first as branded search and inbound quality before it shows up as pipeline. For the part that never fills in a form, look at branded search, direct traffic to key pages, self-reported attribution on the form, sales acceptance and win rate by source. None is perfect alone; together they describe the trend honestly.

If you are not sure what your current content is doing, our B2B marketing audit post covers how to check it in order, before you commission anything new.

Fewer Pieces, Every One With a Reader

B2B content marketing works when it is built around the decision rather than the download. Write for everyone who has a say, give creation and capture their own budgets and their own expectations, say something your category would argue with, and make the material a deal actually asks for before sales has to improvise it. Then judge it on pipeline.

  • Plan content by role on the buying committee, not by topic or calendar slot.
  • Budget and report demand creation separately from demand capture.
  • Test every thought leadership piece by asking whether anyone would disagree with it.
  • Build the business case, the comparison and the procurement answers before a deal needs them.
  • Produce from a source, such as a shoot or a recorded conversation, and cut it many ways.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing leaders ask before they rebuild a content programme.

What is B2B content marketing?

It is content made to help businesses decide to buy from you: articles, video, research, comparisons, business cases and sales material aimed at the people inside a company who make a purchase together. Done well, it does two jobs: it builds understanding of the problem among buyers who are not shopping yet, and it answers each member of the buying committee once they are.

How is B2B content marketing different from B2C?

The decision is made by a group rather than one person, the sales cycle runs over months rather than minutes, and the content has to survive scrutiny from finance, procurement and technical reviewers as well as the person who found it. That means fewer pieces, each written for a specific role, and a much larger share of material built for the middle of a deal rather than the start of it.

What types of content work best for B2B buyers?

Point-of-view content that challenges an assumption works best before buyers are shopping; Edelman and LinkedIn found 86% of hidden buyers want content that does that. Once a deal is live, the most useful pieces are a comparison against the incumbent, a business case a champion can forward, and written security and procurement answers.

How long does B2B content marketing take to work?

Content that captures existing demand, such as comparison and search content, can produce inside weeks. Content that creates demand compounds over two to four quarters and usually shows up first as branded search and better inbound enquiries before it shows up as pipeline.

How do you measure B2B content marketing ROI?

Against pipeline rather than traffic: pipeline created and influenced, cost per opportunity, win rate by source and sales acceptance rate. For account-based programmes, add buying-committee reach and content consumed by role. For the demand that never fills in a form, track branded search, direct traffic to key pages and self-reported attribution together.
