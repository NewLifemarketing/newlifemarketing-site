How to Structure a Google Ads Account for a Local Service Business

Google Ads/Paid Media/Home Services & Trades

by Cruz Lee Posted on August 30, 2026

Most local service businesses don't have a Google Ads problem. They have a Google Ads account structure problem. The keywords are fine. The ad copy is fine. But everything sits in one bloated campaign, so Google can't tell which service is actually making money, and neither can the owner. A clean google ads account structure fixes that: it groups spend by service and intent so every dollar reports back a real cost per booked job, not just a click count.

Why Most Local Service Google Ads Accounts Are a Mess

Say you run a plumbing company and you set up Google Ads yourself two years ago. One campaign. One ad group. Forty keywords ranging from "emergency plumber" to "water heater installation" to "drain cleaning cost." That's normal — it's also why the account can't tell you anything useful.

When every service shares one ad group, Google averages your bids and your ad copy across all of them. The keyword "emergency plumber" (someone with water on their floor right now) gets treated the same as "how much does a water heater cost" (someone three weeks from calling anyone). Different intent, different value, same bucket. The account can't optimize toward what actually books jobs because it can't see the difference.

The Google Ads Account Structure That Actually Works for Local Services

Structure the account around what you'd tell a new office manager if they asked "what do we actually sell, and what's most urgent?" That's usually three layers:

Campaigns — by service line. Furnace repair, AC installation, and duct cleaning are three campaigns, not three keywords in one. This is what lets you set a higher budget and bid on the service that actually converts and pull back on the one that doesn't, instead of guessing at a blended average.

Ad groups — by tight keyword theme, inside each campaign. Under "Furnace Repair," separate ad groups for "furnace repair near me," "furnace not turning on," and "furnace repair cost" — each with its own ad copy that mirrors the exact phrase someone searched. Tighter theming means higher relevance scores, which means lower cost per click for the same position.

Keywords — mostly phrase and exact match, not broad. Broad match hands Google room to interpret "furnace repair" as "furnace filters" or "space heater reviews." For a local service business with a finite budget, that latitude burns money on searches that were never going to book a job. Pair this with a running negative keyword list, so a search like "furnace repair jobs hiring" never triggers your ad — broad match without one is exactly where budget quietly disappears into searches that were never going to book a job. There's a full walkthrough of building that list in how to build a negative keyword list for Google Ads.

Campaign Types Worth Running (and Ones to Skip Early)

Search campaigns should be the foundation for any local service business — they catch people actively looking for the exact thing you do, right now. That's the highest-intent traffic Google Ads sells.

Google's Local Services Ads (the "Google Guaranteed" listings above regular search results) are worth testing alongside Search if you're in an eligible trade — home services and trades, legal, and a handful of others. They bill per lead instead of per click, which shifts the risk profile in a way that's often friendlier for a business still learning what a lead is worth.

Performance Max and Display are where a lot of local service budgets quietly leak. They're built for broad reach and brand awareness, not "book me a job by Friday." Imagine a roofing company in Barrie that puts half its monthly budget into Performance Max hoping for volume — it'll usually get impressions and a handful of cheap, low-intent clicks, while the Search campaign that was actually producing booked jobs stays underfunded. Get Search profitable first. Layer in Performance Max later, with its own separate budget, once you have enough conversion data to judge it fairly.

How to Structure a Google Ads Account by Service Area

If you serve more than one city or region, resist the urge to build one giant campaign with every location bundled in. Once a campaign has enough conversion volume in each area — usually once you're seeing conversions weekly in a given city, not just monthly — split it by location instead. Each geo-specific campaign gets its own budget and its own bid adjustments, so a strong-performing city doesn't get starved because a weaker one is dragging down the shared average.

Until you hit that volume, one campaign with location targeting and bid adjustments by geography is the more practical starting structure — cleaner data beats premature complexity when the numbers are still thin.

Tracking and Optimization Once the Structure Is Live

None of this matters if conversion tracking isn't wired up before the campaigns start spending. At minimum, track: phone calls from ads (call tracking, not just website form fills), form submissions, and — if your CRM supports it — which of those actually turned into a booked job, not just a lead. A form fill and a signed contract are not the same conversion, and treating them as equal is how "structured well" accounts still end up optimizing for the wrong outcome.

Once tracking is live, review search terms weekly for the first month, not monthly. New negative keywords surface fast in week one, and letting bad matches run for four weeks before the first look is expensive relative to catching them in week one.

Google Ads Account Structure for Local Service Businesses, in Short

How to structure a Google Ads account for a local service business comes down to one habit: separate by service before anything else, then by keyword theme, then — only once there's enough data — by geography.

  • One campaign per service line, not one campaign for everything.
  • Tight ad groups by keyword theme, mostly phrase/exact match, backed by a running negative keyword list.
  • Search first, Performance Max later — once Search is profitable and there's enough data to judge it fairly.
  • Split by city only once a single campaign has real weekly conversion volume in each area.
  • Track calls and booked jobs, not just clicks and form fills — and review search terms weekly in month one.

Straight answers

FAQ

What local service business owners ask before they touch their Google Ads account.

How many campaigns should a local service business run in Google Ads?

Enough to separate your distinct services, and no more than that. A business with three core services (say, repair, installation, and maintenance) usually does best with three campaigns, not one combined campaign or ten hyper-narrow ones. Match campaign count to how differently each service needs to be bid and messaged.

Should I structure campaigns by service or by city?

Service first, in most cases. Start with one campaign per service line, using location targeting and bid adjustments for geography within each. Only split further into city-specific campaigns once a single service campaign has enough conversion volume in each city to optimize independently — otherwise you're just splitting a small data set into even smaller, less useful pieces.

How much should a local service business budget for Google Ads?

There's no universal number — it depends on your cost per booked job and your margin per job, not a rule of thumb like 10% of revenue. A more useful approach: figure out what one booked job is worth to you, work backward to what you can afford to pay per lead, and size the budget so you're getting a meaningful number of clicks per week per campaign.
