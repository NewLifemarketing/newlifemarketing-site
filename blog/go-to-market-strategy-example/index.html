Go To Market Strategy Example

The Useful Part of an Example Is the Order Its Decisions Were Made In

Go-To-Market/Strategy/B2B

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 27, 2026

Most go to market strategy example lists are the same handful of famous launches told backwards. They start from the outcome, a product everybody now knows, and work back to a story about why it was always going to win. What they leave out is the only part you could copy: what was decided, in what order, before anybody knew it would work.

A go-to-market strategy is five decisions and then a dated plan. Market, customer, offer, channel and motion. So the examples below are laid out as exactly that, the same five boxes filled in four different ways, so you can see what changes when the business changes.

Three of them are composites, built to show a pattern; their numbers are illustrative. The fourth is real: a market we mapped for a client, from our own published case study.

What a Go To Market Strategy Example Has to Show

If an example does not show these five decisions, it is a story, not a strategy. They are the five pillars our own go-to-market work is built on:

  • Market. How big the addressable market is, which segment comes first, who you are up against and where the gap is.
  • Customer. The ICP, the buying committee, the trigger events that make someone look, and the job they are hiring you to do.
  • Offer. Your position against the alternative, the proof behind it, the objections you will hear, and the price and packaging.
  • Channel. Which channels earn budget, which are noise, and what each one has to return.
  • Motion. Who does the selling, how they are paid, what service levels and handoffs look like.

Then a dated plan on top: owners, dates and the metric each phase is judged on. Without that, as our go-to-market strategy guide puts it, the strategy is a document, not a plan.

If you want the definition first, what is GTM covers it, and the go to market plan post covers how to cost one. This post is the worked part.

Example 1: Sales-Led Software for Mid-Market Manufacturers

A composite. The numbers are illustrative.

A company has built scheduling software for plant floors. The deal is five figures a year, a plant manager finds it, an operations director signs, IT can veto it, and finance wants the payback period before anyone books a demo.

DecisionWhat they settled
MarketMid-sized discrete manufacturers in one province first, roughly 400 named plants. Food processing left for later: different compliance, different buyer.
CustomerPlants running two or more shifts on spreadsheets. Trigger: a new plant manager, or a missed delivery that a customer escalated.
OfferPositioned against the spreadsheet, not against the big ERP vendors. Priced per plant, with a paid 60-day pilot rather than a free trial.
ChannelLinkedIn to the plant and operations roles at the named list, search for the few people already looking, and a short payback calculator for finance.
MotionTwo account executives, each owning half the list. Marketing hands over a plant when two people from it have engaged, not when one form is filled.

The decision that does the most work here is the one about who they are positioned against. Pitching against the ERP vendors means losing on features to a company with a thousand engineers. Pitching against the spreadsheet means winning on the fact that the spreadsheet broke last Tuesday.

And the motion is decided by the deal size. A five-figure annual contract with four people involved will not close from a checkout page. That fixes the cost of everything downstream before a single ad runs.

Example 2: A Product-Led Tool Bought Without a Salesperson

A composite. The numbers are illustrative.

Now a much smaller product: a reporting add-on for agencies, $49 a month, set up in ten minutes. Same five decisions. Almost every answer flips.

DecisionWhat they settled
MarketSmall agencies already using one specific ad platform. Thousands of them, none worth a sales call on their own.
CustomerThe person who builds the monthly client report and hates doing it. They buy, use and approve it themselves.
OfferA 14-day free trial, one plan, a public price. The product has to show its value inside the trial or it never will.
ChannelSearch on the problem, a listing in the platform's integration directory, and templates people can use without signing up.
MotionNobody sells. Onboarding emails and the product itself do the work. A person steps in only when an account adds a tenth seat.

The comparison with example 1 is the lesson. A product-led motion only works when one person can find, try, buy and use the thing alone. Put a committee in front of that $49 tool and the motion breaks. Put a free trial in front of the plant software and you have a lot of trials and no pilots.

Here the offer decision carries the weight. If the trial does not end in a moment where the report builds itself, there is no salesperson to rescue it.

Example 3: A Real Go To Market Strategy Example From a Regulated Market

This one is not a composite. It is from our case study on the platform that found a hidden 98-store chain, and every number below is published there.

The client is a licensed Ontario cannabis edibles brand. It sells into Ontario's licensed cannabis stores, from 100-store chains down to single shops, and its product reaches the shelf through the provincial wholesaler. So the sale is really won with the retail buyers who decide what to stock.

DecisionWhat the market forced
MarketFinite and public: all 1,903 authorized cannabis stores in Ontario, taken from the regulator's own register rather than a data vendor's list.
CustomerThe licensed company behind the stores, not the sign over the door. One company often runs several banners, so counting storefronts counts one buyer several times.
OfferShaped by the rules. Inducements are banned: no rebates, paid shelf placement, exclusivity or paid travel. Every play had to be built around education, training and samples.
ChannelResearch before contact. The goal was written as research first, no outreach, so the first call goes to the right person about the right gap.
MotionTiered by footprint: 11 key accounts with 20 or more stores get a one-to-one plan, 61 with four to 19 get a regional play, 162 with two or three get a broader one, and 391 single stores wait for phase two.

The customer decision is where this example earns its place. In the original spreadsheet of about 237 accounts, four store banners sat as four separate mid-sized accounts. Checking who actually owns them turned up the same numbered company in two of the banners' website footers and a loyalty program shared across all four. Merged, they were one 98-store company: the second-largest cannabis chain in Ontario.

That is the difference between pitching four mid-sized accounts and pitching one buyer who controls 98 shelves. No channel choice could have fixed a target list that had the buyer wrong.

What This Go To Market Strategy Example Got Right First

The order. Research came first, then warming, because an account is only worth warming once you know who the buyer is, what is missing from their shelf, and which rules shape the conversation. The result was one ranked list of 625 accounts covering every licensed store in the province, before any outreach was planned against it.

It also shows what a market you can list makes possible. When the market is a countable set of companies, the go-to-market points at named accounts, and that is where account-based marketing starts. Our account-based marketing guide picks up from there.

Example 4: A Component Maker Selling Through Distributors

A composite. The numbers are illustrative.

A manufacturer makes a specialised industrial valve. Contractors and plant engineers buy it, but almost never direct: they buy from the distributor they already have an account with. The manufacturer has two customers, and only one of them signs a purchase order.

DecisionWhat they settled
MarketThree regions where two distributors between them cover most of the counter sales. Regions without a stocking distributor wait.
CustomerTwo of them. The engineer who specifies the part, and the distributor branch that decides whether to stock it.
OfferTo the engineer: a spec sheet and a drawing file that drops straight into their design. To the distributor: margin, a stocking program and a promise not to sell around them.
ChannelSearch and technical content for the engineers. For the distributors, a field rep, counter training and co-funded promotion.
MotionRouted through somebody else. The manufacturer's own team sells to distributors and supports engineers. It never takes an order from an end customer.

The motion decision dominates this one. Selling through a partner means your marketing creates demand that someone else captures. If the distributor does not stock the part, every specification you won goes to a competitor's equivalent at the counter. So the plan has to win the shelf before it spends on the specifier.

Four Examples Side by Side

Lined up, the pattern is clear. The five questions never change. Which one carries the weight does.

Who buysA committeeOne personA named company's buyerA distributor, for an engineer
Market sizeHundreds, nameableThousands, anonymousA public registerA few partners
Decision that carries itPositioningOfferCustomerMotion

What breaks itTreating one contact as the buyerA committee in front of the purchaseCounting signs instead of ownersSelling around the partner

The row that matters most is the last one. Each motion has one mistake that sinks it, and it is almost never a channel mistake. It is a decision that was made by default because nobody made it on purpose.

How to Turn a Go To Market Strategy Example Into Your Own Plan

Do not copy the answers. Copy the order. A go-to-market plan is a sequence of decisions, and taken out of order the later ones get made by default: a launch date before a price, a channel before a segment.

Copy the Order, Not the Answers

  1. Segment. One segment to win first, chosen because you can reach it and because winning it makes the next one easier.
  2. Offer. What is being sold, described as the outcome somebody buys rather than the capability you built.
  3. Pricing. The number and the logic behind it. Price is a positioning decision before it is a revenue one.
  4. Motion. Sold by a person, bought without one, or routed through somebody else. This decides the cost of everything after it.
  5. Launch. The sequence and the date, with the dependencies named.
  6. Review. A date set in advance where the plan is kept, changed or stopped, and the evidence that would justify each.

When you move to a second segment, it starts again at step one, carrying what the first one proved.

Test the Riskiest Assumption Before You Build

A go-to-market strategy is cheapest to change on paper and most expensive to change once a team has been hired around it. So the order that works is evidence first, then the smallest test of the assumption most likely to be wrong, then the build.

Every example above has one. For the plant software, it is whether pilots convert. For the reporting tool, whether trials reach the moment of value. For the distributor play, whether anyone will stock it. Find yours before you fund the rest. On our engagements that is the first 30 days: customer and lost-deal interviews, a competitive teardown and a data review, before anything is built.

Why Famous-Launch Examples Mislead

The household-name examples are not wrong. They are just unusable, for three reasons.

  • Survivorship. You only read about the launches that worked. The ones that made the same choices and failed are not on the list, so the list cannot tell you which choices mattered.
  • The motion rarely matches yours. Most famous examples are consumer or self-serve products. If a committee of six signs your deals, a story about viral sign-ups describes a different business.
  • They skip the decisions. "They focused on one market first" is an outcome. Which market, rejected against which others, on what evidence, is the strategy, and it is the part that is never written down.

A useful go to market strategy example is one close enough to your own business that you could fill in the same five boxes. That usually means a smaller, less famous one, with its decisions shown.

The Bottom Line

Every go-to-market strategy answers the same five questions: market, customer, offer, channel and motion. What changes from business to business is which question carries the weight. For a sales-led product it is usually positioning; for a self-serve one, the offer; for a finite market, getting the buyer right; for a partner-led one, the motion. Copy the order the decisions were made in, test the riskiest one before you build around it, and set the review date before you launch.

  • An example is only useful if it shows the decisions, not just the outcome.
  • The five decisions stay the same; which one carries the plan changes.
  • The deal size and the buyer decide the motion, and the motion decides the cost.
  • In a finite market, getting the buyer right comes before any channel.
  • Copy the order, not the answers. Then test the riskiest assumption first.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask when they are working out how to take a product or a new segment to market.

What is a simple go-to-market strategy example?

A software company selling scheduling tools to mid-sized manufacturers picks about 400 named plants in one province, positions against the spreadsheet rather than the big ERP vendors, sells a paid pilot, uses LinkedIn and search to reach the plant and operations roles, and has two account executives split the list. Every part of it is a decision someone made on purpose.

What are the main types of go-to-market strategy?

They are usually grouped by motion: sales-led, where a person sells; product-led, where the buyer finds, tries and buys alone; partner-led, where a distributor or reseller sells for you; and account-based, where the go-to-market points at a named list of companies. Many businesses run more than one, by segment.

What should a go-to-market strategy include?

Five decisions and a dated plan. The market and the segment to win first; the customer, including the buying committee and trigger events; the offer, positioning and price; the channels and what each must return; and the sales motion. Then owners, dates and the metric each phase is judged on.

How is a go-to-market strategy different from a marketing plan?

A go-to-market strategy decides who you sell to, why they should choose you, how it is priced, which channels carry it and who does the selling. A marketing plan is the activity that follows once those decisions are made. A GTM strategy that only covers marketing is a marketing plan with a bigger name.

How long does it take to build a go-to-market strategy?

A focused engagement runs about 90 days: roughly 30 gathering evidence, 30 building and locking the strategy, and 30 launching against it with weekly readouts. The evidence stage decides whether everything after it is aimed at the right answer.
