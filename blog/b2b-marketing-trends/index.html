B2B Marketing Trends

Plan Around Buyers, Not Vendor Roadmaps

B2B/Strategy

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 3, 2026

Most lists of B2B marketing trends are vendor roadmaps with the logos taken off. Each trend turns out to be a product category, and the advice underneath it is to buy one.

The changes worth planning around are slower and less exciting. They are about how business buyers now buy, what the in-market slice of your market now costs to reach, and which numbers survive a conversation with sales. Each one below is judged on a single question: does it change a decision in next year's plan, or does it only change the slide?

B2B Marketing Trends Start With How Buyers Buy

The shift underneath almost every other one is that business buyers do most of their research without you. They read, compare and ask peers anonymously, and by the time a company identifies itself by filling in a form or booking a call, its shortlist is usually written.

That changes where the work has to happen. A programme that only starts once somebody raises their hand is competing for a decision that is mostly made. The work that counts happens earlier and without credit: being the name attached to the problem before anyone searches for a supplier, and being easy to find and easy to understand when they do.

It also changes what a website is for. Treat it less as a brochure and more as the place the evaluation happens unattended. The questions a buyer would have asked a rep in a first meeting now have to be answered on a page, plainly, because there may not be a first meeting until the shortlist is settled.

Buying Groups Got Bigger, So the Unit Became the Account

Gartner's survey of 632 B2B buyers, published in May 2025, found buying groups of five to 16 people drawn from as many as four functions, and 74% of buyer teams showing what it called unhealthy conflict during the decision. A serious purchase is a committee arguing with itself, and most of its members never fill anything in.

A lead is one person. A committee purchase needs a different unit, and that is why account-based thinking has moved from a specialist programme to a planning assumption. The question stops being "how many leads did we get" and becomes "how many of the people who decide at this company have we reached, and do they understand us the same way?"

In practice that means mapping the roles inside each account that matters: the economic buyer, the champion, the technical evaluator, finance, procurement and the blocker. It means writing the material a committee asks for in the middle of an evaluation, such as a comparison against the incumbent and a business case the champion can forward. And it means treating a deal with one engaged contact as fragile, because single-threaded deals die when the champion goes quiet, changes role or gets overruled.

This does not mean every business needs a full account-based marketing programme. It works where the market is a countable list of companies. It does mean that even a lead-based plan should report how many people it reaches inside the accounts that matter, not just how many forms arrive.

The In-Market Slice Keeps Getting More Expensive

Only a small share of any market is buying at a given moment. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, writing for LinkedIn's B2B Institute in 2021, estimated it at roughly 5% of business buyers in a quarter and about 20% over a year. He was clear that the figure is an approximation and moves with how often a category is bought, but the shape holds.

Every competitor in your category bids for that same slice through search and retargeting. So a business capturing in-market demand competently sees cost per lead fall for a while, then flatten. The usual response is to raise the budget, which bids harder for the same finite group, pushes cost per lead up and lowers quality. Somebody concludes the channel has stopped working. It has not; the pool has stopped growing.

The tell is the ratio between branded and unbranded search. If unbranded volume is flat while spend climbs, more spend is a tax rather than an investment. The only way past that ceiling is to build demand among the much larger group who have the problem and are not looking yet, which is what demand generation exists to do, and it is the half of the budget most often cut first.

First-Party Signal Is Beating Bought Intent

Intent data was sold as the answer to anonymous research: a feed telling you which accounts are looking. It is useful, and it is also wrong about many accounts at a rate almost nobody measures. Third-party intent infers, step by step, that somebody at a company read something somewhere, and each step is probabilistic.

The behaviour you can observe directly is more accurate and costs nothing. A named contact returning three times in a week, a pricing page read twice, a document downloaded and then forwarded inside the same company. Most businesses capture none of it, because nobody connected the website to the CRM. The enquiry itself carries more again: what somebody searched before arriving, which page they landed on and what they asked about first.

So the order of trust runs first-party signal, then enquiry context, then bought data, and the budget should follow the same order. Before renewing a feed, run one test: take last quarter's flagged accounts and count how many became a conversation, then compare that against a random sample from the same list that was never flagged. If the two numbers are close, the feed is being paid for and is not doing anything.

Then decide who acts and how fast. A signal has a half-life measured in days. One that reaches a person through a weekly meeting has been bought and thrown away.

AI Made Content Cheap, and Agreement Worthless

Writing tools mean anyone can now publish a competent article on any topic in an afternoon. The predictable result is more content saying the same agreeable things, and readers who have learned to skim past all of it.

That does not make content less important. It makes volume worthless as a measure of it. A programme can publish for two years, produce a great deal of material and create no demand at all if nobody saw it or if everything it said was agreeable. The test was always whether more of the right people know what you do and have a view about it. Cheap production only made the test harder to pass by output alone.

What still works is the material a machine cannot produce from the public web: a point of view somebody will put their name to, numbers from your own work, a teardown of how a real decision was made, a comparison honest enough to lose a reader you were never going to win. If you want the detail on how this plays out at the top of the funnel, our post on how AI is changing top-of-funnel demand generation covers it.

Reporting Is Moving From Leads to Opportunities

Marketing-qualified leads are still the number most reports open with, and the one sales trusts least. Impressions, clicks and MQLs can all trend up while sales says the quality has not changed, which usually means the programme is measuring activity with no relationship to who actually buys. Of all the B2B marketing trends here, this is the one sales notices first.

The reports that hold up in front of a board read numbers closer to revenue:

  • Cost per opportunity, not cost per lead. A cheap lead nobody can sell to is the most expensive line in the report.
  • Sales acceptance rate: the share of what marketing passed over that sales agreed was real.
  • Win rate by source, so the channels that produce deals are told apart from the ones that produce forms.
  • Pipeline created and influenced, with demand creation and demand capture reported separately, because one converts this month and the other compounds over quarters.
  • Branded search volume over time, the slowest number here and the most honest one for the creation half.

The honesty rules matter as much as the list. Label platform-reported values as platform-reported, state the attribution window on every view, flag early data as early, and never add numbers from different ad platforms together and call the total revenue. Each platform claims credit for the same sale, so the sum always flatters.

Flat Budgets Make Every Trend Compete for the Same Dollar

None of these B2B marketing trends arrives with new money. Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey, published in May, put marketing budgets at 7.8% of company revenue, up from 7.7% the year before, with 56% of CMOs saying they lack the budget to carry out their strategy. The survey leans towards large companies, but the pattern is familiar at every size: the plan grows and the budget does not.

So a trend is never free. Adopting one means funding it out of something that already exists, and the honest version of next year's plan names what gets cut. A team that adds an intent feed, an ABM pilot and an AI content programme without stopping anything has not adopted three trends. It has spread a flat budget thinner across more things, and made each one too small to show a result.

How to Decide Which B2B Marketing Trends Deserve Budget

A go-to-market plan comes down to five decisions: which market you play in, which customer inside it, what offer and position you take, which channels earn the budget, and what sales motion carries it. A trend deserves money only if it changes one of those five. If it changes none of them, it is a tool, and a tool should be bought when the plan calls for it, not because the category is busy.

Run each candidate through three questions before it goes in the plan:

  1. Which decision does it change? Name it. "Buying groups are bigger" changes the customer decision and the measurement. "There is a new platform" usually changes nothing.
  2. What does it replace? With a flat budget, every addition is a subtraction somewhere else. Write down where.
  3. What would make you stop? Decide in advance, in a sentence, the result at which it is no longer worth running. Agreed afterwards, the decision gets made by whoever argues hardest at renewal.

B2B Marketing Trends to Leave Off the Plan

Leave off anything that is a product looking for a problem. Intent platforms and ABM software are genuinely useful and completely incapable of deciding which accounts are worth winning, what to say to a CFO, or who follows up on Tuesday. If the tool would sit on top of a target account list nobody agreed and a funnel sales and marketing define differently, buying it will not fix either.

Leave off anything you cannot staff. A signal programme needs somebody available to act on it within days, and a demand creation programme needs a minimum term before it shows anything. If neither the people nor the patience exists, the honest choice is to do capture properly this year and come back to the rest.

And leave off anything judged on a timeline it cannot meet. Committee-led purchases take longer than a quarter, so a programme judged at ninety days gets cancelled just as the early evidence starts to move.

What These B2B Marketing Trends Add Up To

Read together, the B2B marketing trends worth acting on all point the same way. Buyers decide more on their own and in bigger groups, the in-market slice gets more expensive to compete for, and the numbers that hold up are the ones closest to revenue. The response is less about new tools and more about doing the fundamentals in the right order: be known before the search, reach the committee rather than one contact, read your own signals first, and report what sales would agree with.

  • Assume the shortlist is written before anyone contacts you, and put the evaluation on your own pages.
  • Count people reached inside the accounts that matter, not just forms.
  • Watch branded against unbranded search before raising capture spend.
  • Use first-party signal before buying intent data, and test any feed you renew.
  • Fund a trend only if it changes one of the five go-to-market decisions, and write down what it replaces.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing leaders ask when they are deciding which changes belong in next year’s plan.

What are the biggest B2B marketing trends for 2027?

The ones that change decisions rather than tools: buyers researching anonymously and arriving with a shortlist, larger buying groups that make the account rather than the lead the unit, rising costs to reach the small share of a market that is buying now, first-party signals outperforming bought intent data, and reporting that moves from leads to opportunities and pipeline.

Is account-based marketing still worth it?

Where the market is a countable list of companies and deals are signed off by a committee, yes. It suits businesses selling larger deals to named accounts. Where the market is everybody, a full programme is usually the wrong spend, though counting how many people you reach inside your best accounts is useful at any size.

Should we buy intent data?

Not before using the signals you already own: return visits from named contacts, pricing page views, forwarded downloads and what people say when they enquire. Intent data is directional and works as a way to prioritise, not as a reason to contact somebody. If you already pay for a feed, compare conversation rates for flagged and unflagged accounts before renewing.

How should B2B marketing be measured now?

On cost per opportunity, sales acceptance rate, win rate by source and pipeline created and influenced, with demand creation reported separately from demand capture. Platform-reported figures should be labelled as such, the attribution window stated, and numbers from different ad platforms never summed into one revenue figure.

How do we fit new trends into a flat budget?

By treating every addition as a subtraction. Fund a trend only if it changes a real go-to-market decision, name what it replaces, and write down in advance the result at which you would stop it. Spreading a flat budget across more initiatives usually leaves each one too small to show anything.
