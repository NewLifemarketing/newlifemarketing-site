Go To Market Plan

Most Launches Don’t Fail on Effort. They Fail on Decisions Nobody Wrote Down.

B2B/Go-To-Market

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 21, 2026

A go to market plan is the document a launch is actually run from: who you are selling to, why they should pick you over what they use now, which channels get the money, and who does what in the first 90 days. Most companies have something they call one. Far fewer have one that answers all four questions in writing, with a number and a name next to each answer.

The gap shows up a quarter later. The launch happened, the campaigns ran, the team worked hard, and the pipeline is thin. Nobody can say whether the segment was wrong, the message was wrong or the channel was wrong, because none of those was ever decided on purpose. This post is how to build the plan so that question has an answer before you spend the money.

Strategy, Plan and Marketing Plan Are Three Documents

Half the confusion around go-to-market comes from three different documents sharing one name. They are related, they are written in order, and treating them as one thing is how a company ends up with a campaign calendar and no idea who it is for.

The strategy is the set of choices. The plan turns those choices into something a team can execute on Monday morning: a budget per channel, a list of things to build, a date for each, and the metric each phase will be judged on. The marketing plan is one part of what follows, and it is only as good as the two documents above it.

On our own go-to-market strategy work, the line we hold is that a strategy without owners, dates and metrics is a document, not a plan. That sentence is the whole difference between the first two columns.

Start From Evidence, Not a Workshop

The usual first step is a workshop: leadership in a room, a whiteboard, a list of target customers and a positioning statement by lunch. It feels productive. It produces the company's opinion of itself, which is the one input the market does not care about.

Buyers make most of their decision without you. Gartner's research on the B2B buying journey found that buyers spend only about 17% of their purchase time meeting with potential suppliers, and that time is split across every supplier they are considering. The rest goes to researching on their own and arguing it out with their own buying group. A plan built on what you believe about them, rather than on what they told you, is aimed at a buyer who mostly isn't in the room.

So the plan starts with evidence, gathered before anything is decided:

  • Customer interviews. Why they bought, what they compared you to, what nearly stopped them, and who else had a say. Keep going until the answers start repeating; that is the sign you have found the real reasons.
  • Lost-deal interviews. More useful than wins, and harder to get. A lost deal tells you which objection you have no answer for and which competitor you are actually being measured against.
  • A competitive teardown. Not a feature grid. How each alternative positions itself, what it charges, which channels it shows up in, and where nobody is saying anything.
  • Your own data. Win rate by segment, deal size by segment, sales cycle length, and where opportunities stall. The segment that closes fastest at the best margin is often not the one leadership talks about most.

That is how our own 90-day track starts: the first 30 days are customer and lost-deal interviews, which we conduct ourselves, a competitive teardown, a data review and internal alignment sessions. Nothing is written into the plan until it has a source.

The Five Decisions a Go To Market Plan Has to Make

Every section of the plan is a decision somebody has to own. We work through five, in order, and nothing below a decision ships until the decision above it is settled. Reorder them and you get the classic failure: a channel plan built before anybody agreed who the customer is.

1. Market: where you play

The addressable market, how it segments, who else is competing for it, and where the white space is. The output is a short list of segments ranked by how winnable they are now, not how big they could be eventually. A segment you can name 200 companies in beats one described as "mid-market companies that care about efficiency."

2. Customer: who exactly buys

The ideal customer profile, the buying committee inside it, the trigger events that put an account in market, and the job they are hiring you to do. Write down who signs, who champions, who evaluates and who can block. They are asking different questions, and a plan that answers only the champion's will stall at the economic buyer.

If the answer is a countable list of named companies, the plan points at account-based marketing from here on, and the rest of the decisions get made per account tier.

3. Offer: why you, over what they do now

Positioning, proof, objection handling, and pricing and packaging. The competitor is usually not another vendor. It is the spreadsheet, the incumbent, or doing nothing. Your position has to say what changes for the buyer if they switch, in their language, and the proof has to be specific enough that a sceptical finance lead would accept it.

4. Channel: where the money goes

Which channels earn budget, which are noise, and what each one must return. This is the section that most needs numbers, so it gets its own section below. The short version: a channel goes in the plan with a cost and an expected return attached, or it does not go in.

5. Motion: how the sale actually happens

The sales model, territory and compensation implications, service levels and handoffs. A product-led motion, an inside-sales motion and a field-sales motion need different plans, different content and different budgets. If the motion changes the way reps are paid, the plan has to say so, or the reps will keep selling the old way.

The one-page go to market plan summary

Before any detail, the plan should fit on a single page: the segment in one sentence, the buyer in one sentence, the position in one sentence, the three funded channels with their budgets, the motion, and the three numbers the first 90 days will be judged on. If it cannot be written that short, one of the five decisions has not been made yet.

Work the Budget Back From the Number

Most channel budgets are set by last year's spend plus or minus a percentage. A real plan sets them the other way round: start with the revenue the launch has to produce, and work back through the funnel to what each stage must deliver and what that costs.

An illustrative example, with round numbers so the arithmetic is visible:

  1. The target: $1.2M in new annual contract value in the first year.
  2. Average deal: $40,000, so the plan needs 30 closed deals.
  3. Win rate on qualified opportunities: 25%, so it needs 120 opportunities.
  4. Meeting-to-opportunity rate: 40%, so it needs 300 first meetings.
  5. Channel mix: now each channel is assigned a share of those 300 meetings, with an expected cost per meeting, and the budget is the product.

Your numbers will differ, and the first time you do this most of them will be guesses. That is fine. A written guess can be checked against the first month of results and corrected. An unwritten one cannot, and it is how budget ends up with whichever channel had the best month.

One caution about working backwards: it only counts buyers who are in market now. The B2B Institute's 95:5 rule, from Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, puts the share of business buyers in market for a given category at any moment at roughly 5%. Dawes is clear the exact figure depends on how often the category is bought, but the point stands. A plan funded entirely on this quarter's meetings ignores the 95% who will be buying next year, so set aside a deliberate share for demand generation that creates future demand, and judge that share on different measures over a longer window.

The First 90 Days of a Go To Market Plan

A plan with no dates is a wish list. The first 90 days are where the plan stops being a document and becomes a schedule, and they break cleanly into three blocks of roughly 30 days. This is the track we run:

  1. Days 0 to 30, evidence. Customer and lost-deal interviews, the competitive teardown, the data review and internal alignment sessions. Output: the five decisions, drafted and sourced.
  2. Days 31 to 60, build. Positioning and messaging locked, the channel plan costed, content and enablement built, tracking wired. We test the messaging with real reps before it is locked, because a message sales will not say out loud is not a message.
  3. Days 61 to 90, launch and learn. Campaigns live, weekly readouts against the metrics agreed on day one, and the plan revised on evidence rather than on mood.

Every line in each block gets one owner, not a team. "Marketing" does not own the battlecards; a named person does, with a date. The weekly readout then has a short agenda: what was due, what shipped, what the numbers say, and which decision the numbers are now questioning.

Agree the day-90 metrics with finance before launch, not at the review. If part of the budget is creating demand rather than capturing it, say up front that it will show movement at 90 days, not full payback. A plan that gets judged on a standard nobody agreed to usually gets cancelled just before it starts working.

One Funnel, Agreed Before Launch

If sales and marketing describe the customer differently, there are two funnels, two messages and a permanent argument about lead quality. The plan is the one place both teams sign the same definitions, so it should carry them explicitly:

  • Stage criteria. What makes a lead, a meeting, an opportunity and a closed deal, written so that two people looking at the same record would classify it the same way.
  • Handoff rules. Who owns a contact at each stage, and exactly when ownership moves.
  • Service levels. How fast sales follows up on a handed-off meeting, and what happens to the ones they do not.
  • Shared reporting. One dashboard, built from the CRM, that both teams are measured on. Every opportunity traceable back to what created it.
  • Enablement sales will use. Battlecards, objection handling and proof assets built from real deals, not from the brand deck.

None of this is glamorous, and all of it decides whether the plan is measurable. A launch with clean stage definitions and a mediocre campaign teaches you something by week six. A launch with a brilliant campaign and no agreed definitions teaches you nothing, because every number is disputed.

Why Most Plans Become a Deck Nobody Opens

When a launch underperforms, the instinct is to push harder: more posts, more spend, more outbound. Effort is almost never the problem. The plan failed earlier, at one of a handful of predictable points:

  • "We launched and nothing happened." The segment, the position against the incumbent, or the channel was decided by opinion, so there is nothing to go back and correct.
  • "A competitor repositioned and now we sound like them." The differentiation was never written down, so it eroded quietly.
  • "We cannot say which channel deserves the next dollar." No costed channel plan, no tracked funnel, so the loudest channel wins.
  • "We win deals we shouldn't and lose ones we should." Almost always positioning: the offer is being understood as something next to what it is.
  • "The plan is a deck nobody opens." Decisions were made, but nobody got a name, a date or a number next to them.

The fix for the last one is structural. The plan should be a living working document reviewed every week of the launch window, with a change log. When the evidence says a decision was wrong, you change the decision deliberately and write down why, instead of drifting campaign by campaign until the plan no longer describes what the company is doing.

A Go To Market Plan Is Written, Costed and Dated

A go to market plan earns its keep by making the launch checkable. Five decisions settled from evidence, a channel budget worked back from the revenue it has to produce, 90 days of owners and dates, and one funnel both teams signed. When the first results come in, you know which decision they are questioning, and you can change that one thing instead of starting over.

  • The strategy is the decisions; the plan is those decisions costed, dated and owned; the marketing plan comes after both.
  • Start with customer and lost-deal interviews, not a leadership workshop. Buyers spend most of their decision away from you.
  • Settle market, customer, offer, channel and motion in that order. Nothing below a decision ships until it is made.
  • Work the channel budget back from the revenue target, and fund future demand separately on a longer window.
  • Give every line in the first 90 days one owner and a date, and agree the day-90 metrics with finance before launch.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask before they commit a launch to paper.

What is a go to market plan?

It is the operating document for taking an offer to a market. It records the decisions behind the launch (which segment, which buyer, what position, which channels, what sales motion) and turns them into a costed, dated schedule with an owner for every line and the metrics each phase will be judged on.

What is the difference between a go-to-market strategy and a go to market plan?

The strategy is the set of choices about where and how you will win: segment, customer, offer, channel and motion. The plan is those choices made executable, with a budget per channel, a list of what gets built, dates, owners and success metrics. A strategy without owners, dates and metrics is a document rather than a plan.

What should a go to market plan include?

At minimum: the target segment and ideal customer profile, the buying committee, positioning and proof, pricing and packaging, a channel plan with budget and expected return per channel, the sales motion and handoff rules, shared funnel definitions, and a 90-day roadmap with owners, dates and success metrics. It should also fit on one summary page.

How long does a go-to-market plan take to build?

A focused build takes about 90 days if it includes launching: roughly 30 days gathering evidence through customer and lost-deal interviews, a competitive teardown and a data review, 30 days locking positioning and costing the channels, and 30 days running campaigns with weekly readouts. The document itself is usually settled by day 60.

Who should own the go-to-market plan?

One named person, usually a senior marketing or revenue leader, with sales, marketing, product and customer success all signing it. Each line inside it then has its own single owner. Shared ownership of the whole thing is how a plan becomes a deck nobody opens.
