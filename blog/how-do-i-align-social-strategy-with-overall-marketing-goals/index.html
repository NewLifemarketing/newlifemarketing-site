How Do I Align Social Strategy With Overall Marketing Goals

Social Doesn't Get Goals of Its Own

B2B/Social Media/Strategy

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 21, 2026

How do I align social strategy with overall marketing goals? Start by taking away social's own goals. A social plan with targets of its own, such as followers, posts per week or an engagement rate, can hit every one of them and still not move the number the business is judged on.

Alignment is not a meeting where the social lead shows the CMO a content calendar. It is a chain. The revenue target sets the marketing objectives. Each objective hands social a specific job. Each job gets one metric that would move if social did it and stay flat if it did not. Break any link and you get the familiar result: a busy feed, and a pipeline report that never mentions it.

Below is how to build that chain for a B2B company, which jobs social can honestly carry and which it cannot, and the numbers we report and refuse to report on our own social work.

How Do I Align Social Strategy With Overall Marketing Goals? Start at Revenue

Most social strategies are written from the bottom up. Which platforms, how many posts a week, which content pillars, what tone. Each of those is a fair question, and none of them can be answered until you know what the business needs social to do. So work top down, four levels, in this order:

  1. The business goal. The number the leadership team is measured on: new revenue, accounts won, net retention, a new market entered.
  2. The marketing objective. What marketing has committed to in support of it: pipeline from a target account list, awareness in a new segment, expansion inside existing customers.
  3. Social's job. The specific part of that objective social is better placed to do than anything else in the plan. Not "support the objective". A job you could hand to a person.
  4. The metric. One measure per job that would change if social did it and stay flat if it did not.

Here is what that looks like when it is written down. The targets are illustrative, not a client's numbers; the shape is the point.

Business goalMarketing objectiveSocial's jobThe metric
Win 12 new accounts this year from a list of 150Every buying committee on the list has seen us before the first sales callBe the credible company they find when they check us outProfile visits, and engagement from people at listed accounts
Grow net revenue retentionExisting customers adopt the second productKeep customers seeing proof of what the second product doesSaves and shares on customer-proof posts, DMs from existing accounts
Enter a new segmentA new job title knows the category and our nameReach people who have never heard of usReach and views by format, inside that audience
Close a pipeline gap this quarterMore qualified first conversationsDrive registrations to a session the plan already runsRegistrations, attendance, calls booked

Every row starts with something the board reads and ends with a number social can actually move. That is the whole test. If the last column says "followers", the chain broke somewhere above it, and no amount of content will repair it.

The other thing the table does is make social's goals arguable. A CFO can disagree with "be the credible company the committee finds", and that disagreement is useful. Nobody can disagree with "grow engagement 20%", because nobody can say what it is for.

Give Social the Jobs It Is Actually Good At

On our social media marketing page we put it plainly: social is genuinely strong at three things and weak at three others.

Strong atWeak at
Being found. Your profile is a search result, and the credibility check before anyone calls.Direct response on its own, detached from paid media or a real offer.
Staying in front of people who already know you, so you are the name they remember when the need arrives.Predictable lead volume in week one.
Proof. Showing the work, the team and the results in a way a website structurally cannot.Replacing a sales process.

Almost every misaligned social plan we see was handed a goal from the right-hand column. "Forty MQLs a month from organic social" is a marketing objective pushed down onto a channel that cannot carry it. When it misses, the conclusion drawn is that social does not work for B2B. The real failure was the assignment.

So for each marketing objective, write two things: the part social carries, and what carries the rest.

Marketing objectiveSocial carriesThe rest is carried by
Pipeline from named accountsCredibility when the committee checks youAccount-based outreach, LinkedIn Ads and sales
Lead volume this quarterThe proof that makes the ads convertGoogle Ads or Meta Ads, with social running underneath
Awareness in a new segmentReach with people who do not know you yetPaid distribution into the same audience
Retention and expansionStaying in front of existing customersLifecycle email, customer success and sales

Social appears in every row. Aligned social is not a smaller role. It is a correctly sized one, and a correctly sized role is the only kind that survives a budget review.

The lead-volume row deserves its own warning, because it is where most plans go wrong. If the business needs leads this month and nothing else is running, our own advice is to put the money into Google or Meta first and run social underneath it. Social then does what it is good at, making the ad-driven visitor trust what they find, instead of being blamed for a target it was never built to hit.

In B2B, the Audience Is a Committee

Most B2B marketing goals are about accounts, and an account is a buying committee. Several people check you out, separately, at different times, and each sees something different: a founder's post, the company page, a video, a customer's comment. Social's job in an account-based plan is that every one of them finds the same credible company.

That changes what aligned looks like in practice. On LinkedIn, the posts come from the company page and from real people inside the business, because people follow people rather than logos. The target account list in the marketing plan becomes social's audience list too. Our account-based marketing guide covers how that list gets built, and our post on B2B social media marketing covers how to run social for a committee once you have it.

One Metric Per Job, and Never the Follower Count

Once each job is written down, the metric almost picks itself, and it is rarely the number on the default dashboard. Here is what we report on the social accounts we run, and what we refuse to present as a result.

What we reportWhat we refuse to report as a result
Reach and views by format, so you know what is actually being watchedFollower count as a headline metric
Saves, shares and comments, the signals the algorithms weight nowImpressions with no context or comparison
Profile visits, link clicks and DMs, the actions closest to revenueA spike from one viral post, presented as a trend
What we are changing next month, and whyOnly the metric that happened to go up this month

The refusals are about alignment, not taste. A follower count cannot be traced to any objective in the table above. It feels like progress and predicts the least about whether anyone buys. Profile visits and DMs can be traced, because they are the moment somebody from a target account stopped scrolling and did something.

Match the metric to the job, not to the platform:

  • A reach job (new segment, new market) is judged on reach and views inside the right audience. Reach outside it is noise, however large.
  • A consideration job (staying in front of people who know you) is judged on saves, shares and comments, because those are people choosing to keep or pass on what you said.
  • A credibility job (the committee checking you) is judged on profile visits, link clicks and DMs.
  • A pipeline job is judged on what the destination produces, which is the next section.

Then connect them to the system the rest of marketing reports from. A DM or a link click that becomes a contact in the CRM is where social's numbers meet the marketing plan's. Without that link, social reports in its own currency forever, and the plan never finds out what it bought. That connection is the work of marketing analytics and attribution, and it is worth doing before the next planning cycle rather than after it.

Give the Reach Somewhere the Plan Already Owns

Social on its own gets judged on reach, engagement and follower growth. All of them are real, and none of them is revenue. The gap between the two is usually not the content. It is that the reach has nowhere to go.

The destination we build for this, where the offer supports it, is a webinar. Every platform drives registrations. The session is free, teaches something real and has no gate. Then attendance does the qualifying: somebody who registers, turns up and stays to the end has shown more intent than any form fill can, so the sales conversation afterwards starts warm. The month ends with three numbers a revenue leader can act on: registrations, attendance and calls booked.

It does not have to be a webinar, and it is not a box every account gets. We scope it, because an hour of teaching only earns the ask at the end when the offer is substantial enough. What matters for alignment is the principle: social's destination should be something the marketing plan already owns. A session, a guide, an assessment, an event. When social feeds a destination the plan already measures, social's results show up in the plan's own numbers without anyone having to argue for them. Our demand generation guide covers how those offers fit into the wider plan.

Plan Organic and Paid as One, Because the Buyer Sees Both

A buyer does not know which of your posts were boosted. They see one company. A plan that sets organic goals in one meeting and paid goals in another produces two messages, two audiences and two calendars, and the buyer sees the seam.

On LinkedIn the link is direct. The organic presence is what makes the paid layer work, and a company page with nothing on it converts nobody. That is why our LinkedIn Ads and social work are usually bought together rather than one after the other. The same holds for production: the shoot day that feeds the social calendar also feeds the Meta and Google ad creative, so the cost is shared across organic and paid instead of being charged to one.

And search sits in the same plan. Captions, on-screen text and spoken words carry the same intent keywords that drive the SEO work. One keyword set working across search and social, instead of two teams guessing separately.

Aligned, in practice, means three shared documents: one audience list, one message hierarchy, one calendar. Organic and paid each take their jobs from those, and neither one gets to invent its own.

How Do I Align Social Strategy With Overall Marketing Goals? A Five-Step Check

Run this against the social plan you have now. Each step either passes or tells you exactly what to fix.

  1. Quote the plan. Write the business goal and the marketing objective at the top of the social plan, word for word. If the social team cannot quote them, they are not aligned to them.
  2. Assign one or two jobs. Take them from the strong column: being found, staying in front of people, proof. Next to each, name what carries the rest of the objective.
  3. Choose platforms from the jobs. For a B2B or professional services company that usually means LinkedIn and YouTube, with Instagram carrying culture and proof. Owning two properly beats being mediocre on six.
  4. Set one metric per job, connected to the CRM. Reach for reach, saves and shares for consideration, profile visits and DMs for credibility. Not followers.
  5. Give the reach a destination the plan already measures, and agree when the numbers will first be judged.

How Do I Align Social Strategy With Overall Marketing Goals When Results Take 90 Days?

By agreeing the timeline before the first post, not after the first report. In the social accounts we run, month one buys consistency, month two buys a library of content, and month three is the first month decisions are made from evidence rather than opinion. That is why we do not ask anyone to judge an account before then.

Put that timeline into the marketing plan itself. If the plan needs pipeline inside 30 days, social is the wrong lever for that month, and the plan should say so and fund Google Ads or Meta Ads for it, with social building underneath. If the plan runs on quarters, review social's jobs and metrics with it every quarter: reweight toward the formats and pillars that are carrying their job, and drop the ones that are not.

The Bottom Line

If the question on your desk is how do I align social strategy with overall marketing goals, the answer is to stop letting social have goals of its own. Start from the revenue number, hand social the jobs it is good at, give each job one metric that traces back to the plan, and give the reach a destination the plan already owns. Do that, and social stops reporting in its own currency.

  • Work top down: business goal, marketing objective, social's job, one metric.
  • Social is strong at being found, staying in front of people, and proof.
  • It is weak at direct response on its own, week-one lead volume, and replacing sales.
  • For B2B, the audience is the buying committee and the target account list.
  • Report profile visits, saves, shares and DMs; never lead with followers.
  • Plan organic and paid from one audience list, one message and one calendar.
  • Agree a 90-day timeline before the first post.

FAQ

What B2B marketing and revenue leaders ask when social has to answer to the plan.

What are examples of social media goals that support marketing goals?

Good examples start from a marketing objective. If the objective is pipeline from a target account list, social's goal is being the credible company the buying committee finds, measured on profile visits and engagement from those accounts. If it is awareness in a new segment, social's goal is reach inside that audience. If it is a pipeline gap this quarter, it is registrations to a session the plan already runs.

Should social media have lead generation goals?

Rarely on its own. Organic social is weak at direct response and at predictable lead volume in the first weeks. If the plan needs leads this month, put the budget into Google or Meta first and run social underneath it. Social earns a pipeline goal when it feeds a destination, such as a webinar, where registrations, attendance and calls booked can be counted.

Which social media metrics actually matter to revenue?

Profile visits, link clicks and DMs sit closest to revenue, because each one is somebody taking an action. Saves, shares and comments show what people chose to keep or pass on. Follower count matters least: it feels like progress and predicts the least about whether anyone buys, which is why we do not report it as a headline result.

How long before social media shows results against marketing goals?

In the accounts we run, month one buys consistency, month two builds a library of content, and month three is the first month we steer from evidence. Agree that timeline in the marketing plan before the first post. If you need booked meetings inside 30 days, run Google Ads and let social build underneath.

Which social platforms should a B2B company focus on?

Fewer than you think. For B2B and professional services we usually start with LinkedIn and YouTube, with Instagram carrying culture and proof. Pick the platforms from the jobs social has been given, not the other way round. Owning two platforms properly beats being forgettable on six.
