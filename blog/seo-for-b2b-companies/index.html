Most of Your Buyers Will Read You Long Before They Tell You Their Name.

B2B/SEO

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 13, 2026

SEO for B2B companies gets sold as a traffic programme, and that is why so much of it disappoints. A business that sells five- and six-figure contracts does not need more visitors. It needs to be on the shortlist that a buying committee writes quietly, weeks before anyone fills in a form, and most of that shortlist is written in a search box.

That changes what you build, who you write it for and what you count. This post covers how B2B search actually behaves, why small numbers are a good sign, how to map searches to the people who sign off, where search sits next to demand generation and account-based marketing, and how to measure it in pipeline instead of positions.

SEO for B2B Companies Is a Shortlist Problem

A meaningful B2B deal is not one person clicking a form. It is a committee of six to ten people, most of whom never fill anything in, evaluating you over months against an incumbent and the option of doing nothing. Each of them researches on their own schedule, and almost all of that research is anonymous.

That is the uncomfortable fact underneath every B2B search strategy. By the time an account identifies itself, the shortlist is usually written. The operations lead has already read three explanations of the problem. Finance has already looked for what solutions like yours cost. Somebody has searched your name next to a competitor's. None of them told you, and none of them will show up in a lead report.

So the job of SEO for B2B companies is narrower and more valuable than "get more traffic". It is to be the useful answer at each of those private moments, so that when the committee sits down to compare vendors, you are already one of the names on the page.

Get that framing wrong and everything downstream goes wrong with it. A team chasing traffic writes for whoever searches most. A team chasing the shortlist writes for the handful of people who will actually sign, and is happy when only they show up.

Why the Small Numbers Are the Point

The first thing a B2B team notices when it looks at search data is how small everything is. A consumer brand chases phrases searched tens of thousands of times a month. The searches that matter to a company selling compliance software to mid-market manufacturers might each run in the dozens.

That is not a problem to solve. It is the market telling you its size. Traffic is not the goal; traffic from people who want to buy what you sell is. A thousand visitors who will never be customers is a worse outcome than forty who will, and in B2B forty is often most of the market looking that month.

The broad head terms are usually the wrong target anyway. Someone searching a broad industry term is frequently a student, a job seeker or a competitor. Someone searching for a specific problem in their own words, with the constraint that matters to them attached, is a buyer. Those specific searches are also the ones bigger competitors have not bothered to answer properly, which is where a smaller company wins.

The practical consequence: judge a keyword on who types it, not how many. A phrase with 30 searches a month that only a VP of operations would ever type can be worth more than one with 3,000 that nobody with budget uses.

Search for Every Seat on the Buying Committee

Most B2B content is written for one imagined reader, usually the champion who will run the product day to day. The rest of the committee goes looking too, and finds nothing written for them, so they find it on someone else's site.

Each seat asks a different question, in different words, at a different stage. Map them before you write anything.

What does SEO for B2B companies look like for each role?

Who is searchingWhat they typeThe page that answers them
The person with the problemThe symptom, in plain words: why something keeps breaking, how other teams handle itAn explainer that names the problem better than they could
The practitioner who will use itHow-to and "X vs Y" searches, integrations, setup questionsA comparison or a how-to written by someone who has done the work
FinanceCost, pricing models, what a return looks like, what it replacesAn honest page on what it costs and what drives the price
IT, security or legalCompliance, data handling, implementation riskA plain answer to the objection, before it is raised in a meeting
The executive sponsorThe category and the strategic question behind itA point of view: why this problem matters now, and what to do about it

Two things fall out of that table. First, much of what a B2B company needs ranks for searches that never mention its product, because the committee has not decided a product is the answer yet. Second, the finance and risk pages are usually missing entirely, which is why deals stall at a stage marketing never sees. We cover the content side of this in more depth in our post on B2B content marketing for the whole buying committee.

One Problem per Page, One Page per Problem

The most common structural failure on a B2B site is a single "Solutions" page listing six things the company does in a paragraph each. Google cannot rank a paragraph. It ranks pages. Six services with no individual pages is six searches the business is not competing for at all.

The fix is a keyword map, not a content calendar. Sort every search you find by what the person is trying to do: learn something, compare options, or buy now. Commercial searches go on service and solution pages. Informational searches go to content, because they reach the committee earlier, before anyone has decided who to call. Then give every page exactly one primary search to own, and write it down.

Writing it down is what prevents cannibalisation, where two of your own pages chase the same search and both rank worse than one would have. It is more common than it sounds. We have found it live on a client site: three pairs of pages with identical titles and identical meta descriptions, published weeks apart, quietly splitting each other's rankings. Nobody had decided to do that. Nobody had a map.

The same discipline holds for content. A blog that publishes whatever seemed interesting that week ends up with thirty posts aimed at searches nobody makes, or at searches so competitive a newer site has no chance. Every post should exist because the map says a buyer asks that question and no page answers it yet.

Search Captures Demand. It Does Not Create It.

Here is the limit nobody selling SEO for B2B companies likes to say out loud. Search reaches people who are already looking. In most B2B markets that is a small slice of the companies that will eventually buy, and everyone competes for the same slice. That is why cost per lead climbs every year in mature categories while the pipeline stays flat.

Search cannot fix that on its own, because it cannot make someone search. That is the job of demand generation: education, a point of view and executive presence aimed at the problem, so that next quarter more people have the problem in mind and know your name when they start looking.

The two work as a pair. Demand generation builds the searches; search catches them. The capture side should be built before anyone spends money creating demand, because demand arriving at nothing is demand handed to a competitor. And the best evidence that demand generation is working shows up in search data: branded search volume rising over a quarter, more direct arrivals, a larger share of enquiries that already knew the name before they typed it.

If you are running both, report them separately. Capture converts this month. Creation compounds over quarters. Reported as one number, the slow half always looks like the one to cut.

Your Search Data Is Intent Data You Already Own

B2B teams spend real money on third-party intent data, which infers that an account is researching your category from reading behaviour somewhere else. It is directional at best. Meanwhile, the most accurate buying signal most companies have is sitting in their own analytics, unconnected to anything.

Every enquiry carries three pieces of information that describe the problem better than any firmographic score: what the person typed before arriving, which page they landed on, and what they asked about first. All three are discarded by default. Connect the site to the CRM and they travel with the lead, so sales opens the first call knowing whether this person came in on a pricing question or a "how do I fix this" question.

The same goes for behaviour on the site. A named contact returning three times in a week, a pricing page read twice, a document downloaded and then forwarded inside the same company: these are first-party signals, they are accurate, and they are free. Organic search is what brings most of that behaviour to your site in the first place, which makes it the cheapest input to an intent and signal programme, and one that most programmes ignore.

For teams running account-based marketing, the link is direct. If organic visits can be matched to accounts, search tells you which named accounts on your list are researching right now, and which pages they read. That is a reason to prioritise an account this month that no bought feed will give you as reliably.

Measuring SEO for B2B Companies Against Pipeline

Most SEO reporting is built to make the work look busy. Posts published, keywords tracked, impressions delivered. The test for every number is simple: if it can go up while the business gets nothing, it is activity, not a result.

For a B2B company the results sit in the CRM, not in a rank tracker. The numbers worth reporting are:

  • Pipeline created and influenced by organic search, by opportunity, not by form fill.
  • Cost per opportunity, not cost per lead, because a cheap lead that never had budget is not cheap.
  • Win rate by source, so you can see whether search-sourced deals close better or worse than the rest.
  • Sales acceptance rate for organic leads, which tells you whether you are attracting the right committee members.
  • Branded search volume over time, the clearest read on whether the market knows your name.

Positions still matter as a leading indicator, with two conditions. Report from Search Console's true site-wide totals rather than a top-25 export, which quietly ignores everything else you rank for. And never celebrate a first-place ranking on a phrase nobody searches. A position with no search behind it is decoration.

Every number should carry the window it covers and the source it came from. That sounds administrative. It is the difference between a report you can defend when the board asks why this budget exists and one you cannot.

How Long SEO for B2B Companies Takes, Honestly

Not one month. The first weeks are spent fixing technical problems and publishing into an index that has not caught up yet, and the numbers can look worse before they look better. New pages typically take days to a few weeks to be indexed, and considerably longer to settle into a stable position.

B2B adds a second lag on top. Even once a page ranks and the right person reads it, the deal it influences closes on the committee's timetable, which runs to months. A programme judged at month three is being judged before the first search-influenced deal could reasonably have closed.

That is where most of them die. The payoff curve is genuinely slow, and almost everyone quits in the window just before it turns. If the business cannot commit to a term long enough to see a sales cycle through, it is better to know that before starting, and spend the money on capture that pays back faster.

Search is also not something you finish. Competitors keep publishing, Google keeps changing how it ranks, and buyers keep changing how they ask. A site that stops being worked on does not hold its position. It slowly gives it back.

The Bottom Line

SEO for B2B companies is not a traffic programme. It is how you get onto a shortlist that a committee writes without telling you. Build pages for every seat on that committee, give every page one job, pair search with demand generation so there is something to capture, and measure the whole thing in pipeline rather than positions.

  • Most B2B research is anonymous. The shortlist is written before anyone fills in a form.
  • Small search numbers are normal. Judge a keyword on who types it, not how many.
  • Write for the whole committee, including finance and risk, not just the champion.
  • One primary search per page, recorded in a map, or your own pages compete with each other.
  • Search captures demand; demand generation creates it. Report the two separately.
  • Count opportunities, win rate and branded search, not posts, impressions or rankings.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing and revenue leaders ask before putting budget into search.

Is SEO worth it for B2B companies?

Yes, when it is aimed at the shortlist rather than at traffic. Most of a B2B buying committee researches anonymously, often weeks before anyone contacts a vendor, and much of that research happens in search. Being the useful answer at those moments is how you end up on the list. It is a poor fit only when nobody in your market is searching for the problem yet, which is a job for demand generation first.

How is B2B SEO different from B2C SEO?

Smaller numbers, longer cycles and more people. B2B searches are usually far lower in volume and far more specific, the decision involves six to ten people rather than one, and a deal can take months to close after the first visit. So B2B SEO writes for several roles, values precise searches over popular ones, and measures success in opportunities and pipeline rather than sessions.

What keywords should a B2B company target?

The specific problems your buyers describe in their own words, the comparisons they run, and the cost and risk questions finance and IT ask. Sort each search by whether the person wants to learn, compare or buy, put the buying searches on service pages and the rest in content, and give every page one primary search. Avoid broad head terms: the people typing them are often students or competitors.

How long does B2B SEO take to work?

Expect the first month to show little or nothing, and possibly a dip while technical problems are fixed. New pages take days to weeks to be indexed and longer to settle. In B2B, the deals that search influences then close on the buyer's timetable, often months later, so judge the programme over a full sales cycle rather than a quarter.

How do you measure SEO for B2B companies?

In the CRM. Track pipeline created and influenced by organic search, cost per opportunity, win rate and sales acceptance for search-sourced leads, and branded search volume over time. Rankings are a useful leading indicator if they come from Search Console's full totals and the phrases have real searches behind them. Posts published and impressions are activity, not results.
