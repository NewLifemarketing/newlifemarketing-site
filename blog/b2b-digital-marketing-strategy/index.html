B2B Digital Marketing Strategy

Most Strategies Are a List of Channels. A Real One Says What You Won't Do.

B2B/Strategy

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 16, 2026

A B2B digital marketing strategy is not the list of channels you are active on. Most documents with that title are exactly that: LinkedIn, Google Ads, a blog, an email newsletter, maybe a webinar, each with its own budget line and its own monthly report. That is an inventory. It tells you what is running. It does not tell you why, in what order, or which one deserves the next dollar.

A strategy is the set of choices that sit above the channels. Who you are going to win, what you are going to argue, what has to exist before you spend, what each channel is for, and how you will know any of it is working. Get those right and the channel decisions mostly make themselves. Skip them and you end up funding whichever channel had the best month.

What a B2B Digital Marketing Strategy Actually Is

Three words get used as if they mean the same thing, and the confusion is expensive. A strategy is a small number of decisions about where you will compete and how you will win. A plan is the dated, costed activity that carries those decisions out. Tactics are the individual campaigns, posts and ads inside the plan.

Most teams start at the bottom. Someone asks what the LinkedIn budget should be, or whether the company needs a podcast, and the conversation is about tactics before anybody has agreed who the tactics are aimed at. The result looks busy and reads well in a monthly report. It also produces the familiar complaint: we are doing everything, and the pipeline has not moved.

A useful B2B digital marketing strategy fits on a page and answers five questions in order:

  1. Who you are trying to win, by name or by situation, and who you are deliberately ignoring.
  2. What you are arguing: the one claim that makes you the obvious choice over the incumbent and over doing nothing.
  3. What has to exist before money is spent: the pages, forms and follow-up that catch a buyer when the problem gets urgent.
  4. What each channel is for, and what it is not for.
  5. What you will count, and over what window, agreed before the first campaign goes live.

Order matters. Our go-to-market work runs on the same principle: taken out of order, a channel chosen before a segment, the later decisions are made by default. The digital part is almost incidental. Your buyers do nearly all of their early research online now, so the choices above are made online or not at all.

Choose Who You Will Win Before Anything Else

Every other decision depends on this one, and it is the one most often left vague. "Mid-sized manufacturers in Ontario" is a market. It is not a target. A target is specific enough that sales and marketing would describe the same customer if you asked them separately, and specific enough that you could list most of the companies in it.

That test matters more than it sounds. When sales and marketing describe the customer differently you get two funnels, two messages and a permanent argument about lead quality. No channel fixes that, because the problem is upstream of every channel.

Two questions sharpen it quickly. Is your market countable? If the total number of companies that could ever buy is in the hundreds, your strategy should be built around named accounts, and account-based marketing stops being a tactic and becomes the shape of the whole thing. If it is in the tens of thousands, you are building recognition in a category, and the strategy leans on demand generation and search.

And who actually signs? A meaningful B2B deal is decided by a group, not a person. The champion who finds you is rarely the one who approves the spend, and the one who approves it often never visits your site at all. A strategy that only speaks to the champion has quietly decided to lose the meeting where the champion pitches you internally.

Decide What You Are Arguing

Once you know who, you need one claim that gives them a reason to pick you over the alternative they already have. Not five claims. One, stated in the buyer's words rather than the product's, and repeated across every channel for long enough that people remember it.

This is where most programmes fail, and the failure is invisible from inside. Changing the message feels like progress. A new quarter brings a new campaign theme, a new tagline, a new angle for the ads. Each one is reasonable on its own. Together they mean nobody in the market has heard the same thing from you twice, so nothing compounds.

The symptom to watch for is winning deals you should not win and losing ones you should. That is almost always a positioning problem: the market understands your offer as something adjacent to what it is. More spend on the same muddled message just buys more of the wrong conversations.

A good test is whether a competitor could run your ads with their logo swapped in. If they could, you have described the category, not your position in it.

Build the Place Everything Lands First

The most common sequencing mistake in B2B is buying attention before there is anywhere useful to send it. A LinkedIn campaign points at a generic homepage. A paid search ad lands on a contact form with no explanation of price, process or fit. The campaign is judged a failure in week three, and the channel takes the blame for a problem the website caused.

Capture is built before the spending starts, because demand that arrives at nothing is demand handed to a competitor. In practice that means a few unglamorous things exist before a single ad runs:

  • A page for each problem you solve, written for the person who has it, not for a search engine.
  • An honest answer to the cost question. Finance will look for it whether you publish it or not.
  • Proof a sceptic can check: case studies with real numbers, named where the client allows it.
  • A form that asks only what you need, and a follow-up that happens in hours rather than days.
  • Tracking that ties a visit to a contact and a contact to an opportunity in the CRM.

None of this is exciting. All of it decides whether the channels you fund next produce pipeline or produce a cost-per-click chart.

Choosing Channels for a B2B Digital Marketing Strategy

Only now does the channel conversation start, and it starts with a different question. Not "which channels are best for B2B?" but "where does our buying group already spend attention, and what job do we need done there?" A channel earns its place by doing one job well. Asking a single channel to introduce you, educate the committee and close the deal is how you end up concluding that the channel does not work.

Which channel does which job in a B2B digital marketing strategy?

ChannelThe job it does wellThe job it does badly
Search, paid and organicCatching buyers who already know they have the problem and are lookingCreating demand. It can only harvest the share of the market already searching
LinkedInReaching named people at named accounts by role, seniority and companySame-week conversions. Nobody is on LinkedIn to buy today, and it is the most expensive click in B2B
Meta and videoInterrupting the scroll with something worth watching, then retargeting the people who watchedPrecise targeting of a buying committee by job title
Content and thought leadershipMaking a problem legible before the buyer is looking, so they arrive already understanding itAnything measured inside a month
Email and the CRMFollowing up with people who have already raised a hand, at the right momentReaching anyone who has not

Two patterns fall out of that table. Search is capture: it is the most measurable channel and also the one with a hard ceiling, because it only reaches the people already in the market. Everything you do to create demand shows up later as people searching for you by name. And LinkedIn is access, not intent. It is the only place you can put a message in front of a specific buying committee on purpose, which is worth paying for, but only if the structure is built to earn attention before it asks for a meeting. Our LinkedIn ads run as three stages for that reason: prospect, nurture, convert.

You do not need every channel. A B2B digital marketing strategy for a company selling into 300 named accounts might be LinkedIn, a handful of deep pages, and a sales team working signals. A company selling to thousands of small firms might be search, video and retargeting. The test of a channel is whether it does a job the strategy needs done, not whether competitors use it.

Make the Channels Hand Off to Each Other

Channels that each report their own numbers will each look mediocre, because each one is doing part of a job. The value is in the handoff.

A sensible sequence looks like this. Video or thought leadership reaches people who do not yet know they have the problem. The ones who watch or engage get retargeted with something more specific: the explainer, the comparison, the cost page. The ones who visit those pages twice become a signal for sales. When someone eventually searches, they search for you by name, and paid search catches them at the moment it becomes urgent. The CRM follows up automatically and a person follows up quickly.

Run properly, cold ads introduce you, retargeting closes the people who showed interest, and the CRM picks up from there. Run as separate line items, the cold campaign looks expensive, the retargeting looks cheap and gets the credit, and somebody cuts the cold campaign. Three months later the retargeting pool is empty and the cheap channel has stopped working too.

Write the handoffs into the strategy. For each channel: who it reaches, what it hands on, and to which channel or person.

Use the Signals You Already Own

Most of the useful information about who is in the market is already on your own site, and most businesses throw it away. A named contact coming back three times in a week. A pricing page read twice. A document downloaded and then forwarded inside the same company. Those are first-party signals: accurate, and free.

The enquiry itself carries more. What somebody typed into a search box before arriving, which page they landed on and what they asked first describe the problem better than any firmographic score, and nearly every form discards all three.

Bought intent data has a place, but it is directional. It is a way to decide which 50 of 500 accounts get attention this month, not a reason to contact somebody who has shown no interest in you specifically. The sensible order is your own site first, enquiry context second, purchased data third, and the budget should follow that order. It is usually arranged the other way round. If you want this to be a deliberate part of the strategy rather than a dashboard, our page on intent and signal marketing covers the four decisions to make before buying anything.

One rule matters more than the data: decide who acts on a signal and how fast before you collect it. A signal goes stale in days. If the route from a flagged account to a human conversation runs through a weekly meeting, you are paying for information you cannot use.

Measuring a B2B Digital Marketing Strategy

Measurement belongs in the strategy, not in the report that follows it, because what you agree to count decides which work survives a tight quarter. The half of B2B marketing that captures existing demand is easy to measure: a search, a click and an enquiry in the same week. The half that creates demand reaches people who are not in the market yet, which is why it was cheap to reach them, and it shows up months later. Report the two as one number and the slow half gets cut every time.

So split them, and give each its own evidence:

  • Pipeline created and influenced, from the CRM, not from the ad platforms.
  • Cost per opportunity, rather than cost per lead. Cheap leads that never had budget are the most expensive thing in the funnel.
  • Win rate and sales acceptance rate by source, so you can see which channels bring deals sales actually wants.
  • Branded search volume over time, read over a quarter. If more people are looking for you by name, the creation half is working.
  • The branded to unbranded ratio. If unbranded search is flat while spend climbs, extra capture budget is a tax rather than an investment.

State the attribution window on every view, label platform-reported numbers as platform-reported, and never add up conversions across platforms and call it revenue. Each platform claims the same deal. A B2B digital marketing strategy judged on summed platform numbers will always look like it is working, right up to the moment finance compares it with the bank account.

Agree the review date at the start as well. Decided afterwards, whether to keep, change or stop a channel gets settled by whoever argues hardest.

Signs Your Strategy Is Really a Tactics List

Most teams recognise their own situation in at least one of these:

  • You cannot say which channel deserves the next dollar, so budget follows whichever had the best month.
  • Each channel has its own report and none of them mentions pipeline.
  • The message changed three times this year and every change felt like progress.
  • Sales and marketing would give different answers to "who is our customer?"
  • A new channel gets added because a competitor is on it, not because the strategy has a job for it.
  • The strategy is a deck nobody has opened since the meeting it was presented in.

Any one of them means the choices above the channels were never made, or were made and then quietly abandoned. The fix is not another channel. It is going back up to the first question and answering it in writing. If you already have that and need the dated, costed version, our B2B marketing plan post starts from the pipeline number and works back.

The Bottom Line

The channels are the easy part. What makes a strategy is the set of choices above them: who you will win, what you will argue, what has to exist before you spend, what each channel is for and what you will count. Make those choices in that order, write them down, and give every channel one job and a handoff. Then judge the whole thing in pipeline, over a window you agreed before you started.

  • A strategy is choices. A plan is the dated activity. Tactics are the campaigns. Start at the top.
  • Pick a target specific enough that sales and marketing would describe the same customer.
  • One claim, in the buyer's words, repeated long enough to be remembered.
  • Build the pages, proof and follow-up before buying attention.
  • Give each channel one job and write down what it hands on.
  • Measure creation and capture separately, in pipeline, with the window stated.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing and revenue leaders ask when the channel list has stopped explaining the results.

What is a B2B digital marketing strategy?

It is the short set of decisions that sit above your channels: which companies and buyers you are trying to win, the one claim that makes you the obvious choice, what has to exist on your site before you spend, what job each channel does, and how you will measure it. A B2B digital marketing strategy is not a list of the channels you are active on. That is an inventory, and it cannot tell you where the next dollar should go.

What is the difference between a marketing strategy and a marketing plan?

The strategy is the choices: who, what you argue, which channels and why. The plan is the dated, costed activity that carries them out, with owners, budgets and review dates. A plan without a strategy is a calendar. A strategy without a plan is a document nobody acts on. You need both, and the strategy comes first.

Which digital channels work best for B2B?

It depends on the job. Search catches buyers who are already looking. LinkedIn reaches named people at named accounts but rarely converts in the same week. Video and Meta interrupt attention and feed retargeting. Content makes a problem understood before anyone searches for it. Email and the CRM follow up with people who have already raised a hand. Pick channels for the job your strategy needs done, not because a competitor uses them.

How do you measure a B2B digital marketing strategy?

In the CRM, not the ad platforms. Track pipeline created and influenced, cost per opportunity, win rate and sales acceptance rate by source, and branded search volume over a quarter. Measure the work that captures existing demand separately from the work that creates new demand, because one shows up this month and the other shows up over several quarters.

How long before a new strategy shows results?

Capture, mainly search and retargeting, can produce enquiries within weeks once the landing pages and tracking exist. Demand creation compounds over two to four quarters and shows up first as more people searching for you by name and better inbound quality, before it shows up as pipeline. Agree that timeline, and the review date, before anything launches.
