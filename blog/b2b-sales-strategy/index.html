B2B Sales Strategy

Most Deals Are Decided Before the First Call

B2B/Strategy

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 31, 2026

A B2B sales strategy is mostly a decision about who you will not sell to. Teams skip that part because it feels like turning revenue away, and then spend the year chasing deals that were never winnable, pitching the wrong person inside the right company, and arguing with marketing about whose leads they were.

The tactics that get called sales strategy, the cadence, the script, the CRM fields, all sit downstream of a handful of decisions most teams never write down. Make those decisions and the tactics mostly choose themselves. Skip them and no amount of activity fixes the number.

What a B2B Sales Strategy Has to Decide

Strip away the vocabulary and a B2B sales strategy answers five questions. Which companies are worth winning, and which are not. How those companies buy: through a rep, on their own, or through somebody else. Who inside each company has to be convinced. Where marketing's work ends and sales' work begins. And how anyone will know, a quarter from now, whether it is working.

It sits inside a larger set of decisions. A go-to-market strategy settles the segment, the position against the incumbent, the offer and the price, and then the sales motion. If those are missing, the sales strategy ends up making them by accident: whichever deals the loudest rep closed last quarter quietly become the target market. We wrote about the order those decisions go in when we covered the go-to-market plan. This post is about the sales end of it.

Each of the five gets written down, with an owner and a number. A strategy that lives in the head of the VP of sales leaves the company when they do.

Start With Who You Will Not Sell To

When the ideal customer is vague, reps spend their time on deals that were never winnable. They are not lazy. They are working whatever came in, and with no agreed definition of a good account, everything that came in looks like a good account until month five.

The fix is a list. Most B2B markets are a countable set of companies, and a sales strategy should start from those companies by name rather than from a description like "mid-sized manufacturers." Three hundred named accounts, ranked by what each is worth, is something a sales team can work. An audience description is something they can only interpret.

Counting properly is harder than it sounds. In an account research build for a brand selling into Ontario's licensed cannabis stores, the market looked like 1,903 stores. Grouped by the company that actually decides what goes on the shelf, it was 625 buyers. Four banners that sat in the spreadsheet as four mid-sized accounts turned out to be one 98-store chain. A sales team working the original list would have run four separate pursuits at one buyer, and told that buyer four different stories.

The Exclusions Belong in the B2B Sales Strategy Too

Write down who is out, and why. Too small to justify the sales cost. In a segment where you lose to the incumbent on price every time. Requiring a feature you do not have and are not building. A short exclusion list does more for win rate than a long target list, because it stops the slow, expensive pursuits that end in "we went another way" after six months.

If you are winning deals you should not and losing ones you should, that is almost always a positioning problem rather than a sales one: buyers are understanding the offer as something adjacent to what it is. No sales training fixes that.

Choose the Motion Before You Hire

Every B2B offer is sold in one of three ways. A person sells it. The buyer buys it without one. Or somebody else sells it for you, a partner, a distributor, a reseller. Most companies end up with a mix, but one of the three carries the revenue, and that choice decides the cost of everything downstream.

  • Sales-led makes sense when the deal is large enough to pay for a rep's time across a long cycle, and when the buyer needs a conversation to get comfortable.
  • Self-serve fits a smaller, simpler purchase where a rep would cost more than the deal earns. The sales team's job becomes helping the accounts that outgrow it.
  • Partner-led fits a market someone else already has access to. You trade margin for reach, and you lose some control over how you are described.

The expensive mistake is hiring before deciding. Three account executives hired for a product that buyers would rather purchase on their own will spend a year proving it, at full salary. Decide the motion, then build the team the motion needs.

Sell to the Committee, Not the Champion

A meaningful business purchase is signed off by six to ten people, and most of them never fill in a form. The champion who took the first meeting is one of them. The others include somebody in finance, somebody who will have to use the thing, and often somebody whose job is to say no.

Single-threaded deals die when the champion goes quiet, changes role or gets overruled. Everybody in sales knows this. Fewer sales strategies plan for it. In our view, getting to more of the committee is the single biggest lever on win rate in committee sales: a deal where six of eight committee members already understand you is a different deal to one where only the champion does.

So the strategy should name, for each tier of account, which roles have to be reached and how you will know when they have been. This is where sales and marketing stop being separate jobs. A rep can run a relationship with two or three people. Reaching the other five, the ones who will never take a call, is marketing's work, and it is the core of account-based marketing: tailored content for the top tier, plays built for clusters of similar accounts in the middle, programmatic reach for the long tail.

One Funnel Between Sales and Marketing

"Marketing's leads are junk." "Sales doesn't work them." If both lines are said in your building, the problem is not either team. It is that there are two definitions of a good account, so there are two funnels, two messages and a permanent argument about lead quality.

The fix is unglamorous and written down:

  • One funnel definition, with stage criteria both teams agreed to, so a "qualified" opportunity means the same thing in both reports.
  • Handoff rules: what marketing passes to sales, with what context attached, and what sales hands back when a deal is not ready.
  • Service levels for follow-up: how quickly sales responds to what marketing passes on, and what happens when it does not.
  • Shared reporting, so the weekly meeting looks at one set of numbers rather than two that disagree.

What Goes Back to Marketing

Not everything marketing creates is ready for a rep. Demand that fits a target account goes into the account programme. The rest goes to self-serve or nurture. Nothing is thrown at sales indiscriminately, because a rep who has been handed ten bad leads stops opening the eleventh. The mechanics of stages and exit criteria are in our post on building a sales pipeline; the strategy's job is to agree who owns each stage.

Work the Timing, Not Just the List

A good list still has a timing problem. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, writing for LinkedIn's B2B Institute, put the share of a business market buying in a given quarter at perhaps 5%, and was clear that it is a rough figure rather than a precise rule. Most of the accounts on your list are not buying this quarter. A B2B sales strategy that treats all of them the same burns rep time on the 95% and reaches the 5% late.

Most buying research is anonymous, and by the time an account identifies itself, the shortlist is usually written. The answer is to watch for signals, starting with the ones you already own. A named contact returning to your site three times in a week. A pricing page read twice. A document downloaded and then forwarded inside the same company. Those are accurate and free, and most businesses capture none of them because nobody connected the site to the CRM.

Two rules make signals worth anything. Decide who acts and how quickly before you collect them, because a signal has a half-life measured in days and one that reaches a rep after next week's meeting has been thrown away. And treat bought intent data as a way to decide which accounts get attention first, not as proof that somebody is ready to talk. There is more on both on our intent and signal marketing page.

Give Reps What Real Deals Needed

Sales enablement usually means a deck from the brand team and a battlecard written by someone who has never been on a call. Reps stop using both within a month, and they are right to.

Build it from the deals instead. Interview the customers who bought and, more usefully, the prospects who did not. The lost-deal interviews tell you which objection killed the deal, which competitor was in the room and what the buyer believed about you that was not true. Turn that into the objection handling, the comparison against the incumbent, and the proof assets a champion can forward to the people who were not in the meeting.

Then test the messaging with real reps before it is locked. If the people who have to say it out loud cannot say it without wincing, it is not finished.

Measuring a B2B Sales Strategy

The measures belong in the strategy itself, agreed before the quarter starts. Agreed afterwards, they get chosen to flatter whatever happened. The ones that tell you whether the strategy is right, rather than whether the team is busy:

  • Win rate by source and by segment. If the excluded segments were winnable, this is where you find out.
  • Sales acceptance rate: how much of what marketing passes on, sales agrees is real.
  • Committee reach per open deal: how many of the people who sign off have engaged.
  • Cycle length on target accounts against everything else.
  • Lost-deal reasons, recorded the same way every time, so a pattern can show up.

Set a review date for each quarter, and decide in advance what the review can conclude: keep, change or stop, with the evidence that would justify each. Decided beforehand, the call gets made on the numbers. Decided afterwards, it gets made by whoever argues hardest.

A B2B Sales Strategy Is a Set of Written Decisions

The useful version of a B2B sales strategy is short. It names the accounts worth winning and the ones that are not, picks the motion, says which members of the buying committee have to be reached, draws one funnel both teams own, and agrees how it will be judged before the quarter starts. The cadences, scripts and CRM fields follow from that.

  • Start from a counted, named account list, and write down who is excluded.
  • Decide whether a rep, the buyer or a partner carries the sale before hiring.
  • Plan to reach the six to ten people who sign off, not just the champion.
  • Give sales and marketing one funnel, one set of handoff rules and one report.
  • Agree the measures and review dates before the quarter starts.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask when the number is not moving and sales and marketing disagree about why.

What is a B2B sales strategy?

It is the set of decisions behind how a business sells to other businesses: which accounts are worth winning and which are excluded, whether a rep, the buyer or a partner carries the sale, who inside each account has to be convinced, where marketing hands off to sales, and how the result is measured. The cadences and scripts follow from those.

How is a sales strategy different from a go-to-market strategy?

A go-to-market strategy settles the segment, the position against the alternatives, the offer and price, the channels and the sales motion. The sales strategy works inside those decisions and covers how the sales team wins the accounts the strategy points at.

Should sales or marketing own the B2B sales strategy?

Sales owns the number, but the strategy only works if marketing agrees to the same account list, the same funnel definition and the same handoff rules. Where those are written separately by each team, the two funnels drift apart and the argument about lead quality never ends.

How many people are involved in a B2B purchase?

For a meaningful business purchase, typically six to ten people sign off, and most of them never fill in a form or take a sales call. A strategy that relies on one champion loses the deal when that person goes quiet, changes role or gets overruled.

How often should a sales strategy be reviewed?

Once a quarter, on dates set in advance, with the possible outcomes (keep, change or stop) and the evidence for each agreed beforehand. Long sales cycles mean some measures, like win rate on a new segment, need two or three quarters before they say anything reliable.
