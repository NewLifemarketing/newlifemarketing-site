Why Is My Website Not Ranking? 7 Causes and Fixes

SEO/Troubleshooting

by Rudy Mcnabb Posted on October 2, 2026

Why is my website not ranking? Almost always, it’s one of seven things, and they’re quick to check in the right order. Start at the top of this list and stop when something fails. That’s your problem.

We'll cover:

  • First, Check That Google Can Even See Your Site
  • Your Page Doesn’t Match What People Want
  • The Page Is Too Thin to Compete
  • Nobody Else Vouches for You
  • Your Site Is Slow, Broken on Mobile or Hard to Use
  • You’re New, or You Changed Something Recently
  • You’re Aiming at Searches You Can’t Win Yet
  • FAQ

First, Check That Google Can Even See Your Site

Before you blame content or competitors, confirm the page is in Google’s index. Type site:yourdomain.com/your-page/ into Google. If nothing comes back, the page isn’t indexed, and no amount of writing will help.

Three usual culprits:

  • A noindex tag. Developers add it while a site is being built and forget to remove it. View the page source and search for “noindex.”
  • Blocked in robots.txt. One stray line can wall off your whole site.
  • No path to the page. If nothing on your site links to it, Google may never find it.

Open Google Search Console, paste the URL into the inspection bar and read what it says. “URL is not on Google” with a reason beside it is the clearest answer you’ll get all day.

Your Page Doesn’t Match What People Want

This is the most common reason a website is not ranking, and the least obvious. Google doesn’t rank pages for containing a phrase. It ranks pages that give people what they were looking for.

Say you run a roofing company and your page targets “roof replacement.” Search that phrase and look at page one. If every result is a pricing guide and yours is a sales pitch with a “call us” button, you’re answering a question nobody asked. Rewrite the page to match what’s already winning, then add what they miss.

Open the top five results and note the format (guide, list, service page), the questions they answer and what they leave out. That gap is where you get in.

The Page Is Too Thin to Compete

A 300-word page against a 2,000-word page that answers every follow-up question will lose. Not always, but usually.

Length isn’t the goal. Completeness is. If a reader finishes your page and still has to go back to Google, it didn’t do its job. Add the real details: what it costs, how long it takes, who it’s for, what can go wrong. Specifics beat padding every time.

Also check for pages competing with each other. Two of your own pages chasing the same search split the signal, and often neither wins. Pick the stronger one, fold the other into it and redirect.

Nobody Else Vouches for You

Links from other sites still count as votes. A page with zero of them, competing against pages with dozens from real, relevant sources, starts behind.

You don’t need hundreds. You need the right few: local business associations, suppliers, partners and press that actually relate to what you do. Skip any offer to buy 500 links for a small fee. That’s how sites get penalized. Link building is one piece of the larger plan in our SEO work.

Your Site Is Slow, Broken on Mobile or Hard to Use

Google indexes the mobile version of your site first. If a phone user has to pinch, zoom and wait six seconds for the page to load, they leave, and the rankings eventually reflect it.

Run your page through PageSpeed Insights and Google’s mobile-friendly checks, then fix in this order:

  1. Compress oversized images. This is the single most common cause of a slow page.
  2. Remove plugins and scripts you don’t use.
  3. Make sure buttons and text are readable without zooming.

You’re New, or You Changed Something Recently

Sometimes nothing is wrong. A new domain or a freshly published page can take weeks to settle. If you redesigned your site, changed URLs without redirects or moved to a new platform, rankings often dip and take a while to recover.

Check the timeline. Did traffic drop on a specific date? Match that date against anything you changed. If the drop lines up with a redesign and the old URLs now return errors, fix the redirects first.

Patience is only the right answer if nothing else on this list is broken. Don’t use it as an excuse.

You’re Aiming at Searches You Can’t Win Yet

If you’re a five-person business going after “marketing agency” or “plumber,” you’re competing with companies that have spent a decade on it. Fine to want. Wrong place to start.

Go narrower. A specific service plus a specific place or situation has less competition and brings buyers who are closer to calling. If you serve a local market, our guide to choosing a local search engine optimization company covers how that works. Win the narrow searches first, build authority, then reach for the bigger terms.

The Bottom Line

A website that is not ranking usually has one clear blocker. Check the basics in order, because fixing the first thing that fails beats polishing everything at once.

  • Confirm the page is indexed first. If site: shows nothing, no other fix matters yet.
  • Match what searchers actually want, then make the page more complete than the ones already ranking.
  • Fix slow load times, mobile problems and a handful of relevant links before chasing anything fancier.
  • Go after narrower searches you can win now, and build up to the big terms.

Straight answers

FAQ

What business owners ask when their website isn’t ranking.

How long does it take for a new website to rank?

Most new pages take several weeks to be indexed and months to climb for anything competitive. Specific, low-competition searches can move faster. If a page is still missing from the index after a couple of weeks, something is blocking it.

Can I pay Google to rank higher organically?

No. Paid ads sit above the organic results and bring traffic quickly, but you can’t buy an organic position. Ads and SEO work side by side: ads for now, SEO for the long run.

Should I rewrite my page or build a new one?

If the page is already indexed and roughly on topic, improve it. Rewriting keeps whatever history the URL has earned. Build a new page only when you’re targeting a different search entirely.
