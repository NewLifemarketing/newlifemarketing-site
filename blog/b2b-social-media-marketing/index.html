B2B Social Media Marketing

Seen by the Committee Before the Call

B2B/Social Media

by Cruz Lee Posted on November 1, 2026

B2B social media marketing is judged on the wrong audience. Most teams measure the people who follow the company page, when the people who decide whether you win a deal are a buying committee who will look you up once, quietly, a few days before the first call, and never follow anything.

Build for that visit and the rest of the plan gets simpler: who posts, which platforms, what the paid budget is for, and what goes in the report. Build for followers and you get a busy feed that sales never mentions.

What B2B Social Media Marketing Is Actually For

Social is strong at three things: being found, staying in front of people who already know you, and proving that the company is real. It is weak at direct response on its own, at producing a predictable number of leads in its first month, and at replacing a sales process. Buy it for the second list and you will conclude it does not work.

The reason is timing. Most of the companies you would like to sell to are not buying this quarter. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, in work published with LinkedIn's B2B Institute, estimated that roughly 5% of a category's business buyers are in the market in any given quarter, and about 20% over a year. He offered it as an approximation rather than a precise rule, and the figure varies with how often a category is bought. The direction does not. The other 95% will buy later, and the job of b2b social media marketing is to be the name they already know when they do.

That changes what a good post looks like. A post asking a stranger for a demo is aimed at the 5% and wasted on everyone else. A post that teaches something specific, shows the work or puts a real person's judgement in front of the reader is useful to both groups, and it is still there when the other 95% come looking.

The Buying Committee Checks You Before Anyone Calls

A meaningful B2B purchase is signed off by six to ten people, and most of them never fill in a form. Each of them is asking a different question. The economic buyer wants to know whether you are a safe bet. The technical evaluator wants to know whether the product does what the deck says. Finance wants to know whether you will still exist in three years.

None of them asks you. They look. By the time somebody calls, they have already checked the company page, the founder's profile and whatever the team has been posting, and that check is the cheapest place in the whole funnel to lose a deal. A LinkedIn page that last posted in spring tells the committee nobody is home, and it undercuts every other dollar you spend sending them there.

So the first test of a social programme is not reach. It is whether a stranger from a target account, landing on your profiles cold, finds enough recent, specific evidence to make the next conversation easier. That is a test you can run yourself this afternoon: open your own page in a private window and read it as the sceptic on the committee would.

Post From People, Not Just the Company Page

People follow people. A company page is necessary, since LinkedIn requires one to run ads and it is the first thing a prospect checks after seeing one. But it is rarely what earns attention. The posts that travel are written by someone inside the business, with a face and a name on them, saying something they would defend in a meeting.

That matters for paid as much as organic. LinkedIn's Thought Leader Ads let you sponsor an organic post published by a real person, a founder, a director or a technical lead, so it appears in the feed as their content rather than as an ad from a logo. The author approves the boost in Campaign Manager, and once approved the post locks and cannot be edited. Which means the format depends entirely on real people having already published something worth paying to show more widely.

One Person Who Will Post Is Enough to Start

This is where most plans stall, because a programme built on employee posting assumes the employees will post. Usually they will not, at least not on a schedule. Start smaller. One director willing to publish is enough to begin, and they do not have to write every word. The material can be produced with them: an interview recorded once and cut into several posts, a short clip from a call they were already on, a written argument drafted from what they said and edited until it sounds like them.

What does not work is writing ad copy and pretending it is organic. Readers can tell, and so can the committee member who checks the author's profile and finds nothing else there.

Consistency Is a Production Problem

Feeds go quiet for a predictable reason. Somebody remembers at four in the afternoon that nothing went out today, a stock graphic and a caption go up, and by the second month there is nothing left to post. It looks like a discipline problem. It is a supply problem: nobody scheduled the material the posts were supposed to be made from.

The fix is to decide the month before it starts. Every post, on every platform, with the caption written and the asset already produced, approved in advance and then scheduled. Nothing is decided on the day, and nothing depends on anyone remembering.

That only works if one piece of production can feed many posts. A single recording session, whether an interview with your head of product or a walkthrough of a real customer problem, can become a long-form video, several short clips, a carousel and a written post. Repurposing is not reposting, though. The same square graphic pushed to every platform performs on none of them, because each one rewards a different format. The hook gets re-cut, the caption gets rewritten for how people read on that platform, the first two seconds change. Same idea, a genuinely different asset.

Own Two Platforms Instead of Six

Concentration beats coverage. A B2B company that is forgettable on six platforms would be better off fully owning two, and for most B2B and professional services businesses those two are LinkedIn and YouTube, with Instagram carrying culture and proof if there is capacity for it.

  • LinkedIn is where B2B credibility is built: long written posts and native video, from the company page and from real people inside the business. It is also the only platform where you can put something in front of a named job title at a named company, repeatedly.
  • YouTube is a search engine as much as a social platform. A long-form video that genuinely teaches something keeps being found for years, which makes it the closest thing social has to a search asset, and it is the raw material for a month of short clips.

The test for adding a third is whether you can feed it without starving the first two. Usually you cannot, and an account that posts twice and goes silent is worse than no account at all.

Organic Is What Makes the Paid Layer Work

Paid social and organic social are usually bought by different people from different budgets, and that split is expensive. A company page with nothing on it converts nobody, so the organic presence has to exist before the ads that send people to it.

On LinkedIn, the paid layer works best built around a named account list rather than a set of filters. Industry, company size and seniority produce an audience of people who resemble your buyers. An account list produces the companies you actually want, with job function and seniority layered on top so the economic buyer, the champion and the technical evaluator all see you. Current customers, live pipeline and competitors get excluded before launch, not after somebody notices the spend.

Then the ask has to match the temperature of the audience. Cold, retargeting and account-list traffic belong in separate campaigns, even when they run the same asset, because mixing them lets the platform optimise toward the cheapest person to reach rather than the right one. The request for a meeting appears only in the last stage, against people who have already seen you several times. Asking a stranger for thirty minutes from a cold ad is how most LinkedIn budgets get spent, and then the platform gets blamed.

The B2B Social Media Marketing Signal Most Teams Ignore

Campaign Manager has a view showing which companies are engaging with your ads, not just how many clicks there were. Most advertisers never open it. Exported weekly and handed to sales, it is a warm outbound list: these specific target accounts have now seen you several times and responded. That is the bridge between social and account-based marketing, and it turns a paid campaign into a source of qualified outbound as well as inbound.

Treat it the way you would treat any intent signal. It tells you where to look, not what will close, and it decays. A list exported on Monday and read at the next monthly meeting has been bought and thrown away.

Give the Reach Somewhere to Go

Reach with nowhere to send it is attention that leaks. For a considered B2B purchase, the most useful destination is usually a live session: a webinar that gives away real methodology rather than teasing it, promoted from every platform in the plan.

Attendance does the filtering. Somebody who registers, turns up and stays to the end has shown more intent than any form fill can, and the conversation afterwards starts warm, informed and much shorter. It also gives the whole content programme a job, so every clip and post has somewhere specific to point.

Not every offer suits one. If your buyers would not give up 40 minutes to learn how you think, the destination might be a document, a benchmark or a short diagnostic instead. What matters is that there is one, and that it is wired into the CRM so somebody follows up.

Measuring B2B Social Media Marketing Without Counting Followers

Follower count is the number that feels like progress and predicts the least. It can rise every month while nothing happens in the pipeline. A useful report for b2b social media marketing reads the signals that sit closer to revenue, and says plainly which of them are early.

  • Saves, shares and comments, which are the signals feeds now weight, and a fair proxy for whether a post was worth reading.
  • Profile visits, link clicks and direct messages: the actions closest to someone deciding to talk to you.
  • Target accounts engaged, and how many roles inside each one you have reached. A deal with one person engaged dies when that person goes quiet.
  • Sales acceptance of what social and paid social produced: the share sales agreed was real, not the share a form captured.
  • Pipeline influenced, with the attribution window stated on every view and platform-reported figures labelled as platform-reported.

Give it a fair window. The first month of a properly run programme buys consistency, the second buys a library of material, and the third is the first month where decisions can be made from evidence rather than opinion. Judging it in week two produces the same verdict as never starting.

B2B Social Media Marketing Is Built for the Committee

The version of b2b social media marketing that pays for itself is aimed at a small number of people who will look you up once, at a moment you do not control. It is published by real people, fed by planned production rather than afternoon panic, concentrated on two platforms, joined to a paid layer built on a named account list, and measured on accounts and conversations rather than followers.

  • Plan for the 95% of the market not buying yet, and the committee that checks you before the call.
  • Get one real person posting before building a programme around employees.
  • Decide the month before it starts, and produce once for many posts.
  • Own LinkedIn and YouTube properly before adding anything else.
  • Report target accounts engaged, roles reached and sales acceptance, not follower count.

Straight answers

FAQ

What B2B marketing leaders ask when the feed is busy and sales still never mentions it.

What is B2B social media marketing?

It is using social platforms to sell to other businesses: building recognition inside the companies you want to win, giving the buying committee something credible to find when they look you up, and feeding sales with accounts that are engaging. It works on a longer cycle than consumer social, because most of the market is not buying at any given moment.

Which social media platforms are best for B2B?

For most B2B and professional services businesses, LinkedIn and YouTube, with Instagram carrying culture and proof if there is capacity. Fully owning two platforms does more than being forgettable on six.

Does B2B social media marketing generate leads?

Some, but that is not what it is strongest at. Organic social is best at being found, remembered and believed; leads come when a paid layer, an account list and a destination such as a webinar are built on top of it. If you need lead volume this month, start with search ads and run social underneath.

Should B2B companies post from the company page or from employees?

Both, but people carry more of the weight. The company page is required to run LinkedIn ads and is checked by every prospect, while posts from a founder or director earn more attention and can be sponsored as Thought Leader Ads. One person willing to publish is enough to start.

How long does B2B social media take to show results?

Plan on about 90 days before judging it. The first month buys consistency, the second builds a library of material, and the third is the first where you can steer from evidence. Pipeline effects on a long B2B cycle can take longer than that to show up in closed revenue.
