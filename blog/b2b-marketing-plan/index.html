B2B Marketing Plan

A Plan Your Finance Team Can Check

B2B/Strategy

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 30, 2026

A B2B marketing plan is usually written in the wrong direction. It starts with a list of activities, a webinar a month, a new website, more LinkedIn, and puts a budget at the bottom. The plan that survives its first bad quarter runs the other way: it starts with the revenue number, works back to the pipeline and the accounts that number needs, and only then decides which channels get the money.

That order matters because of what happens in month four. Results are slow, somebody asks what marketing is for, and a plan built from activities can only answer by listing what it did. A plan built from the number can show where the pipeline is against what it was meant to be, and why.

What a B2B Marketing Plan Is For

Three documents get called a plan, and they do different jobs. The go-to-market strategy decides where and how you win: the segment, the position against the incumbent, the offer, the sales motion. The go-to-market plan turns those decisions into owners and dates. The B2B marketing plan sits underneath both. It is the operating document for marketing's share of the year: how much pipeline marketing is responsible for, which accounts it is aimed at, what the money buys, and how anyone will know whether it is working.

If the strategy above it is missing, the marketing plan ends up making strategic decisions by accident. A channel budget quietly decides the segment. A content calendar quietly decides the position. That is how a company ends up with a busy marketing team and a sales team that describes the customer differently. When that is the situation, fix the strategy first; no amount of planning underneath it will compensate.

When the strategy exists, the plan has four questions to answer in writing, each with a number next to it:

  • How much pipeline marketing owes the business, by quarter.
  • Which accounts that pipeline has to come from.
  • What the budget buys, split by the job each part does.
  • How it is judged, and when.

Work Back From the Revenue Number

Start with the new revenue the business needs next year and divide it back through your own funnel. The numbers below are an illustration, not a benchmark. Replace every one of them with your figures from the last four quarters of closed deals.

StepExampleWhere your number comes from
New revenue target$1,200,000The board or the budget
Average new deal$60,000Closed-won, last four quarters
Deals needed20Target divided by deal size
Win rate on qualified opportunities25%Your CRM, not an industry average
Opportunities needed80Deals divided by win rate
Share marketing is responsible for50%Agreed with sales, in writing
Marketing-sourced opportunities40, about 10 a quarterThe number this plan is built to hit

Two things fall out of that table straight away. First, the target is opportunities, not leads. Forty qualified opportunities is a number sales will recognise. Four hundred leads is a number they will argue with. Second, the sales cycle sets the calendar. If a deal takes two or three quarters to close, the opportunities that pay for next year have to be created early in it, so a plan that ramps spend in the second half is a plan for the year after.

The budget question usually arrives here, and the honest answer is that there is no correct percentage. Gartner's 2026 CMO Spend Survey put average marketing budgets at 7.8% of company revenue, up from 7.7% in 2025, but its 401 respondents mostly work at companies with more than $1 billion in revenue. A benchmark from that sample tells a mid-sized B2B company very little. The funnel above tells it more: what an opportunity has cost you in the past, multiplied by how many you need, is a budget you can defend line by line.

Count the Accounts Before the Channels

Most B2B markets are a countable list of companies, and the plan should start from that list rather than from an audience description. "Mid-market manufacturers in Ontario" is a targeting idea. Three hundred named companies, ranked, is a plan.

Counting them properly takes more work than it sounds. In an account research build we ran for a brand selling into Ontario's licensed cannabis stores, the market looked like 1,903 stores. Grouped by the company that actually decides what to stock, it was 625 buyers, and four banners that looked like four prospects turned out to be one 98-store chain. A plan that budgeted for 1,903 targets would have overspent, and a plan that treated those four banners separately would have sent one buyer four different pitches.

Tiering the Accounts in a B2B Marketing Plan

Once the list is real, tier it by what each account is worth, because that decides what each one can be given. A short list of the most valuable accounts can carry one-to-one work: researched briefs, tailored content, executive outreach. A larger middle tier gets plays built for a cluster of similar companies. The long tail gets programmatic reach. Most programmes run all three at once.

Then map the people. A meaningful business purchase is signed off by six to ten people, and most of them never fill in a form. Single-threaded deals die when the champion goes quiet, changes role or gets overruled. So the plan should say, per tier, which roles it has to reach inside each account and how it will know when it has. This is the core of account-based marketing, and where it is new to a company we usually start it as a contained pilot: one tier, one objective, a defined list and a fixed window, while the existing programmes keep running.

Split Capture From Creation

Every B2B marketing plan funds two different kinds of work, and the most useful thing it can do is keep them apart.

Capture reaches the buyers already looking: search, review sites, retargeting, the page and the follow-up that catch somebody when the problem has become urgent. It converts this month and is easy to report.

Creation reaches the much larger group who will have the problem but are not looking yet. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, writing for LinkedIn's B2B Institute in 2021, put the share of a business market buying in a given quarter at perhaps 5%, and said plainly it is not a precise rule. The direction holds for most categories. At any moment, most of the companies that will buy from you in the next two years are not in the market.

That is why capture alone stops working. Everybody bids for the same small in-market slice, cost per lead climbs and pipeline stays flat. Demand generation is the work on the other 95%.

How much goes to each? Les Binet and Peter Field's analysis of B2B effectiveness cases for the B2B Institute, The 5 Principles of Growth in B2B Marketing, suggested roughly 46% brand and 54% activation for B2B, against about 60/40 in consumer marketing. They describe it as a guiding principle from a small sample, and that is how to use it: a starting split to adjust, not a ratio to hit.

The administrative point matters more than the ratio. If the two halves share one budget line and one report, creation loses every time a quarter tightens, because capture's numbers arrive the same week and creation's arrive next year. Give them separate lines, separate targets and a minimum term for creation written into the plan. A programme stopped at month three has bought the cost and none of the return.

Give Each Channel One Job

A channel plan written as a list of platforms, each with a slice of budget, cannot be judged, because nothing says what each slice is supposed to do. Write it as jobs instead:

  • LinkedIn: reach the named accounts. It is the one platform that can reliably put something in front of a named job title at a named company, repeatedly. Clicks commonly run $8 to $15 on our LinkedIn Ads work, which is cheap precision if a closed account is worth five figures and expensive noise if it is not.
  • Search: capture the people already looking. Small volume in most B2B categories, high value per click, judged on opportunities rather than form fills.
  • Meta and Google retargeting: stay in front of the list. Keep the accounts that have already engaged seeing you between sales touches.
  • Content: give the champion something to forward. The comparison against the incumbent, the business case, the answers a sceptic on the committee will ask for.

Each line in the plan then carries a budget, the job, and what it must return to keep its place next quarter. A channel that cannot say what it is for gets cut first, and it should.

Measuring a B2B Marketing Plan Before It Spends

The measures belong in the B2B marketing plan itself, agreed with sales and finance before the first dollar goes out. Agreed afterwards, they get chosen to flatter whatever happened. The ones that hold up:

  • Pipeline created and influenced, against the quarterly number from the funnel above.
  • Cost per opportunity, not cost per lead. Cost per lead is lowest where the leads are worst.
  • Sales acceptance rate: how much of what marketing passes on, sales agrees is real.
  • Win rate by source, so each channel is judged on the deals it produces.
  • Committee reach per target account: how many of the people who sign off you have reached.
  • Branded search volume over a quarter, the slow measure of whether creation is working.

Write the reporting rules down too. Platform-reported numbers are labelled as platform-reported, the attribution window is stated on every view, early data is flagged as early, and three platforms' figures are never added together and called revenue, because each platform attributes independently and their claims overlap.

Set the Review Dates Now

A plan is a set of bets, and some of them will be wrong. The question is whether you find out deliberately or by drift. Put the review dates in the plan, one a quarter, and decide in advance what each review is allowed to conclude: keep, change or stop, with the evidence that would justify each. Decided beforehand, the call is made on the numbers. Decided afterwards, it is made by whoever argues hardest.

Each review should ask the same few things. Is marketing-sourced pipeline on track against the quarter's number? Is the account list still right, or has something changed in the market? Is the creation budget still protected, or has it been quietly moved to capture? Which channel has not done its job for two quarters running?

Signs the Plan Has Turned Into a Calendar

It happens slowly. The weekly meeting reviews what was published rather than what moved. The report leads with impressions. Nobody can say which accounts the last campaign was aimed at. Sales has stopped attending. Any two of those, and the plan has become a list of activities again, which is where it started.

A B2B Marketing Plan Starts and Ends With the Number

The useful version of a B2B marketing plan is short and specific. It says how much pipeline marketing owes, which named accounts it comes from, what each part of the budget is for, and how the whole thing will be judged, before any of it is spent. Everything else, the calendar, the campaigns, the creative, follows from those answers.

  • Work back from the revenue target to opportunities, using your own win rate and deal size.
  • Build the plan on a counted, tiered list of accounts and the people who sign inside them.
  • Fund creation and capture separately, with a minimum term for creation.
  • Give every channel one job and one number it must return.
  • Agree the measures and the review dates before the first dollar goes out.

What should a B2B marketing plan include?

Four things, each with a number: the pipeline marketing is responsible for by quarter, the named accounts it has to come from, the budget split by the job each part does, and the measures and review dates it will be judged on. A calendar of campaigns follows from those; it is not the plan.

How do you split a B2B budget between brand and lead generation?

How is a B2B marketing plan different from a go-to-market plan?

The go-to-market plan settles who you sell to, why they should choose you, how you sell and what happens at launch. The marketing plan is the operating document underneath it: the pipeline marketing owes, the accounts, the budget and the measures for the year.

How often should a B2B marketing plan be reviewed?

Once a quarter, on dates set when the plan is written, with the possible outcomes (keep, change or stop) and the evidence for each agreed in advance. Long sales cycles mean some measures need two or three quarters before they say anything reliable.
