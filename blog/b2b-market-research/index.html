B2B Market Research

Know Who Buys Before You Spend

B2B/Go-To-Market

by Cruz Lee Posted on October 24, 2026

Most B2B market research answers a question nobody was going to act on. A survey confirms that buyers value reliability, a report sizes the category in billions, and the plan that follows looks exactly like the plan before it, because none of the findings were tied to a decision.

Useful research works the other way round. Write down the decisions the next year depends on: which companies to sell to, why they would pick you over the incumbent, who inside them signs, and where they look before they ever talk to a seller. Then go and find the evidence for each one, mostly from people and records you already have access to.

Start B2B Market Research With the Decision

Before commissioning anything, list the calls the research has to settle. A good list is short and uncomfortable: which segment gets the budget first, what you say you are better at, which channels earn money and which are noise, and whether the sales motion you have can actually reach the people who buy.

Each question on that list points at its own method. Sizing a segment is desk work with public counts. Why a deal was won or lost is an interview. Who sits on the buying committee is in your CRM and in your reps' heads. Where buyers look before they call is partly your own analytics and partly asking them. Research that starts from the method ("let's run a survey") tends to produce whatever that method is good at, whether or not it bears on the decision.

It also keeps the work small. A B2B seller rarely needs a statistically representative sample of a category. It needs enough evidence to stop arguing about four or five choices, written down where sales, marketing and finance can all see it.

That is how the evidence stage of our go-to-market strategy work is set up: five decisions (market, customer, offer, channel and motion), and nothing ships until they are settled.

B2B Market Research Counts Companies, Not People

Consumer research thinks in millions of people. B2B market research should think in companies, because companies are what you sell to, and for most B2B sellers the number is smaller than the category reports suggest.

Take Ontario. Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada's Key Small Business Statistics 2025 counts 418,322 employer businesses in the province as of December 2024. Only 6,640 of them have 100 to 499 employees, and 1,528 have 500 or more. If your offer only makes sense for organisations above 100 staff, your whole Ontario market is roughly 8,000 companies before you filter by industry, and once you do, it is often a list a team could read in an afternoon.

Turn B2B market research into a countable account list

  • Start with firmographics you can verify: industry, size band, region. Public counts like ISED's tell you the ceiling.
  • Narrow with what predicts a good customer, not what describes any customer: the technology they run, the trigger that makes them buy, the size at which they can afford you and still grow afterwards.
  • Name the companies. If the result is a few hundred, it is a target account list, and the rest of your research can be about those companies specifically.

That last step changes everything downstream. Once the market is a list of names, research stops being about "the category" and becomes about accounts you can look up, call and track. Our account-based work defines the ICP as accounts that can buy at your size and expand afterwards, "not everyone who could theoretically use the product", and the count is where that discipline starts.

Interview the Deals You Won and Lost

The most valuable B2B market research most companies never do is talking to the buyers they already have records for. Your won deals know why they picked you. Your lost deals know why they did not, and they are usually more candid about it than anyone on your payroll.

Recent buyers are the right sample because the decision is fresh and the process is real. A cold survey of "IT decision-makers" tells you what people say they value; a 30-minute call with someone who signed a contract last quarter tells you what actually moved the decision, who else was in the room and what nearly killed it.

Questions that earn their time:

  1. What was happening that made you start looking? The trigger is what your marketing has to find and reach.
  2. Who else was involved, and what did each of them need to hear? This maps the committee from the inside.
  3. Which other options did you look at, and how did they get on the list? This tells you where the shortlist was written.
  4. What nearly stopped it? Objections from a real deal are worth more than any guessed list.
  5. How would you describe what we do to a colleague? If it does not match your positioning, the market has repositioned you already.

Have someone other than the account's rep run the lost-deal calls. A buyer will tell a neutral interviewer things they would never say to the person who was trying to close them. On our go-to-market engagements the first 30 days are evidence, and customer and lost-deal interviews are part of it, conducted by us, alongside a competitive teardown and a review of your own data.

Research the Buying Committee, Not the Form

The person who fills in your form is rarely the person who decides. Forrester's State of Business Buying 2024 found that, on average, 13 people within an organisation are involved in the buying decision, and 89% of purchases involve two or more departments.

So the unit of research is the committee. For each role, you want to know what changes for them if they buy, what they are afraid of, and what they need to see before they agree. The economic buyer is asking about return and risk. The person who will use the thing every day is asking whether it will make their week worse. The technical evaluator is asking whether it fits what they already run, and someone in procurement or finance is asking whether it can wait.

Those are different questions, and one message cannot answer all of them. Research that treats "the buyer" as one person produces messaging that speaks to none of them. In our account-based marketing programmes the value proposition is written separately for the economic buyer, the champion, the technical evaluator and the blocker, "because they are asking different questions", and that only works if the research found out what those questions are.

Your CRM already holds part of this. Pull the contacts attached to your last 20 closed opportunities and note their roles. If most deals show one or two contacts, either your team is single-threaded or the CRM is missing the people who actually decided. Both are findings.

Find Out How the Shortlist Gets Written

The hardest fact for a B2B seller to accept is how late it arrives. 6sense's 2025 B2B Buyer Experience Report, drawn from nearly 4,000 buyer responses, puts first contact with sellers at 61% of the way through the buying journey. Buyers initiated 79% of those first engagements. The winning vendor was already on the day-one shortlist 95% of the time, and the vendor the buyer favoured before contact won roughly 80% of the time.

Read that as a research brief. If the shortlist is mostly written before anyone talks to you, then the most important thing to learn is where it gets written: which peers, communities, review sites, analysts, searches and content your buyers rely on in the stretch before they reach out. Ask about it in every interview. It is the question that most directly decides where the channel budget goes.

Then look at your own signals. The visits, return visits, pricing-page views and email engagement already sitting in your analytics and CRM show which accounts are researching before they ever identify themselves. Those first-party signals are under-used at most companies and cost nothing extra, which is why our intent and signal work exhausts them before recommending a data purchase. Bought intent data has its place, but it is directional: it tells you where to look, not what will close.

Read the Competition the Way a Buyer Does

Most competitive research is a feature grid built from competitors' websites. That tells you what they claim. It does not tell you what a buyer comparing you would conclude, which is the only version that matters.

Do the teardown from the buyer's seat. Search the way your interviewees said they searched. Read the pages a buyer would land on, request the demo, sit through the follow-up. Note what each competitor leads with, what proof they show and what they leave out. Then put the result next to what your lost deals told you, because the reason you lose is usually in the gap between the two.

Two patterns turn up often enough to look for on purpose:

  • Everyone says the same thing. When four vendors all lead with "trusted partner" and "end-to-end", the category reads as interchangeable and price decides. The opening for you is whatever a buyer cares about that nobody claims.
  • You are being bought as something adjacent. If you win deals you should not and lose ones you should, the market is usually reading your offer as a different thing from what it is. That is a positioning problem, and only buyers' own words will show you which thing they think you are.

What comes out should be usable by sales the same week: a short battlecard per competitor with what they say, where they are strong, where they are weak, and the question that exposes it.

What B2B Market Research Should Hand You

Research is finished when the decisions are made, not when the report is long. Measure the output against the questions you started with:

The decisionWhere the evidence comes fromWhat it should produce
Which companies to sell toPublic business counts, your closed deals, firmographic and technographic dataAn ICP and segmentation model with account scoring, and a named target list
Why a buyer picks youWon and lost deal interviewsPositioning and messaging, per segment, in buyers' own words
Who has to agreeInterviews, CRM contact roles, salesA buying-committee map with each role's question answered
Where the shortlist is writtenInterviews, first-party signals, search and community behaviourA channel plan with budget allocation and expected return per channel
Who you are up againstA teardown from the buyer's seat, lost-deal reasonsBattlecards sales will actually use

Before anything is locked, test it with the people who have to use it. We run a messaging test with real reps before positioning is finalised, because a message that sounds right in a workshop and dies on the first call was never right.

And put owners and dates on it. B2B market research that ends as a deck is a document; research that ends as a 90-day plan with owners, dates and the metric each part is judged on is the start of a go-to-market. If you already have a plan and want to check whether it is aimed at the right accounts, our B2B marketing audit post covers the same ground from the other direction.

Research the Decisions, Then Stop

B2B market research earns its cost when every finding changes a decision. Count the market in companies, talk to the buyers who already chose for or against you, map the people who have to agree, find out where the shortlist gets written, and read the competition from the buyer's side. Then write it down, test it with sales and put dates on it.

  • List the four or five decisions the research must settle before choosing any method.
  • Size the market as a count of companies; in a narrow B2B segment it is often a few hundred names.
  • Interview recent won and lost deals, with someone other than the rep on the lost ones.
  • Research each role on the buying committee separately; one message cannot answer all of them.
  • Find out where buyers build their shortlist, because most of it exists before they call you.

Straight answers

FAQ

What founders and revenue leaders ask before they commission market research, or run it themselves.

What is B2B market research?

It is research into the companies you sell to rather than individual consumers: how many there are, which ones are a good fit, who inside them makes the decision, why they choose one supplier over another and where they look before they talk to a seller. Done well, it is tied to specific decisions such as which segment to target, how to position the offer and which channels to fund.

How is B2B market research different from B2C research?

The populations are far smaller, so you can often name every company in the market instead of sampling. Decisions are made by groups rather than one person, so you have to research several roles inside the same account. And the people worth hearing from are busy and hard to reach, which makes interviews with your own recent buyers and lost deals more useful than broad surveys.

How many customer interviews are enough?

Enough that new interviews stop telling you anything new. Cover both won and lost deals, more than one segment if you sell to several, and more than one role on the buying committee. When the reasons people give start repeating, you have the pattern; more calls past that point add confidence, not insight.

Can intent data replace market research?

No. Intent data is directional: it is good at showing that an account is researching a category and poor at telling you whether it will buy from you or why. Use it to decide where to look and when, and use interviews and your own deal history to understand the decision itself. Your own first-party signals, from your site, CRM and email, are worth exhausting before you pay for a platform.

How long does B2B market research take?

For a focused go-to-market decision, the evidence stage can fit in about 30 days: interviews, a competitive teardown and a review of your own data. On our go-to-market engagements that first month is followed by 30 days building and locking the strategy and 30 days launching against it with weekly readouts.
