Dryden Pay Per Click PPC

Mostly, Not This

A dryden pay per click ppc account is the wrong purchase for most businesses in a town of 7,388 people with thirty local searches a month, and saying so first is more useful than anything that could be said afterwards about bidding.

The size of it

Thirty Searches Is Not A Channel

The number is small enough that the usual advice stops applying entirely.

A campaign needs inventory: searches to appear against. Thirty a month across a town this size means a handful of impressions a day for most trades, and no budget of any size can be absorbed by that.

Which matters because a budget is an instruction to spend. Pointed at a market with almost nothing in it, an account will reach for whatever else is available — broader matches, automatic placements, geography nobody drew — and nothing reports that as a problem.

The budget gets spent, the clicks are real, the enquiries are not, and the monthly report describes a busy account the whole time.

So the first honest recommendation here is usually to spend the money on the profile, the pages and the material instead, and to use advertising for two narrow jobs that genuinely need it.

The two jobs

What A Dryden Pay Per Click PPC Budget Is Genuinely For

Both are temporary, both are testable, and neither is a monthly retainer.

Buying an answer

Does this offer land, does this page convert, which words do the people who actually enquire use. A fortnight of modest spending settles each, and the answers then direct unpaid work that would have been guesswork.

Reaching the region

A map result places you relative to where the searcher is standing, so a business here is largely invisible an hour out. Paid reach has no such constraint, and for anything serving the wider northwest that is a real job.

Retargeting, underneath both

The audience has already visited and demonstrated interest, reaching them again costs a fraction of reaching a stranger, and it is the most common thing missing entirely from small accounts.

Each needs a stated end: a number, a date, or an answer. Without one of those three a test quietly becomes a permanent line item, which in a market this size is the commonest way a small budget turns into a large one with nothing to show.

How a phrase is matched

The Setting That Decides How Wide You Cast

One of the few genuinely technical choices, and it is worth understanding in plain words.

A phrase can be matched exactly as typed, matched to close variants, or matched to anything a system judges related. The three behave completely differently and cost completely differently.

Broad matching finds volume, which sounds like what a small market needs and is usually the opposite. In a thin auction it reaches for anything remotely adjacent and spends the budget on the adjacent things.

So in a small market the sensible default is tight: the exact phrases people use, plus a short list of close variants, and nothing else until there is evidence worth widening for.

Widening later is a decision made from data. Starting wide is a decision made from hope, and the money that answers the question has been spent by the time the answer arrives.

When the ads run

When A Dryden Pay Per Click PPC Account Should Not Be Running

A setting with a visible effect on cost per enquiry, left at the default in most accounts.

The platform will spend the budget evenly across the week unless told otherwise, including at nine in the evening and all day Sunday. Those clicks cost the same and convert far worse for anything that needs a person.

So the schedule should match the hours somebody actually picks up, plus an hour either side for the people who are planning rather than acting.

Where the enquiry arrives as a form rather than a call, the restriction matters less, but the speed of the reply matters more. An enquiry answered within minutes converts several times better than one answered the next morning.

Look at which hours produced enquiries rather than clicks after a month. In a small account that is a handful of rows and it usually points at a schedule nobody would have guessed.

Reading what was searched

Fifteen Minutes A Week In One Report

The only maintenance a small account genuinely needs, and the one that is skipped.

The report listing what people actually typed before clicking is where an account is managed. Everything else — bids, budgets, settings — moves less than this does.

Read it weekly. Anything irrelevant gets excluded, anything unexpectedly good gets its own treatment, and anything that reveals a service you do not offer gets noted for the business rather than the account.

That last one is worth more than the advertising. People search for things you could sell and do not, and this report is the only place that demand is visible.

In an account this size the report is a page long. A supplier charging a monthly fee who cannot show you what they excluded last month is not doing the only job that needs doing.

Keep the exclusion list even if the campaign stops. It is the one asset a short run leaves behind, it is a few lines of text, and starting again without it means paying for the same irrelevant clicks a second time.

Keep the notes beside it as well: what the average click cost, which hours produced anything, which phrases turned out to be worthless. Six lines written at the end of a run is the entire institutional memory of a campaign, and without it the next one begins by buying the same answers again.

Our scope

What We Run Here

Narrow, and frequently the recommendation is to run nothing.

We build and run Google and Meta accounts against cost per enquiry, write the pages the ads point at, and set up the tracking that connects an enquiry to its source. We manage over $70,000 a month in advertising spend for Ontario businesses and the owner runs the paid media personally.

What we would usually say to a dryden pay per click ppc enquiry

For most businesses here the honest answer to a dryden pay per click ppc enquiry is a fortnight to settle a specific question, retargeting left running underneath, and the rest of the budget spent on the profile, the pages and the material. That is a smaller engagement than a retainer and in a thirty-search market it is the correct one.

We are in Minesing, a long way south. For an advertising account that changes nothing; for filming it means travel, which we price as its own visible line rather than folding it into a fee.

Is there enough search volume to advertise here?

Not for most trades. Thirty searches a month means a handful of impressions a day, and no budget can be absorbed by that. The risk is not losing an auction; it is that a budget pointed at an empty market reaches for broader matches and automatic placements, and nothing reports that as a problem.

What should we spend the money on instead?

The Business Profile, one page per thing you sell, reviews collected on purpose, and material about your own work. All of those keep earning after the card is declined, and in a town this size they usually outperform a quarter of advertising.

When is advertising genuinely worth it here?

To settle a question quickly — does the offer land, does the page convert, which words do people use — and to reach the wider region, where a map result cannot place you. Retargeting runs underneath both. All three need a stated end or a fixed job.

How do we decide the budget?

From three numbers: what a customer is worth, what share of enquiries convert, and the maximum defensible cost per enquiry. In a town this size the third frequently comes out below what a click costs, which is a useful answer reached deliberately rather than after a quarter of spending.
