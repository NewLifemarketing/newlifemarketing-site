Where they started

Forty Years, Somebody Else’s Name On The Box

Sophia’s Garden Artisan has been making chocolate for more than forty years. For most of that time, they made it for other people.

Producing to spec under another company’s label means somebody else sets the retail price, somebody else owns the relationship with the store and the customer, and somebody else keeps the margin that comes with owning it. The product was theirs. Everything around it belonged to a client.

Contract manufacturing is a price-taker’s business. You compete on cost against whoever quotes lower next quarter, you carry the production risk, and you have no pricing power — because what is being bought is capacity, not brand. Forty years of excellent work under other labels leaves you with exactly as much brand equity as you started with: none.

This was not a rescue. Going own-brand was a deliberate long-term plan, decided from a position of strength rather than forced by a lost account. That distinction shaped everything — there was time to do it properly, and no reason to cut corners to hit a number.

No pricing power

The buyer is purchasing capacity, and capacity is comparable. The only lever either side has is price.

No brand equity

Four decades of production built somebody else’s name. None of it compounds for you.

No customer relationship

The store and the shopper belong to whoever is on the box. You never learn who buys, or why.

The constraint

And You Cannot Advertise Your Way Out Of It

The usual route out of contract manufacturing is to build a consumer brand. In licensed cannabis, that route is closed by law.

Under the Cannabis Act, a licence holder cannot promote to consumers the way an ordinary confectionery company can. No lifestyle imagery. No testimonials about how a product feels. Nothing that could appeal to young people. No depiction of consumption. And on top of the statute, the major ad platforms prohibit the category outright in their own policies — so even compliant consumer advertising has almost nowhere to run.

For most brands, marketing spend is the lever you pull to build recognition. Here that lever does not exist. Any launch plan that assumed consumer advertising was going to fail before it started.

Off the table

Lifestyle imagery. Effect and benefit claims. Consumer testimonials. Youth appeal. Depictions of consumption. Paid social, and consumer advertising on the major platforms.

What remains

Communication with the licensed trade. Educational content about the company and its capability. The packaging itself. And being findable by a buyer who is already looking for a supplier.

The strategy

Sell To The Buyer You Are Allowed To Sell To

The restriction decided the plan. If you cannot market the product experience to consumers, you market the company and its capability to the people who decide what goes on the shelf.

Point the whole launch at the retail buyer

The decision everything else followed from

The people who determine whether Sophia’s Garden reaches a customer at all are OCS-licensed retailers choosing what to stock. So the brand had to look like something a store wants on its shelf, and the company had to look like a supplier worth carrying and easy to order from.

Build the brand the product needed

Before anything could be sold under their own name

Identity, colour system, naming, domain and the packaging system. In own-brand confectionery the package is not decoration — it is the only promotional surface that reaches a consumer legally and reliably, every single time. It has to do the work an ad campaign would normally do, from a few square inches, inside a regulated format.

Make them credible before the first conversation

Because a buyer researches a supplier first

A full website designed and built from scratch, with SEO behind it. A retail buyer looking into a new supplier finds a real company with forty years of production behind it — not a manufacturer with a phone number.

Publish constantly, inside the rules

From day one, not after launch

A 30-day social media plan, daily blog posts and research articles, plus Google Ads. No video advertising. Volume matters here because the one thing you can do freely is be informative, and the company that publishes is the one a buyer finds.

The centrepiece

A Private Ordering Portal For Licensed Retailers

The single most important thing we built is not public. It is a custom login portal for OCS-licensed retailers.

A licensed retailer logs in and sees the full product range — the catalogue that cannot legally be shown to a consumer. From inside the portal they can get in touch directly, and that enquiry lands in the CRM as a lead, attributed and followed up rather than lost in an inbox.

It solves the exact problem the Cannabis Act creates. The product information a buyer needs in order to stock you is information you are not allowed to broadcast. Putting it behind a licensed-retailer login makes it available to precisely the audience permitted to see it, and to nobody else — while turning what would have been an anonymous website visit into a named, trackable trade lead.

Gated by licence

The full range is visible to OCS-licensed retailers, and not published to the open web.

Contact in context

A buyer enquires while looking at the product, rather than hunting for an email address.

Straight into the CRM

Every enquiry becomes a tracked lead with an owner and a follow-up, not an inbox message.

The discipline

Compliant By Design, Not By Review

In a regulated category, compliance is either built into how the work is produced, or it becomes the bottleneck that stops work shipping.

The common approach is to create marketing normally and have somebody check it afterwards. That fails at volume. Reviews become a queue, the queue becomes a backlog, and the backlog becomes a reason not to publish — which is fatal when the plan depends on publishing daily.

The alternative is a content rule applied before anything is made. Here it is one sentence: we market the company and its know-how, never the product experience. Process, craft, sourcing, standards, four decades of capability — all publishable. How it tastes, how it feels, who should use it and why — never. Applied at the point of writing, it holds at daily cadence.

Publishable

The company, the process, the craft, the standards, the licence, the people, the capability, the category itself.

Never published

Product experience, effects, benefits, consumption, lifestyle framing, or anything that could read as appealing to young people.

What we built

Every Piece, And Why It Exists

Brand identity

Logo, colour system, typography and naming — the foundation that made an own-label product possible at all.

Packaging system

The most important asset in the programme, and the only advertising that legally reaches a consumer.

Domain & website

Designed and built end to end — the credibility check a retail buyer runs before a first conversation.

Retailer login portal

Gated product range for OCS-licensed retailers, with enquiries routed straight into the CRM as leads.

CRM & lead routing

Every trade enquiry captured, owned and followed up rather than sitting in an inbox.

SEO programme

Technical and content work aimed at trade search intent, not consumer intent.

30-day social plan

A rolling content calendar built so the compliance rule is applied at the point of writing.

Daily blog & research articles

Published every day. In a category where you cannot advertise, being informative is the channel.

Google Ads

Education-first campaigns about the company and its capability. No video advertising.

What happens next

Launch Is 14 September 2026

Forty years in, the chocolate does not change. The position does.

Before this, Sophia’s Garden was a manufacturing plant. From 14 September it is a branded company with its own products, its own name on the box, and a direct line to the retailers who stock it.

We are not publishing results yet, because there are none to publish. Once the line is on shelves we will report what actually happened — shelf presence, trade enquiries through the portal, and search visibility for the terms a buyer uses — each with the window it was measured over. That is the same standard applied to every other number on this site.
