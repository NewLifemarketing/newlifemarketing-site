CRM Kingston

Built For The Busy Fortnight

A crm kingston project is worth buying for a specific reason in this city: when the year concentrates into a handful of weeks, the enquiries that get lost are lost in those weeks, and no amount of discipline survives a fortnight where everybody is already at capacity.

A spreadsheet and a good memory are entirely adequate for most of the year. They stop being adequate in the fortnight when enquiries arrive faster than anybody can answer them, and that is also the fortnight in which the year is decided.

Leads do not get lost loudly. Nobody announces that a quotation was never sent or a voicemail never returned; the enquiry simply stops, and six weeks later somebody notices the month was quieter than it should have been.

So the right way to size a crm kingston project is not by the average week. It is by the worst one: how many enquiries arrive, how many people are handling them, and what has to be true for none of them to go unanswered.

Built to that standard, the system is over-engineered for ten months and exactly right for the two that matter.

What a season does to a system

Built In February, Abandoned In July

A system is adopted in the quiet months and tested in the loud ones.

Anything needing a decision at the counter is the first thing to go when the queue is long. A dropdown with eleven options becomes whichever one is nearest the top, and a free-text field becomes empty.

So the test for a configuration is not whether it is complete. It is whether somebody could use it correctly while a customer waits, which rules out most of what gets designed in a planning meeting.

Design for the worst fortnight of the year and the rest of the year looks after itself. Design for a quiet Tuesday and the data stops being trustworthy exactly when you most need it to be.

The practical version is four fields, all of them obvious, none requiring a judgement call. Anything subtler belongs in a note somebody writes afterwards.

Before anything is bought

Write The Weekly Report First

One page, by hand, before a product is chosen. It is the fastest specification anybody has found.

Sketch the report you would want to read every Monday morning. Not a dashboard — a page, with the handful of numbers the business is actually run on and nothing else.

Every field the system needs is now visible, because each number on that page has to come from somewhere. Everything not on the page is a field somebody wanted rather than a field anybody needs, and those are what make a system unusable.

It also settles arguments in advance. Two people wanting different configurations usually want different reports, and that disagreement is much easier to resolve on paper than inside a half-built system.

An afternoon of this routinely removes a third of the intended scope, which is an afternoon that pays better than any week of the build.

Keep the page afterwards. When somebody proposes a new field six months in, the question is which number on that page it improves, and a field that improves none of them is a field somebody wanted rather than one the business needs. That single question prevents most of the bloat that makes a crm kingston build unusable by its second year.

Designing for the peak

What A CRM Kingston Build Should Do In The Busy Weeks

Three things that only matter when everybody is at capacity, which is when they matter most.

Acknowledge instantly. An automatic reply that names a person and a timeframe buys you hours of goodwill and costs nothing, and in a fortnight where a reply might otherwise take two days it is the difference between an enquiry that waits and one that goes elsewhere.

Make the next action visible without anybody asking. A list of what is overdue, by owner, that updates itself. In a quiet month people remember; in a busy one they do not, and the list is what stops a record from being quietly abandoned.

And make the hand-off cheap. When the person who took the enquiry is unavailable, the next person needs the context in one screen rather than in somebody’s head. That is a field-layout decision made at configuration time and it cannot be retrofitted during a busy week.

None of that is sophisticated. All of it is routinely missing from systems that cost a great deal.

Getting out again

A System You Cannot Leave Is Not One You Own

Worth settling on the day it goes in, because it is unarguable afterwards.

Ask for an export of your own data before anything is configured, and confirm it arrives in a form somebody else could read. Businesses that ask after a relationship sours discover the answer is a screen they can print.

The same applies to the automations. A plain-language list of what fires, when, and what it says is a document that transfers; a flowchart inside somebody else’s account is not.

None of this assumes things go wrong. It is that a supplier who agrees readily is telling you something useful about how they work, and one who resists is telling you something more useful still.

It costs nothing at the start. Asked for at the end, it is a negotiation conducted from the weaker position.

Our scope

A Short List, And The Season Is On It

What we take on, and the condition we would attach to taking it on here.

We build sales and marketing systems and run the advertising that feeds them, managing over $70,000 a month in spend for Ontario businesses with the owner on the media personally. The seam between the two is the part we are genuinely better at than a generalist.

Timing a crm kingston build around the season

A crm kingston build should go live in the quiet half of the year, never four weeks before the peak, because a system nobody has used yet meets its first real test at the worst possible moment. We would rather postpone a start date than hand over in May.

Outside that: no ERP work, no multi-site deployments, no regulated data environments. We are based in Minesing, a few hours west, which is a travel line for filming and makes no difference to a build.

Which system should we use?

Almost any of the major platforms will do what a business this size needs, so the question matters less than it feels. What matters more is which one your team will actually type into during a busy fortnight and what it connects to. We would rather spend the first week on stage definitions than on a product comparison.

We manage fine on a spreadsheet. Do we need this?

For ten months of the year, probably not. The question is what happens in the weeks when enquiries arrive faster than anybody can answer them, because that is when things get lost and it is also when the year is decided. Size the system by the worst week rather than the average one.

Can you fix the system we already have?

Usually, and it is nearly always cheaper than migrating. Most systems we are asked to replace are not broken; they are configured for a process the business has outgrown, with fields nobody fills in and stages nobody agreed. Reconfiguring around a re-decided process costs a fraction of a migration and keeps the history.

Will this tell us which marketing works?

Only if source capture is built deliberately, which it rarely is by default. The work is carrying the campaign and the page that produced an enquiry into the record and onto the report. In a market of 450 monthly searches, that field is the only evidence the numbers will ever give you.
