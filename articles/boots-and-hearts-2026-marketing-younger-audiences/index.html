Boots & Hearts 2026: What the Data Says About How Music Festivals Now Market to a Younger Crowd

Festival Marketing/Gen Z/Country Music

NewLife Research Posted on August 10, 2026

This analysis draws on data from 16 credible sources, including company results, large consumer surveys, industry and market-research reports, and news reporting.

Boots & Hearts, Canada's largest country music festival, wrapped its 2026 edition at Burl's Creek Event Grounds in Oro-Medonte on August 91,2. The single clearest signal in the data around events like it is this: the audience filling these fields is now majority young. Gen Z and millennials make up 63% of today's country music fan base, according to a 2025 Live Nation survey of country listeners across seven countries14. The festivals reaching that audience are not doing it with billboards. They are doing it with the audience's own phones.

Live Music Is Booming, and Canada Is Part of It

The backdrop is a live-music market at record highs. Live Nation reported 159 million fans across its concerts in 2025, up 5% year over year across roughly 55,000 shows, on record revenue of US$25.2 billion4. The global music festival market specifically was valued at US$2,158.2 million in 2024 and is projected to grow at a 24.0% compound annual rate through 2031, with North America the largest region at roughly 40% of that total5.

Canada is not a bystander. The Canadian Live Music Association's first-ever national benchmark, released in January 2025 with consultancy Nordicity, put the combined economic impact of live music and its tourism at $10.92 billion, supporting more than 101,640 jobs and $5.84 billion in labour income6. In 2023 alone, live music festivals and venue shows in Canada drew 19.69 million visitors6. The demand underneath that is real and measurable: global on-demand audio streams topped 5.1 trillion in 2025, up 9.6% year over year13.

The Country Audience Got Young, Fast

Country music is where this generational shift is sharpest, which matters for a festival like Boots & Hearts. In Live Nation's 2025 research on nearly 1,600 country listeners across the U.S., U.K., Germany, Sweden, Norway, Australia and New Zealand, two of every three fans fell into the Gen Z or millennial age brackets, for a combined 63% of the fan base14. Attendance at country shows has nearly doubled since 201914. Among Gen Z country listeners, 92% said they are attending the same number of country events or more than in past years14.

The fans themselves credit social platforms for the surge. In the same research, 46% said social media has made country more visible, and 91% said the genre is now reaching a wider audience than ever14.

For This Audience, Discovery and Attendance Are Social Acts

The reason phones matter more than billboards is that younger fans find and experience music inside social apps. Deloitte's 2024 Digital Media Trends survey found that 82% of Gen Z and 70% of millennials discover new artists or music through social media or user-generated-content video sites10. By contrast, only about 23% of all people surveyed, and roughly one-third of Gen Z and millennials, said they discover new music through streaming-service recommendations10. Around 75% of Gen Z and millennials have heard music in a video, show or game and then gone looking for that artist10.

Figure 3: How Younger Fans Discover New Music

Deloitte Digital Media Trends 2024. Source: 10

The scale of this behaviour is confirmed across the largest studies in the field. IFPI's Engaging With Music 2023, based on more than 43,000 respondents across 26 countries, found that among 16-to-24-year-olds, short-form video platforms such as TikTok are the single most popular way they engage with music every day, ahead of both audio and video streaming9. The platforms these fans live on are concentrated and knowable: Pew Research Center's 2025 data shows 80% of U.S. adults aged 18 to 29 use Instagram, and roughly half of that age group use TikTok every day16. In Canada specifically, DataReportal counted 33.0 million active social media identities as of October 2025, equal to 82.1% of the population, with Instagram reaching 21.0 million people and TikTok reaching 16.6 million adults15.

Crucially, posting is no longer something fans do after a show. It is part of why they attend. Live Nation's 2025 "Living for Live" report, a survey of 40,000 people across 15 countries, found 94% of fans post on social media at concerts, and that for half of Gen Z, posting is part of the reason they go in the first place7. After the show, 86% rewatch their own videos and 68% stream clips of events they could not attend7. Eventbrite's 2026 "Social Study" adds the forward-looking demand: 79% of 18-to-35-year-olds plan to attend more events in 20268.

Why Fan-Made Content, Not Ads, Is the Marketing Engine

If the audience is young, social-first, and posts as a reflex, the most efficient marketing a festival has is the content its own crowd produces. The trust data explains why this outperforms paid media. Nielsen's Trust in Advertising study found 88% of global respondents trust recommendations from people they know above any other channel, roughly 50% more than they trust lower-ranked formats like banner, mobile and SMS ads11. A friend's festival photo carries weight a paid ad cannot buy.

Live experiences also convert. Event Marketer's EventTrack 2026 research, surveying over 1,000 marketers and attendees, found 61% of consumers are more inclined to purchase after attending a live or experiential event12. Discovery itself is increasingly word-of-mouth: 69% of the 18-to-35 group in Eventbrite's study rely on personal networks to find experiences8. And the affinity is durable, with nearly 40% of Gen Z and millennial fans following their favourite artists directly on social media, a standing channel a festival's acts and hashtags plug straight into10.

Boots & Hearts and #BootsLife: The Local Case

Boots & Hearts is a textbook local example of the pattern the data describes. The festival, founded in 2012 and now held at Burl's Creek in Oro-Medonte, is billed as Canada's largest country music festival with a venue capacity of 45,000-plus1. Its 2026 edition ran August 7 to 9, weathering thunderstorms that closed festival operations on the middle day1,2.

Its most visible marketing asset is not an ad campaign. It is a hashtag. Boots & Hearts runs the #BootsLife user-generated-content campaign, explicitly asking attendees to post their country photos using the tag3. That is the exact mechanism the numbers reward: a young, country-leaning, phone-first crowd that already posts 94% of the time, turning its own weekend into a stream of trusted, peer-to-peer promotion for next year's event. The festival supplies the moment. The audience supplies the marketing.

For any business chasing the same demographic in this part of Ontario, the takeaway from the data is narrow and concrete. The audience is young, it is reachable on Instagram and TikTok, and it trusts other people far more than it trusts brands. The organizations winning that attention are the ones giving people something worth posting.
