How Much of the Web Still Fails Disabled Users? 20 Sources on Errors, Lawsuits and Lost Sales

Websites/Accessibility/Research

NewLife Research Posted on November 16, 2026

This analysis draws on data from 20 credible sources, including government statistics, legislation and regulatory findings, independent studies and industry reports.

In February 2026, an automated scan of the top one million home pages found detectable accessibility failures on 95.9% of them.1 That is worse than a year earlier, when the figure was 94.8%, and the average page now carries 56.1 errors, up 10.1% in a single year.1

The people those errors shut out are not a niche. Statistics agencies in Canada, the UK and Australia put the share of their populations living with a disability between 21% and 27%.5,8,9 And the cost of ignoring them is now measured in court filings. US federal website accessibility lawsuits rose 27% in 2025.3

Almost every home page fails, and the count is rising

WebAIM, a non-profit, has scanned the top million home pages every year since 2019. Its 2026 run found 56,114,377 distinct errors.1 Six kinds of failure account for 96% of them.1

Low-contrast text is the most common, on 83.9% of pages. Missing alternative text on images follows at 53.1%, then form inputs with no label at 51%, links with no text at 46.3%, buttons with no text at 30.6% and pages that do not declare their language at 13.5%.1

Seven years of the same scan show slow progress on some of these and none on others. Missing alt text fell from 68% of pages in 2019 to 53.1% in 2026. Missing document language fell from 33.1% to 13.5%. Empty buttons went the other way, rising from 25% to 30.6%, and low contrast jumped back to 83.9% in 2026 after three years of decline.1

Figure 1: Share of Top Million Home Pages With Each Failure, 2019 to 2026 (%)

Source: 1.

A second, much larger crawl lands in the same place. The HTTP Archive covers about 17 million sites a month.2 In its 2025 Web Almanac, only about 30% of sites passed the colour contrast audit, and about 25% of mobile form inputs had no accessible name at all.2 About 13 to 14% of images carried no alt attribute, and only about 24% of pages had a skip link that lets a keyboard user jump past the navigation.2

The two scans disagree on how bad things look at a glance. The median Lighthouse accessibility score was 85% in 2025, up one point from 2024.2 A score of 85 sounds like a pass. WebAIM's count says nearly every page has at least one failure. Both can be true, because a score averages many checks while a single unlabelled checkout field is enough to stop a screen reader user from paying.

These are floors, not ceilings. Ireland's National Disability Authority notes that the automated axe-core engine it uses purports to detect up to 57% of WCAG 2.1 AA errors.14 Whatever an automated scan finds, a manual audit finds more.

Manual audits find the same thing

Government testers in two countries have now checked sites by hand as well as by machine, and the results match the crawls.

The UK Government Digital Service monitored 1,203 public sector websites and 21 apps between January 2022 and September 2024.13 Only 10 of 1,151 simplified tests found no issues. None of the 52 sites tested in detail, and none of the 21 apps, was fully compliant when first checked.13 Testers logged 29,787 issues. Colour contrast failed on 77.3% of simplified-tested sites and visible keyboard focus on 76.3%.13

The encouraging number in the same report is what happened next. Organisations fixed 16,482 of those issues, 55.3%, once they were told about them.13

Ireland's monitoring for 2024 covered 232 automated reviews, 23 in-depth website reviews and eight in-depth app reviews.14 In the in-depth reviews, the largest share of errors, 45%, fell under "Name, Role, Value", the rule that buttons, menus and form controls must tell assistive technology what they are and what they do.14 And 66.7% of websites and 100% of apps reviewed in depth had no accessibility statement that met the EU Directive.14

Users notice the same pattern. In WebAIM's survey of 1,539 screen reader users, 46.8% said web accessibility had not changed over the previous year and 18.6% said it had got worse. Only 34.6% saw improvement.12 CAPTCHA was again the most problematic item, followed by menus, tabs and dialogs that do not behave as expected.12 That matches Ireland's finding almost exactly: the failures that hurt most sit in the interactive parts of a page.

One in four adults, in every market measured

The audience these sites fail is large, and in every country with a recent survey it is growing.

Statistics Canada found that 27% of Canadians aged 15 and over, 8.0 million people, had a disability in 2022. That was up five points from 22% in 2017.5 The fastest growth was among youth aged 15 to 24, from 13% to 20%.5

In the UK, 16.8 million people, 25%, were disabled in 2023 to 2024, up from 11.9 million (19%) a decade earlier.8 In Australia, 5.5 million people, 21.4%, had a disability in 2022, up from 17.7% in 2018.9 The CDC puts the US figure at more than one in four adults.6 Worldwide, the WHO estimates 1.3 billion people, one in six, live with significant disability.7

Figure 2: Share of the Population With a Disability, Latest National Survey (%)

Source: 5, 8, 9, 7.

Not all of those disabilities affect how someone uses a website, and the definitions differ by country. The CDC breakdown shows which ones most often do. Among US adults, 13.9% have a cognitive disability, 12.2% a mobility disability, 6.2% a hearing disability and 5.5% a vision disability.6 Cognition and mobility, not vision, are the largest groups, which is why keyboard access and clear, predictable interfaces matter as much as screen reader support.

Disabled people are online, and on their phones

The assumption that disabled customers are not online does not survive the data either. In the EU in 2024, 78.2% of people aged 16 to 74 with a severe disability used the internet at least weekly, against 93.5% of people with no disability.10 Among 16 to 24 year olds, the figures were 87.4% and 98.7%.10

Figure 3: Regular Internet Use in the EU by Level of Disability, 2024 (%)

Source: 10.

The US picture is older but points the same way. In Pew Research Center's 2021 survey, 75% of adults with a disability went online daily, against 87% of those without.11 Smartphone ownership was 72% against 88%.11 Among screen reader users specifically, 91.3% use one on a mobile device.12 A site that has only been checked on a desktop has not been checked for most of the people this article is about.

The lawsuit count is rising again

The clearest measured cost is legal, and the US data is the most complete.

Seyfarth Shaw, which has tracked federal website accessibility cases since 2017, counted 3,117 filed in 2025. That is up 665 cases, or 27%, from 2,452 in 2024, and it reversed two years of decline.3 Website cases made up 36% of all 8,667 ADA Title III federal filings in 2025, up from 28% the year before.3

Figure 4: US Federal Website Accessibility Lawsuits per Year, 2017 to 2025

Source: 3.

Federal courts are only part of it. UsableNet, which also counts New York and California state courts, recorded 5,114 digital accessibility lawsuits between January 1 and December 15, 2025, with 1,919 of them, 38%, in those two state court systems.4 The geography also moved. Florida federal filings roughly doubled, from 470 in 2024 to 961 in 2025, second only to New York at 1,021.3

Two numbers in UsableNet's count matter most to a business deciding whether this applies to them. 70% of 2025 filings were against e-commerce, and 64% targeted companies with under $25 million in revenue.4 1,427 lawsuits were filed against companies that had already been sued before.4 This is not a large-enterprise problem, and settling once does not end it.

Nor does a plug-in. In April 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission finalised a $1 million order against accessiBe, a widget vendor the FTC said had claimed its product "can make any website compliant" with WCAG.18 The order bars that claim without evidence. The HTTP Archive found overlays like it on about 2.1% of desktop pages, but on only 0.2% of the top 1,000 sites.2

The rules now cover most of the places a Canadian business sells

For an Ontario business, the obligation is not new. Since January 1, 2021, every designated public sector organization and every organization with 50 or more employees in Ontario has had to make its public website and web content conform with WCAG 2.0 Level AA, with exceptions only for live captions and pre-recorded audio description.15 On conviction, the Accessibility for Ontarians with Disabilities Act allows fines of up to $100,000 for each day a corporation's offence continues.16

Set that against the scans. If 95.9% of the top million home pages fail automated checks,1 and the UK found no detailed-tested public site fully compliant,13 there is little reason to think Ontario's private sector websites are an exception. No source in this set measures their compliance directly.

Selling across borders adds more rules on the same timeline. The European Accessibility Act, Directive (EU) 2019/882, took effect on 28 June 2025 and covers e-commerce and consumer banking services.19 In the US, the Department of Justice set WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the standard for state and local government websites, with compliance now due on April 26, 2027 for governments serving 50,000 or more people and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones.17 Every one of these rules points at the same WCAG standard the scans above measure against.

What it costs in sales

The commercial cost is the least measured part of the picture. The only direct estimate in this set comes from the UK. The Click-Away Pound survey put the spending lost when disabled shoppers abandoned inaccessible websites at £17.1 billion in 2019, up from £11.75 billion in its 2016 survey, when more than 4 million people reported leaving a retail site because of barriers.20

That figure is seven years old and UK only, and nobody has published a comparable Canadian estimate. What the other sources do establish is its scale. One in four adults reports a disability,5,6,8 most of them are regular internet users,10 and almost every page they land on has a barrier an automated tool can find.1

The data also says these barriers are fixable. The six failure types that make up 96% of detected errors are contrast, alt text, labels, empty links, empty buttons and page language,1 and UK public bodies fixed more than half of their logged issues once someone showed them where they were.13

The data has limits. Automated scans miss most of what a manual audit finds, so the WebAIM and HTTP Archive figures understate the problem.14 The UK and Irish manual audits cover public sector sites, not businesses. The lawsuit trackers are US only, and the two count different courts. The national disability surveys use different definitions and age ranges, so their levels should be compared loosely. And the US internet-use figures date from 2021.
