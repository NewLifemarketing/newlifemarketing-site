Do Online Reviews Actually Move Revenue? Here's What the Data Shows

Local SEO/Customer Reviews/Conversion Data

NewLife Research Posted on September 21, 2026

This analysis draws on data from 19 credible sources, including academic and working-paper studies, real-world platform network data, and industry benchmark surveys.

A one-star increase in a restaurant's Yelp rating causes a 5-9% increase in revenue, independent of the restaurant's actual quality5. That number comes not from a survey but from a Harvard Business School study that used Yelp's own rounding rules (3.24 stars displays as 3.0, 3.25 displays as 3.5) to isolate the causal effect of the displayed rating from everything else about the restaurant5. It is one of the few studies in this space that can say "causes" instead of "correlates with," and it is the starting point for a much larger body of data on what reviews are actually worth to a business, not just what consumers say about them.

The Revenue Case: Count Beats Star Rating

Star rating gets most of the attention, but the data on review count is arguably more consistent. An analysis of 200,000 US small businesses found that businesses with an above-average review count earned 54% more annual revenue than businesses below average, and businesses with 200 or more reviews earned more than double the revenue of an average business3. Businesses that generated 25 or more fresh reviews in a 90-day window earned 108% more than average3.

Figure 1: Revenue Index by Review Volume

Indexed to 100 for businesses with a below-average review count. Source: 3

The same analysis found something counterintuitive: revenue peaked for businesses averaging 3.5-4.5 stars, not a perfect 5.03. That is not a fluke of one dataset. It shows up again in ecommerce, at the level of individual products, which is the next section.

Where the Conversion Lift Actually Comes From

At the product-page level, the effect of reviews is front-loaded. A single review on a product page produces a 10% lift in orders, 50 reviews produces a 30% lift, and 100 reviews produces a 37% lift, according to Bazaarvoice's analysis of its retailer network11. Northwestern University's Spiegel Research Center, working with review platform PowerReviews, found the jump from zero reviews to five is the single biggest move: purchase likelihood for a product with five reviews is 270% greater than for the same product with none4. After that, the marginal benefit of each additional review drops off quickly4.

Figure 2: Order Lift by Product Review Count

Percentage lift in orders versus an identical product page with zero reviews. Source: 11

Price matters too. The same Spiegel Research Center study found that displaying reviews lifted conversion 190% for lower-priced products but 380% for higher-priced ones4. Reviews do more work, not less, the more a customer has on the line. Products rated four stars or higher with over 100 reviews convert at 45% in retail settings, per Bazaarvoice's network data11. On the demand side, Yotpo's 2022 survey of 1,500 shoppers found 61% say user-generated content, including reviews, "always" influences their purchase decisions, and 85% say they prefer to see between 50 and 500 reviews on a product12. Thirty percent say a product with zero reviews actively deters them from buying it12.

The Star-Rating Sweet Spot Isn't 5.0

Across several independent datasets, a perfect rating underperforms a strong-but-imperfect one. PowerReviews' analysis of more than 20 million product pages found the optimal conversion range sits at 4.75-4.99 stars, and that products with a perfect 5.0 convert at a rate comparable to products rated only 3.0-3.496. Forty-six percent of consumers say they're suspicious of a perfect rating, rising to 53% among Gen Z shoppers6. The Spiegel Research Center's number is narrower but points the same direction: purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0-4.7 stars and actually declines as ratings approach 5.04.

Figure 3: Indexed Conversion Rate by Star-Rating Band

Indexed to 100 for the lowest rating band. PowerReviews found conversion rates fall again as ratings approach a perfect 5.0. Source: 6

Canadian consumers make the count-versus-rating tradeoff explicit: 74% said they'd rather buy from a business with 15 reviews averaging 4 stars than one with 5 reviews averaging a perfect 5, in Capterra's survey of 608 Canadian consumers14. Fifty-seven percent of that same group read between two and five reviews before deciding, and reviews were most influential for electronics purchases (56%) and travel bookings (50%)14. Consumers filtering by star rating on PowerReviews' network convert at 111.8% the rate of visitors who don't interact with reviews at all, and shoppers who deliberately seek out one-star reviews before buying convert at 108.8% that rate6. Checking the bad reviews is, itself, a buying signal.

How Many People Are Actually Reading Reviews, and Where

The reading habit keeps climbing. In BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey, the share of US consumers who say they "always" read reviews when browsing for a local business rose from 29% in 2025 to 41% in 202612. By 2026, 97% of consumers said they read reviews for local businesses at all, and 85% said they're more likely to use a business after reading positive reviews about it2. Forty-seven percent won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 31% now require a rating of at least 4.5 stars, up from 17% just a year earlier2.

Figure 4: Consumers Who "Always" Read Reviews Before Choosing a Local Business

Year-over-year change in the same annual BrightLocal consumer survey question. Sources: 1, 2

Trust in reviews isn't uniform, though. BrightLocal's 2025 wave found only 42% of consumers now trust reviews as much as a personal recommendation, down sharply from 79% in 20201. That decline lines up with a broader, older pattern: Nielsen's Global Trust in Advertising survey, covering more than 28,000 respondents across 56 countries, found online consumer reviews were the second most trusted source of brand information behind word-of-mouth from people consumers actually know17. Reviews still outperform every form of paid advertising on trust, they just no longer sit at parity with a friend's recommendation.

Where people are reading matters too. Google still leads by a wide margin, but it's no longer alone: 63.6% of consumers check a business's Google reviews before ever visiting it, and Google itself carries 57.5% of all reviews online, with 88% concentrated on just four platforms7. AI tools are the fastest-growing new channel: consumer use of ChatGPT and similar tools to find local business reviews jumped from 6% in 2025 to 45% in 2026, making them the third most-used review source behind Google and Facebook2.

Responding to Reviews Pays, and Almost Nobody Does It

The revenue case for responding to reviews is as strong as the case for collecting them. Businesses that responded to at least 25% of their reviews earned 35% more revenue than average, and businesses replying to at least 50% earned more than $166,000 more annually than non-responders, in Womply's analysis3. Yet 75% of the small businesses in that same dataset didn't respond to reviews at all3. A direct survey of more than 300 small business owners found a similar gap: 86% respond at least sometimes, but only 37% say they always do, and 14% never respond10. Of those who do respond, 81% respond to both positive and negative reviews, and 83% respond within a few days or faster10.

Consumers notice. Eighty percent of BrightLocal's 2026 respondents said they're more likely to use a business that responds to all of its reviews, and 89% expect that response to come from the business owner directly, not a template2. On the platform side, Birdeye's aggregate data across its own client base shows the response rate climbing too: 75.5% of reviews received a business response in 2025, up from 73% the year before, alongside 30.7% year-over-year growth in review volume16. Birdeye's data also puts a number on what a single review is worth in foot traffic: each additional Google review is associated with roughly 80 additional website visits, 63 direction requests, and 16 phone calls19.

Reviews Carry Weight in B2B, Not Just Local Retail

The pattern isn't confined to restaurants and ecommerce. In a benchmark survey of 548 B2B buyers spanning small businesses to enterprise, 92% said they were more likely to purchase software or services after reading a trusted review15. Sixty-seven percent specifically wanted to see a mix of both positive and negative reviews, not just positive ones, before deciding15. That is the same "too-perfect-looks-fake" pattern showing up again, this time among professional buyers evaluating enterprise software rather than consumers buying a product online.

Why Local Search Rewards Reviews Independently of Revenue

Reviews also do work before a customer ever reaches a business's website. In Whitespark's 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors survey of 47 local-search experts scoring 187 factors, review signals and behavioral signals showed the largest year-over-year increase in importance to local rankings8. A high numerical Google rating ranked as the sixth most influential Local Pack factor overall, and the quantity of native Google reviews with text ranked ninth8. Notably, "sustained influx of reviews over time" ranked ahead of a one-time volume of reviews8. That lines up with Womply's revenue data on 25+ fresh reviews in a rolling 90-day window rather than a fixed lifetime total.

The Fake-Review Problem Is Now a Measurable Cost

Reviews only move revenue because customers trust them, which is exactly what fake reviews erode. An analysis of 4 million local business reviews across Google, Facebook, Yelp, and Tripadvisor found Google carried the highest rate of inauthentic reviews, at 10.7%, with the locksmith category worst-affected at 14.5%9. Sixty-seven percent of consumers say they're concerned about review fraud9.

The clearest evidence that this isn't just a perception problem comes from a National Bureau of Economic Research field experiment: 10,000 UK respondents chose among equally-priced products of varying real quality on an Amazon-like platform, and exposure to fake positive reviews for an inferior product measurably shifted consumers toward the worse choice18. The researchers estimated the resulting welfare loss at roughly $0.12 for every dollar spent in the experiment18. A brief educational intervention teaching people to spot fake reviews cut that harm by 44%18. That is evidence the damage from fake reviews is real, but not fixed.

Advertisers get a version of the same trust transfer when they borrow a review platform's credibility. In a controlled test of 20 real advertisements against a survey of 1,000 US consumers, Trustpilot found 66% of consumers say they're "often" or "very often" influenced by customer reviews during a purchase journey, and ads that combined a review-platform logo with a star rating were 2.5 times more persuasive than ads without either13. An ad citing 3,000+ reviews outperformed an otherwise-identical ad citing no review count at all by 33%13.

What the Data Adds Up To

None of these datasets agree on an exact number: one puts the ideal rating at 3.5-4.5 stars, another at 4.0-4.7, another at 4.75-4.99. That spread is itself informative: it means there is no single magic rating to chase, and no dataset here supports optimizing for a perfect 5.0. What the sources agree on is direction. Review count and response rate track revenue at least as strongly as star rating does. The biggest conversion jump happens in the first handful of reviews. And the businesses seeing the upside aren't the ones with the highest rating; they're the ones with the most reviews, the most recent reviews, and the most responses.
