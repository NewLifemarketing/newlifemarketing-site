Dryden Website Design: Is A Site Worth It At All?

In a town of 7,388 that question deserves a real answer rather than an assumption.

112.7

A social page is free, already has your photographs on it, and is where a good share of the town already looks. For a business serving only local customers who all know each other, that is a defensible answer and nobody should pretend otherwise.

Three things change it. If customers come from outside the town, a social page is a weak and inconsistent shop window. If you sell something that needs explaining, there is nowhere to explain it. And if the platform changes its rules, you have no address of your own.

That third point is the one most worth taking seriously. A business whose entire presence sits on somebody else’s platform is renting its own front door, and the rent terms change without notice.

So the sensible version is not an expensive site. It is a small, correct, owned one, with the social page continuing to do what it already does well.

What a small site has to do

Four Jobs, And Nothing Else

A site in a market this size earns its cost by doing these and not by looking impressive.

Say what you do and where you go

Plainly, in the first screen. For a customer an hour out, the travel question is the one that decides whether they call.

Load quickly on a phone with a weak signal

The realistic reader is not on office broadband. Every large image and tracking script is a delay paid for by somebody standing outside with one bar.

Make contact trivial

A tappable number, hours that are current, and a short form that reaches somebody who reads it. This is where most small-town sites lose enquiries they had already earned.

Explain the thing that needs explaining

The service people ask about, the part nobody understands, the reason your price differs. A social page has nowhere to put this and it is frequently the whole reason to have a site.

Four jobs, no more. A site in a town this size that tries to do more is usually paying for pages nobody will ever read.

When something is missing

The Error Page Can Still Save The Visit

Every site has one and almost nobody has looked at theirs.

Somebody follows an old link, mistypes an address, or arrives from a printed leaflet with a page that no longer exists. What they see next decides whether they leave or carry on.

The default is a blank apology. What works instead is a short line admitting the page has gone, the phone number, and links to the three or four pages most people actually want.

It takes twenty minutes to build and it is permanently useful, because old links keep arriving for years after whatever created them.

Check how often it is being reached, too. A sudden rise means a redirect was missed somewhere, and that page is frequently the only place that symptom is visible.

Who cannot use it

Who Cannot Use Your Site Today, And What Dryden Website Design Can Fix For Nothing

A meaningful share of the people reading any site are doing it in conditions the person who approved it never tried.

Somebody with a tremor cannot hit a small link. Somebody reading in bright sun cannot see pale grey text on white. Somebody with a screen reader hears your photographs described, or hears nothing at all. Somebody who enlarges the text finds a layout that falls apart at 150%.

Most of the fixes cost nothing because they are decisions rather than work. Text dark enough to read outdoors. Links and buttons big enough to hit with a thumb. A description written for every image that carries information, and none at all for the ones that are decoration. A form where every field says what it wants above it rather than inside it, because the label inside disappears the moment somebody starts typing.

Headings in order, as an outline rather than as font sizes, so the page can be skimmed by somebody who cannot see it. That one is free and almost universally got wrong.

This is not a compliance exercise and it is not charity. It is the same list of fixes that makes a site usable on a phone, outdoors, on a bad connection — which is how most of your visitors will read it anyway.

The old site

Keep A Copy Of What You Are Replacing

Five minutes on launch day that occasionally saves a week.

Before the switch, take a complete copy of the existing site — the pages, the images, the text — and put it somewhere outside the hosting account. A folder on a drive is enough.

Things are discovered missing afterwards: a photograph nobody has the original of, a page of technical detail everybody forgot, a form nobody realised was being used. Recovering those from a deleted site ranges from awkward to impossible.

Keep the old hosting running for a month after launch if it costs little. It is the cheapest insurance available in the whole project.

And export anything the old site was storing — past enquiries, subscriber lists, bookings — before anybody cancels anything. That data is rarely in the plan and is gone the moment the account closes.

Write down what you took and where you put it. A backup nobody can find is the same as no backup, and the person who made it is usually the only one who knows, which lasts exactly as long as they do.

What appears in the result

What Dryden Website Design Should Write For The Search Listing

Two lines per page that are seen far more often than the page itself.

The title and the description under it are what somebody reads before deciding whether to click. They are written once per page, they take a minute each, and on most small sites they are left at whatever the system generated.

A good title says what the page is and where you are, in that order, without the company name taking the first half. A good description says what somebody will get and gives a reason to choose you over the entry above.

They are also the one piece of writing on a site that can be improved without touching the design, the build or anybody’s schedule.

Do the five pages that matter rather than all forty. The front page, the two or three main services, and the contact page account for most of what anybody ever sees.

Then look at them as a set. Five titles that all begin with the company name are five entries a reader cannot tell apart, and the fix is a few minutes of editing rather than anything structural.

Straight answers

Dryden Website Design FAQ

Straight answers before you ever book a call.

Do we actually need a website?

Not always, and it is worth answering honestly. If every customer is local and finds you through a social page that works, a site may be a cost without a return. Three things change that: customers from outside the town, something that needs explaining properly, and not wanting your entire presence on a platform whose rules change without notice.

What should a small site actually do?

Four things: say what you do and how far you go, load quickly on a phone with a weak signal, make contact trivial, and explain the thing people always ask about. A site in a town this size that tries to do more is usually paying for pages nobody will read.

What if our web designer disappears?

It is the realistic risk when the nearest alternative is hours away. Hosting in your company’s name with your own payment method, the domain registered to you, administrative access you hold, and the source or platform account in your control. All four cost nothing to arrange at the start.

How do we stop it going stale?

Insist that somebody who works there can change text and add a page without emailing anybody, and that they are shown how before the project ends. A site nobody can edit does not get updated; it gets abandoned, and an abandoned site damages the impression it was built to create.
