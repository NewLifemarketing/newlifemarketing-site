Kenora Social Media

The Audience Outside Town

Kenora Social Media is usually sold as a way to talk to the town, which is the audience that already knows you — the district holds 66,000 people against the town’s 14,967, and the ones furthest out are reachable by almost nothing else.

Who is worth reaching

Kenora Social Media: The Town Already Knows You

Two census figures, and the gap between them is the whole argument.

Inside a town of 14,967, a business with a good reputation is already named at every counter. Posting to an audience that is mostly made of those same people is pleasant and it changes very little.

The part of the market that is genuinely unreached lives outside town, in an area that grew 11.7% while the town itself fell 0.9%. Those people have fewer overlapping errands, a smaller local circle, and almost no chance of hearing your name by accident.

A paid audience reaches them at the same cost per person as somebody downtown, which is the one genuinely unusual property of this channel in a district shaped like this one.

So the question to put to any supplier is narrow: what share of this budget reaches somebody who does not already follow us, and where are they.

The three products

Posting, Strategy And Distribution Are Separate

Quotes rarely say which is included, and in a market this small the difference is stark.

Posting

Somebody makes and schedules content. It reaches the people who already follow you, which here is largely the town, and it is the most commonly sold of the three.

Strategy

Deciding what is worth saying, to whom and how far out. Cheap, occasional, and what makes the other two worth anything at all.

Distribution

Money spent reaching people who do not follow you. In this district that is the whole point, and it is the line that quietly shrinks when a blended monthly fee is squeezed.

Who should speak

In A Town This Size A Borrowed Voice Is Audible

People follow people, and where everybody knows somebody that is not a slogan.

An account that reads as a person does the same job as the word of mouth that already carries you. A polished corporate page does a different and weaker one, and it is the version easiest for a supplier to sell.

That has an awkward consequence: the highest-return part cannot be outsourced. Nobody else can be you in public, and a borrowed voice is detectable in a place where people actually know each other.

What can be outsourced is everything around it — the filming, the editing, the paid distribution, the measurement, and the decision about what is worth saying. Those are the parts most owners have neither the time nor the equipment for.

A workable arrangement costs almost nothing: the owner records a voice note or sends three photographs from a job, somebody else turns it into the post, and the owner reads it before it goes out. The voice stays theirs and the hour disappears.

What it runs on

Stock Photography Is The Expensive Option

It costs less and performs worse, which is the worst combination available.

Every supplier has the same stock library. What almost none of their clients have is footage of the actual work: the job going in, the finished result, the person who answers the phone.

In a district where people recognise the lake, the road and the building, stock is not neutral. It is a small specific moment of distrust at exactly the point somebody is deciding whether you are a real business or a category.

Real material also lasts longer, and that matters here because the audience is small enough that a piece of creative gets seen repeatedly within weeks. A library is what lets a campaign keep running without people learning to scroll past you.

One planned shooting day produces months of material. A posting retainer with no production budget attached is an arrangement for distributing material that does not exist.

There is a cheaper half to this that most suppliers will not mention, because it is not billable. A phone plus a clip microphone, in the hands of somebody who is already on the job, captures the finished install, the unexpected problem and the thing that happened on a Tuesday — which is most of what an audience outside town wants to see. The crew is for what needs lighting and planning; everything else only needs somebody to be there and to press record before deciding whether it is worth it.

Measurement

What A Kenora Social Media Report Should Contain

At this scale the platform numbers are decoration and the oldest question is the best instrument.

Engagement rate is a percentage of an audience the platform chose. Reach is purchasable, so a reach figure with no spend beside it says nothing. Followers are the least predictive number on the page, and in a town this size the follower count is mostly people who were always going to know you.

What works is cost per enquiry, plus asking every arriving customer how they heard of you and where they drove from. That second column is the only evidence of whether the money is reaching past the town.

Read it over a rolling quarter. At these volumes one month cannot distinguish a good month from a bad one, which is how useful campaigns get cut and lucky ones get extended.

And watch for fatigue specifically: a rising cost per result with nothing changed in the account is the audience having seen it, not the campaign failing. Plan for it rather than react to it: decide at the start how many distinct pieces a quarter needs, and hold some back instead of opening with everything you have.

Distribution, And The Material To Feed It

A short offer, honestly bounded.

We run paid social against cost per enquiry and film the material it runs on. We manage over $70,000 a month in advertising spend for Ontario businesses and the owner runs the paid media personally.

What we would not take on in kenora social media

Kenora Social Media work that means writing in the owner’s voice is the part that cannot be outsourced honestly, and we would not take it. In a town where people know each other, a borrowed voice is detectable and it costs more than it buys.

We are in Minesing, a long way south, so filming here involves travel, which we price as its own line. We do not sell daily posting retainers, community management or inbox monitoring.

Everyone here already knows us. Why pay for this?

Because the town is the smaller half. The district holds 66,000 people against the town’s 14,967, and the area immediately outside grew 11.7% while the town fell 0.9%. Those people have almost no chance of hearing your name by accident, and a paid audience reaches them at the same cost per person as somebody downtown.

What should we ask a supplier?

What share of this budget reaches somebody who does not already follow us, and where are they. If the answer is none, the campaign is a newsletter with worse delivery: engagement will rise, enquiries will not move, and nothing in the report will explain why.

Should the account be the business or the owner?

Usually the owner, or at least a voice that reads as a person. Word of mouth is the dominant channel in a town this size and an account that sounds like it does the same job. A workable arrangement is the owner recording a voice note and somebody else turning it into the post, with the owner reading it before it goes out.

How often should we post?

As often as there is something worth showing, which is less than a daily schedule implies. Two posts a week of real work beats five of stock graphics, because in an audience this size people see creative repeatedly and learn to scroll past. Spend the difference on reaching the district.
