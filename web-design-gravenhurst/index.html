Web Design Gravenhurst

Two Readers, One Screen

Web Design Gravenhurst has to satisfy two readers who could not be less alike: a person in a car deciding in ninety seconds whether to pull in, and a person in another city deciding a week ahead whether the trip is worth making at all.

The two readers

The driver wants four facts in the first screen and nothing else: are you open, where exactly, is it quick, and can they call. They are on a phone, possibly moving, and anything that takes a second scroll has lost them.

The planner wants the opposite: detail, photographs, what the experience involves, whether to book, what it costs, and whether it justifies an hour in the car. They will read for several minutes if the page gives them something to read.

A site built only for the planner buries the four facts under a gallery. A site built only for the driver gives somebody planning a trip nothing to decide on. Most small-town sites manage to do both wrong.

The arrangement that works is unglamorous: the four facts at the top, always visible, with the depth beneath them. Nobody has to choose, and neither reader has to scroll past the other’s content.

The top of the page

Four Facts That Belong Above Everything

All four are text, and most sites hide at least two of them.

Open now, and today’s hours

Including seasonal changes, and identical to the profile. A contradiction between the two is the most expensive error available in a town people drive through.

Where, in words a driver understands

A landmark and a side of the road beats a postcode. Somebody deciding at speed is matching what they can see.

A tappable phone number

Not an image, not behind a contact tab. For a decision made in a car this is the conversion.

What it costs, roughly

A range with the reason it varies. It serves the planner and it stops the driver pulling in to ask a question you could have answered.

Four blocks of text, no design decisions, and they are the difference between a site that collects visits and one that collects customers. They are also the blocks most likely to be screenshotted and sent to whoever else is in the car, which is how a lot of decisions in this catchment actually get made.

What goes underneath

Depth For The Person Planning A Trip

Below the fold, the page stops being a sign and starts being an argument.

Photographs of the actual place rather than of the region. Everybody here has access to the same water and the same sunset; a visitor scrolling past sees somewhere nice and no reason to choose anybody in particular.

What you do, described specifically enough that somebody can picture their own visit. How long it takes, whether to book, what to bring, and what happens if the weather turns.

And the questions people ask on the telephone, answered in writing. Those are the most-read paragraphs on most sites in a town like this and the ones least likely to have been written.

None of that needs a bigger budget; it needs somebody to write down what is already said out loud twenty times a week.

The practical way to collect it costs nothing. For one month, every time somebody telephones with a question, write the question down rather than only answering it. At the end of that month you will have a page plan drawn entirely from what real customers in this catchment wanted to know, and it will be better than anything a supplier could have guessed at from a brief.

How it has to perform

Fast, Or It Is Not Read At All

Both readers are on phones and one of them is in a moving vehicle.

Every large image, web font and tracking script is a delay, and in this catchment it is a delay paid for by somebody with a weak signal who has another option two minutes up the road.

It is the cheapest thing here to get right and the most commonly ignored, because it is invisible on the machine the site was designed on.

Test it the way a customer does: open the site on a phone away from the office, time how long before anything is readable, and try to call without pinching or scrolling.

Then submit the form and check it reaches somebody who reads it, rather than a mailbox set up by a person who has since left. In a seasonal business that failure can run a whole year before anybody wonders why the summer was quiet.

What sets the price

What Moves A Web Design Gravenhurst Quote

Two quotes that read identically can differ by a factor of three. These are what do it.

How much is bespoke

Every element designed from scratch, against a design system applied consistently. The second is cheaper because the decisions were made once, and on a small site it launches faster.

Who writes the words

Content is the commonest cause of a delayed launch and is almost never in the quote. A built site waiting on copy is money already spent and earning nothing.

What it connects to

A form that emails somebody is trivial. A booking system, a payment, a record created in something else: those are integrations, and integrations are where small estimates break.

What happens after launch

Hosting, platform updates, security patches and somebody to call. A quote with no line for this ends on the day the site starts needing attention.

Our scope

What We Build And What We Would Hand On

Narrow on purpose, so a wasted meeting is avoided.

We build and rebuild presentation sites with the structure decided against the searches each page is meant to own, the redirects written before launch, and the content planned as part of the project rather than chased afterwards.

What web design gravenhurst work should leave behind

Web Design Gravenhurst work with us leaves you able to change the top four facts yourself without emailing anybody, which in a seasonal business is the difference between hours that are current and hours that are from last August. The registry of which page owns which search and the redirect map come with it.

We do not take on open-ended application development or booking platforms built from scratch. Where something has to compute or take payment, that is a specialist job with a different commercial model, and naming it now is cheaper than discovering it in month three.

What should be at the top of the page?

Four facts: whether you are open today, where you are in terms a driver can recognise, a tappable phone number, and roughly what things cost. They serve the person deciding in the car and they stop the planner having to hunt. Everything else belongs underneath.

What goes below the fold?

The detail a planner needs: photographs of the actual place rather than the region, what you do described specifically, how long it takes, whether to book, and the questions people ask on the telephone answered in writing. That last set is the most-read and least-written material on most sites here.

Why does speed matter so much here?

Because both readers are on phones and one of them has another option two minutes up the road. Every large image and script is a delay paid for by somebody with a weak signal, and it is invisible on the machine the site was designed on. Test it on a phone away from the office.

What should we be able to change ourselves?

The hours, at minimum, and ideally the four facts at the top. In a seasonal business the difference between a site that works and one that misleads is whether somebody can update opening times in two minutes without emailing a supplier.
