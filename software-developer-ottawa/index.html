Software Developer Ottawa

Competing For Attention

Finding a software developer ottawa is not a supply problem — 63,400+ people work in the sector here — it is an attention problem, because a small project competes for the same people as 540 companies in Kanata North alone, and the way you scope it decides whether anybody good calls back.

An enquiry that says we need a system built, without a boundary, a budget range or a decision date, costs a developer an hour to scope and has maybe a one-in-five chance of becoming work. In a market with this much demand, that hour goes elsewhere.

The enquiries that get answered are the ones that make the first hour cheap: here is the thing that is slow, here is roughly what the manual version costs us, here is the range we have in mind, here is who decides and by when.

None of that requires knowing what to build. It requires knowing what the problem costs, which is information only you have and which most briefs leave out entirely.

That single change — stating the cost of the problem rather than the shape of the solution — reliably improves both the number of replies and the quality of what comes back.

The half nobody quotes

Moving The Old Data Is The Expensive Part

The build is estimated carefully. The migration is assumed to be an afternoon.

Whatever the new system replaces contains years of records entered by different people under different conventions. Addresses in four formats, a field that meant one thing until 2022, duplicates nobody ever merged, and a column somebody repurposed for notes.

Moving that is not a technical task, it is a decision task, and the decisions belong to you: which records come across, which get archived, what happens to the ones that are plainly wrong, and who rules on the ambiguous ones.

Budget it as its own line with its own hours. Projects that go badly over are far more often undone by this than by anything to do with the software itself, and it is the part a demo never touches.

The useful shortcut is to migrate less. Bring across what is live and what the last two years need, and keep the old system readable for the rest. That decision alone routinely halves the work.

What finished means

Agree How You Will Know It Works

Written before the build, in your words, describing outcomes rather than features.

Not a feature list. A short set of sentences describing things somebody will do with it: a new job is entered in under a minute, last month’s figures come out matching the accounts, a person who has never seen it can complete the common task without being shown.

Each of those is checkable by you rather than by the developer, which is the whole point. A feature list is checked by the person who built it, and it is always complete.

Write them before work starts and they double as the scope. Anything not serving one of them is a change request rather than an omission, and that distinction is where most project disputes actually live.

Who runs the checks matters as much. Somebody who does the job daily, on real data, for a week, before the old process is switched off — and with the authority to say it is not ready.

Where the value usually is

What A Software Developer Ottawa Should Fix First

The highest-return project in most businesses is not new software.

The quote lives in one system, the job in another, the invoice in a third, and somebody retypes the same five fields three times. Nothing is broken and everything takes longer than it should.

Joining those is usually days rather than months, costs far less than any of the systems it connects, and removes the step most likely to introduce an error. It is also unglamorous, which is why it rarely gets proposed.

The same applies at the front door. A form that emails somebody is trivial; a form that creates a record, notifies the right person and appears in the pipeline is an integration, and integrations are where fixed-price estimates break.

Pay for a short investigation before the estimate. A few hours spent opening the systems converts the largest unknown into a number, and it is the cheapest insurance available on a fixed price.

The contractor question

One Person, An Agency, Or A Platform

Three shapes of supplier, and the right one depends on what happens after delivery.

A single developer

Cheapest and fastest, genuinely accountable, and exposed to one person’s availability. Reasonable for a small finishable project, provided you hold the repository and the accounts.

A small agency

More expensive, and continuity is the thing you are paying for. Worth it where the system matters enough that a two-week outage would hurt.

A configured platform

Not a developer at all. Frequently the correct answer, and the one a developer is least likely to propose unprompted.

Ask each supplier which of the three they would choose if it were their money. The answer is informative whether or not they are the one you hire.

One practical note on the first option, because it is the most common arrangement and the one most often mishandled. A single developer working on your repository, with your hosting and your accounts, is a low-risk arrangement; the same developer working on their own infrastructure and handing you a finished thing is a high-risk one, and the difference costs nothing to arrange at the start. Settle it in the first email rather than in the last.

Why We Reply, And To What

A short answer to the question this page opened with.

We build the capture-to-record path and the joins between tools a business already pays for, and we buy the advertising that fills them — over $70,000 a month in Ontario spend, run by the owner personally. That combination is the reason we reply to small, specific briefs rather than to open ones.

The brief a software developer ottawa will answer

A brief that gets a reply from any decent software developer ottawa names one process, says roughly what it costs in hours today, and asks for a price on the first phase only. Briefs that ask for a full system quote against a feature list get ignored by good suppliers and answered enthusiastically by the wrong ones.

What we decline: open-ended product development, bookkeeping, payroll, inventory systems, and replacing an industry package that already works. We are in Minesing rather than the city, and this work is done remotely.

Why do good developers not reply to our enquiry?

Usually because the brief makes the first hour expensive. In a market with 63,400+ tech workers and no shortage of demand, a vague enquiry gets declined quietly rather than evaluated. State what the problem costs you, the range you have in mind, who decides and by when — and the replies change.

Should we build or buy?

Buy, unless you can name something the tool genuinely cannot do that costs you real hours. Work out what the manual version costs first: hours a week times a wage is the budget, and without that number the project has no size and gets priced on impressions.

One developer or an agency?

A single developer is cheapest, fastest and exposed to one person’s availability, which is fine for a small finishable project provided you hold the repository and the accounts. An agency is continuity, and worth paying for where a two-week outage would actually hurt. A configured platform is often the right answer and the one least likely to be proposed.

How should a fixed price handle the unknowns?

By paying for a short investigation first. Integration work is only predictable once somebody has looked at what each system will actually allow, and a few hours there converts the largest unknown into a number. It is the cheapest insurance available on a fixed-price build.
