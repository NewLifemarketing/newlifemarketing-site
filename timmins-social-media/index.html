A finite audience

Timmins Social Media: An Audience You Can Actually Finish

In a city the budget never runs out of people. Here it does, and quickly.

Start from 41,145 people, narrow by whatever your offer actually requires, and the reachable audience for most businesses here is in the low tens of thousands at best. That is enough to run a campaign and nowhere near enough to keep one fresh.

The pattern is consistent and it surprises people every time: two or three weeks of good results, then cost per result climbing while nothing in the account has changed. Nothing is broken. The people inclined to act have acted.

And because the census recorded the population falling rather than rising, there is no stream of arrivals to refresh the pool. What is there is what there is.

So the shape that works is short bursts with a reason behind them — a season, an opening, an offer with a date — rather than a permanent drip, which in a finite audience is a slow way to wear out one piece of creative.

What runs out first

The Constraint Is Material, Not Money

In a market this size you run out of things to show before you run out of people to show them to.

Every supplier has the same stock library and the same templates. What almost none of their clients have is footage of the actual work: the job going in, the machine running, the finished room, the person who answers the phone.

That performs better and, more usefully here, it lasts longer. A library of real material is what lets a campaign keep running past week three without the audience learning to scroll past you.

One shooting day produces months of material and the marginal cost of each additional cut is small when it is planned before the shoot rather than after. In a town where the next shoot may be a year away, that planning is most of the value.

A posting retainer with no production budget attached is an arrangement for distributing material that does not exist.

A market with no replacements

Nobody New Is Arriving To See It

The census recorded the population falling, which removes the thing that normally refreshes an audience.

In a growing town an advertising audience renews itself: people move in, and a campaign that has been running for a year is still reaching somebody for the first time.

Here it is not. The same people see the same material, and there is no incoming stream to dilute the repetition. That is the mechanical reason a campaign here fades faster than the same campaign would in a larger market.

It also changes what retention is worth. A customer lost in a growing town is replaced by somebody who did not previously exist; a customer lost here has to be taken back from whoever has them.

So the arithmetic favours the unglamorous half: noticing when a regular goes quiet, asking for the second job, and making the first purchase easy to repeat. None of that is advertising and all of it outperforms advertising in a market the census says is shrinking.

Measurement

One Post Can Distort A Whole Month

In a town this size an unpaid post that travels reaches a meaningful share of the market.

That is a real possibility here rather than a theoretical one, and it happens often enough to break a monthly comparison. A quarter with one post that got screenshotted and passed around will look like a campaign working.

The only way to tell them apart is to ask. Every arriving customer, what brought them, written down — and in a town where a large share of business moves between people rather than through platforms, that note is the whole report.

Read it across a rolling quarter, because at these volumes a month contains too few enquiries to show anything and too many opinions to settle anything.

And keep it year on year. In a market that is not growing, the useful question is whether your share is, and no single quarter answers it.

What a quote should separate

Four Things Behind One Timmins Social Media Figure

A single number on an invoice usually contains four jobs with very different prices.

Working out what to say

Occasional thinking rather than monthly work. Billed every month, it is charging for a decision that was made once, and it is the quietest line in any retainer.

Making the material

Photographs and film of real work. A genuine cost, routinely assumed to be free, and the reason accounts run out of things to publish by week six.

Publishing and replying

Useful, cheap, and the most visible part of any report — which is why it ends up standing in for the whole arrangement.

Buying attention

Money reaching people who do not follow you. The only line that produces a customer who did not already know you, and the first one cut when a blended fee is negotiated.

Ask for those four as four numbers. A supplier who will not separate them has not decided which of them you are buying, and in a town this size the fourth is usually the smallest figure on the page.

Our part

Distribution, And The Footage To Feed It

A short offer, honestly bounded.

We run paid social against cost per enquiry and film the material it runs on. We manage over $70,000 a month in advertising spend for Ontario businesses and the owner runs the paid media personally.

What we would not take on in timmins social media

Timmins Social Media work that means writing in the owner’s voice is the part that cannot be outsourced honestly, and we would not take it. In a town where people know each other, a borrowed voice is detectable and it costs more than it buys.

We are based in Minesing, several hours south, so filming here involves travel, which we price as its own line. We do not sell daily posting retainers, community management or inbox monitoring either.

What should we spend?

Enough to reach a meaningful share of the town more than once, which is a calculable number rather than a guess because the audience is finite. Then stop. Past the point where everybody inclined to respond has, extra budget buys repetition and wears out the creative you have.

Why did it stop working after a few weeks?

Almost always because the audience has seen it. In a finite market the people inclined to respond do so quickly, and the remaining impressions reach people who have already decided not to. Nothing in the account changed, which is why it is confusing. New material and a rebuilt audience are the fix, planned in advance.

Should the account be the business or the owner?

In a town this size, usually the owner, or at least a voice that reads as a person. Word of mouth is the dominant channel here and an account that sounds like word of mouth does the same job. A polished corporate page is doing a different and weaker one, and it is also the version an agency can most easily sell you.

How often should we post?

As often as there is something worth showing, which here is less than a daily schedule implies. Two posts a week of real work beats five of stock graphics, because the second pattern teaches a small audience to scroll past you and there are no new people arriving to replace them.
