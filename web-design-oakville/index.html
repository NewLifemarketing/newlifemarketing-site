Questions

Web Design In Oakville: Common Questions

Why would we not just pick the cheapest quote?

Plenty of businesses should, and if your website is mainly confirming you exist then the cheapest competent option is the right purchase. The reason it is the wrong answer more often in Oakville is that the buyers here are generally not optimising for price, which the keyword data supports: the local cluster contains no cheap, affordable or budget at all. What they are optimising for is the project going right, which is a different thing to buy.

What should we put on the site that competitors do not have?

Four things, and none is a design decision. Who specifically will do the work. How the project actually runs week to week. What happens when something slips, which it will. And what is covered after launch, including the rate for future changes. Each is a paragraph, each costs nothing, and together they answer the only question a buyer who is not choosing on price is actually asking.

Should we publish prices?

A range is still useful and it is doing a different job here. In a price-led market it answers the main question; in Oakville it mostly filters out people who were never going to be your client and signals that you are not competing at the bottom. More valuable than the project price is the published rate for changes after launch, because that is the number a cautious buyer is actually worried about and almost nobody states it.

Does the design itself not matter in a market like this?

It matters as a floor rather than as a differentiator. An Oakville buyer expects a site to look considered and will notice immediately if it does not, so falling below the standard costs you. Exceeding it wins very little, because every serious competitor is already there. The money that would go into additional visual polish is better spent on the risk answers and on detailed proof, which almost nobody has.

How do we show we are reliable without testimonials?

Describe a real project in enough detail that somebody could judge it, including the part that went wrong and how it was handled. That is far more convincing to a cautious buyer than a row of five-star quotes, because it is specific and because it admits something. A supplier willing to describe a difficult week reads as experienced; one whose case studies all went perfectly reads as either lucky or incomplete.
