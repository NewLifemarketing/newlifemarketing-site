Partner Marketing Toronto

Recruitment Is The Easy Half

Almost every partner marketing Toronto programme we are shown has more signed partners than active ones, and the gap is never a recruitment problem. It is that nobody gave the partner something worth putting their own name on.

The small cluster

Partner Marketing Toronto: Thirty Searches, Three Different Meanings

The phrase covers three distinct arrangements, and confusing them is the first way a programme goes wrong.

Channel marketing agency toronto, partner marketing agency toronto, partner marketing toronto. Three phrases, thirty searches, and under them sit three different businesses: a reseller channel that sells on your behalf, a referral network that introduces, and a co-marketing arrangement where two companies share an audience.

They need different agreements, different material and different measurement. A programme designed for one and sold to the other produces partners who signed and never sold, which is the state most of these programmes are actually in.

The thinness of the cluster also tells you something useful: this is not bought by searching. Companies arrive at partner marketing Toronto programmes because somebody internally has decided the direct motion has a ceiling.

Why signed partners go quiet

A Partner Has Their Own Pipeline To Worry About

The mistake is treating a partner like a salesperson who happens to work elsewhere.

Your partner has their own customers, their own targets and a list of things to do that is already longer than the week. Your product is one of several they could mention, and mentioning it costs them attention they are short of. Nothing about signing an agreement changes that arithmetic.

So the question that decides whether a partner stays active is not whether they like the product. It is whether using your material makes their own week easier. Material that requires them to adapt it, rebrand it or explain it will not be used, however good it is.

The programmes that work give partners something that is immediately useful to the partner's own business: a piece of research they can send under their own name, a campaign ready to run with their logo on it, a landing page they do not have to build. The programmes that fail give partners a logo pack and a portal password.

What a partner marketing Toronto programme has to give before it asks

Something the partner would want even if they sold nothing of yours. That is the test, and it is strict on purpose.

What enablement actually is

Four Things Partners Will Actually Use

Each of these removes work from the partner rather than adding it.

A campaign that runs as-is

Copy, creative and a landing page, with space for their logo and nothing else required. If the partner has to write anything, most of them will not.

Something worth their own audience

Research, a benchmark, a tool. Partners forward material that makes them look informed and ignore material that makes them look like a reseller.

A page you host and they point at

Co-branded, live, and maintained by you. It removes the single most common blocker, which is that the partner has no capacity to build anything.

One named person who answers quickly

Partner programmes die in the gap between a partner question and an answer. Response time is an enablement feature, not an administrative detail.

Measuring it

What A Partner Marketing Toronto Report Should Count

The reported number in this category is almost always the flattering one.

Signed partners is a cumulative number that only goes up and tells you nothing about this quarter. Active partners — those who did something in the last ninety days — can fall, which is exactly why it is the more useful instrument.

Underneath it, two more: how many partners used the material at all, and how many sourced an opportunity. The first is the enablement score and the second is the commercial one, and they move in that order. A programme with rising usage and flat opportunities has a product-fit problem; a programme with flat usage has an enablement problem, and they call for opposite responses.

Report these three monthly and the programme becomes manageable. Report signed partners and it becomes a slide.

The last number worth tracking is time-to-first-deal for a new partner. If it is measured in quarters, the onboarding is the thing to fix, and it is usually cheaper to fix than recruitment is to repeat.

Onboarding

The First Ninety Days Decide A Partner

A partner who has not sold anything in their first quarter usually never will, and that is a design problem rather than a selection one.

The window is short because attention is. A newly signed partner has a brief period in which your product is novel and top of mind, and whatever happens in it sets the habit. If the first ninety days produce one introductory call and a portal login, the habit that forms is that your product is something they will get to.

What works in that window is embarrassingly concrete: one campaign the partner runs in week two, a named contact who answers within a day, and one deal worked jointly so that somebody on their side has actually seen the sale happen. The third item is the one that gets skipped and the one that matters most, because a partner cannot sell a motion they have never watched.

Measure time-to-first-deal from signature, per partner, and look at the distribution rather than the average. A programme where the fast partners are fast because they already knew the product has an onboarding problem hiding behind a decent mean.

Where a cohort of partners all stall at the same point, that point is the defect. It is almost always a piece of material that does not exist, or a question nobody answered quickly enough.

Our part

We Build The Material, Not The Channel

The useful split, said plainly.

What we do is the enablement half: the campaigns a partner can run unchanged, the research worth their audience, the co-branded pages you host, and the measurement that distinguishes active from signed. We also run the paid distribution where a partner has no media capability of their own, which is most of them.

What we do not do is recruit partners, negotiate commercial terms or manage the relationship. Those belong with somebody inside your business who can make commitments, and outsourcing them is how a channel ends up owned by nobody.

Where a company arrives with sixty signed partners and four active ones, the work is almost never more recruitment. It is finding out what the four do differently and building the programme around that.

How many partners should we have?

Fewer than most programmes sign. A small number of partners who actively sell is worth more than a long list of logos, because every signed partner costs attention to maintain whether or not they produce anything. If more than half your partners are inactive, recruiting more will make the ratio worse rather than the revenue better.

Should we pay partners for leads or for closed deals?

Paying on closed deals aligns the incentive and makes the partner wait, which small partners frequently cannot afford. Paying on qualified leads produces volume and arguments about quality. Many workable programmes do both — a small amount early to fund the partner's effort, the larger share on close — and define "qualified" in writing before anybody is paid.

Can this replace direct marketing?

No, and programmes that are expected to usually disappoint. A channel extends reach into audiences and geographies you cannot economically serve directly, and it does so slowly. It is an addition to a working direct motion, not a substitute for one that is not working — a partner will not sell something the vendor cannot sell.

What is the first thing to fix in an existing programme?

Find the partners who are active and ask them what they actually use and why. The answer is usually one or two specific assets, and it is usually not what the programme considers its flagship material. Rebuild the enablement around what the active partners already chose, then take that to the inactive ones.
