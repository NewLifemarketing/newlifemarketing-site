01

The problem

Running On Somebody Else's Software

Most businesses are running a specific operation on software designed for somebody else's.

The template site
A good-looking page that cannot do anything — no booking, no login, no connection to the systems the business actually uses.
The spreadsheet operation
The real business runs in five spreadsheets, three chat threads and one person's head. It works until it does not, and it cannot be handed to anybody.
The subscription pile
A different tool for every function, none of them talking to each other, each charging monthly for a fraction of what is needed.
Clients with nowhere to go
Customers, investors or partners emailing for documents and updates that should live somewhere they can log into.

This is a fit problem, not a technology problem

Off-the-shelf software is built for the average of everyone. A business with any real specificity eventually outgrows it — and the symptom is never “our software is wrong”. It is five subscriptions, a spreadsheet nobody else can read, and a person who cannot go on holiday.

Nothing on this page is about replacing tools that work. It is about the gap between what your business actually does and what you can buy off a shelf.

02

Definitions

Three Different Things

Three products that get called the same thing, and cost very different amounts.

Website
For the public — anyone. Gets found, explains the business, and converts a stranger into an enquiry.
Portal
For people who log in — clients, investors, partners, staff. Gives a specific audience secure access to what belongs to them, and only that.
Platform
For your team, internally. The system the business is actually operated from — people, tasks, pipeline, data, reporting.

Why this distinction comes first

Almost every conversation about “a new website” turns out to be about one of the other two. Getting the tier right at the start is most of the value, because the three are scoped, priced and built completely differently.

The rest of this page goes tier by tier, so you can read only the part you need.

Portals

Tier Two — What A Portal Actually Is

The tier where this page starts covering ground most agencies do not.

A portal is the part of your website that only exists once somebody signs in. The public site is the same for everybody. A portal is different for every person who logs into it, because what it shows is determined by who they are.

Businesses ask for one when

  • Clients keep emailing for documents, reports or updates
  • Different audiences need different things from the same company
  • Sensitive information cannot sit in email attachments
  • The business wants to look like a serious operation

What it replaces

  • A person manually sending files
  • Separate systems, or one inbox doing everything
  • Documents scattered across threads
  • A contact form and a promise to get back to you
08

Case study

One Login, Three Destinations

Three audiences, three completely different portals, one place to sign in.

For a licensed producer client we designed and built a single sign-in on the public website that serves three entirely separate audiences. One login form. The system identifies who the person is and routes them to the portal built for them — with no visibility of the other two.

Investor
Shareholders and financial stakeholders. Performance reporting, financial documents and updates — the material that used to be emailed individually.
Commercial
Retail and trade accounts. Ordering, account information, trade and sell-sheet material.
Licence & certification
Regulatory and compliance access. Licensing documentation and certification records, kept separate and controlled.

What makes portal work genuinely hard

Not the interfaces. It is the single entry point, the role check behind it, and the guarantee that three audiences sharing one front door never see each other's material.

Investors, trade accounts and a regulator-facing compliance library are about as far apart as three audiences get — which is why this is the example we use rather than a friendlier one.

09

Portals

The Part That Has To Be Right First Time

Not the interesting part, and by some distance the most important one.

Role-based access
What somebody can see is determined by who they are, enforced on the server rather than hidden in the interface.
Separation between audiences
Different user groups can share a login page without ever sharing data.
Account management
Invitations, approvals, password resets, and revoking access when somebody leaves.
Audit and visibility
Knowing who accessed what — which matters enormously in regulated industries.
Secure document handling
Files served to authorised users only, rather than sitting behind a link somebody could guess.

We design the permission model before building the interface. Retrofitting access control is how security problems get created, and it is the single most common reason a portal cannot be trusted in production.

Everything visible in a portal is a permission decision. Get it wrong and a commercial account sees investor reporting. This is the least glamorous part of portal work and the part that most determines whether it can be used at all.

Enrichment — What Makes It Actionable

The layer between a list of company names and a campaign you can actually run.

A company name on its own is not actionable. Enrichment runs alongside the sourcing, layering in the detail that turns a company record into something you can actually target: contacts and roles inside the organisation, firmographic detail, and the attributes that decide whether an account is worth pursuing.

The process

Scope To Launch

Five phases, and the first one is where most of the value is decided.

Scope
We map how the business actually works before deciding what to build. Most of the value is decided here — building the wrong thing well is the expensive failure.
Design
Interface and permission model designed together. For anything with logins, who sees what is decided before a screen is built.
Build
Developed in stages, with working software to look at early, rather than a long silence followed by a reveal.
Launch
Data migrated, users onboarded, and the team actually trained on it. A platform nobody was shown how to use does not get used.
Iterate
It changes as the business changes. Platforms are not delivered and abandoned — ours are maintained and extended.

Why scope is the phase that decides the outcome

Every failed custom build we have been asked to rescue failed in the first two weeks, not the last two. The software was fine. It was built for a process the business did not actually follow.

Straight answers

Websites, Portals & Platforms FAQ

What this costs to run, who owns it, how long it takes and what happens if we part ways — answered plainly.

What is the difference between a website, a portal and a platform?

A website is public and identical for everybody — it gets you found and turns a stranger into an enquiry. A portal is the part that only exists once somebody signs in, and it is different for every person who logs in because what it shows depends on who they are. A platform is internal: the system your team actually operates the business from. Most conversations that start with “we need a new website” turn out to be about one of the other two.

How long does a custom build take?

It depends entirely on the tier. A website is measured in weeks; a portal with real role-based access or an internal platform is measured in months, because the permission model and the data structure have to be designed before anything is built. What we will not do is quote a timeline that assumes scope we have not done yet — the scoping phase exists precisely so the estimate means something.

Who owns what you build — the code, the data, the accounts?

You do, all three. The platform, the database and the accounts stay with you. That is not the norm in this category, and it is worth asking every vendor you speak to, because the answer determines how much leverage they have over you later.

What does it cost to run once it is live?

Custom platforms carry running costs a template site does not: hosting, the database, and the automation running continuously in the background. We put those numbers in the proposal rather than on this page, because they depend entirely on what gets built — but they are stated up front rather than discovered on an invoice in month four.

Can you add a portal to the website I already have?

Usually yes, and it is a smaller step than most businesses expect once a site is already connected to real systems. What decides it is the existing build: whether it can carry authentication, and whether the data the portal needs to show already lives somewhere structured. We will tell you on the call rather than after a deposit.

Can different user types log in through the same place?

Yes — that is exactly what we built for a licensed producer client. One login form on the public site, then the system identifies who the person is and routes them to the portal built for them. Investors, trade accounts and compliance access all share one front door and never see each other's material.

Is our data secure, and who can see what?

Access is role-based and enforced on the server rather than hidden in the interface — which is the difference between a portal that is secure and one that merely looks it. Documents are served to authorised users rather than sitting behind a link somebody could guess, and access can be audited. We design the permission model before the interface, because retrofitting access control is how security problems get created.

What happens if we stop working with you?

You keep it. The code, the data and the accounts are yours, so the platform keeps running and another developer can pick it up. We would rather you stay because the work is good than because leaving is painful.

Can you connect it to the tools we already use?

Usually, and we would rather connect than replace. If an existing tool genuinely does its job well, the right build is the one that talks to it instead of duplicating it — the CRM layer in particular is normally a connection rather than a rebuild.

Do you build the lead sourcing, or is that a third-party tool?

We build it. The sourcing, the database behind it and the enrichment layer are all part of the platform rather than a subscription you rent — which is why the database is yours and keeps growing whether or not a campaign is running. We scope what can be collected and under what terms as part of the build.

How is an HQ different from a project management app?

A project management app asks your business to describe itself in somebody else's vocabulary. An HQ is built around how your operation actually works, with the modules you need and none of the ones you do not — people, tasks, leaderboard, live reporting, pipeline and briefings in one place rather than six subscriptions that do not talk to each other. The test is simple: does the team open it without being told to?
